# How Google Actually Makes Money

_Google is famous for free search, but its money comes from an auction that runs behind every query, tolls it pays to whoever owns the search box, and a cloud business that has suddenly become a second engine. Now it is spending more than its operations bring in to win the next default._

## h_open - The price of a default

In twenty twenty-two, Google paid Apple an estimated twenty billion dollars. Not for a factory. Not for a company. For a setting. The search box that opens when you tap Safari on an iPhone. That number comes from a federal judge's ruling.

> `apple` [source_located] Mehta opinion FOF 299: 'In 2022, Google's revenue share payment to Apple was an estimated $20 billion (worldwide queries).' FOF 298: Google pays Apple a percentage of its ad revenue on Safari and Chrome in return for default placement. FOF 299: the 2022 payment 'is more than all of its other revenue share payments combined'. FOF 289: 'In 2021, Google paid out a total of $26.3 billion in revenue share' (Apple, Mozilla, Android OEMs, carriers), 'listed in its financial statements as traffic acquisition costs'. Conclusion: 'Google is a monopolist, and it has acted as one to maintain its monopoly.' (sources: mehta)  

## h_twist - The price of a default

Why pay that much for a default? Because behind every search box runs an auction, and that auction is Google's money machine. Last year, Alphabet, Google's parent, took in four hundred and three billion dollars. By our calculation, about one point one billion dollars a day.

## m1_bids - The auction

Here is how it works. You type running shoes. Advertisers have already bid on those words, and Google runs an auction in a fraction of a second. But the highest bid does not automatically win.

> `auction` [source_located] Mehta opinion FOF 240-242: 'Google's text ads auction is a classic second-price auction, with modifications ... the winner, instead of paying the price of their highest bid, pays one cent above the first runner-up.' 'An auction winner is not determined solely based its bid ... LTV = bid x pCTR – β'; pCTR, the predicted click-through rate, 'is a score between 0 and 1'. FOF 243-252: 'pricing knobs' (squashing, format pricing, rGSP); 'Google strategically has used pricing knobs to raise text ads prices'; studies 'showed that Google could raise prices using squashing without losing advertisers.' (sources: mehta)  

## m1_quality - The auction

According to the court's findings, Google multiplies each bid by the predicted chance that you will click, a score between zero and one. So a cheaper ad that people want can beat an expensive ad nobody clicks.

## m2_remedy - The toll booths

Yet the payments did not stop. In the remedies decision, the court barred exclusive contracts and ordered Google to share some search data with rivals. But it did not ban paying partners for placement. Cutting the money off, the judge wrote, could cripple some of them. Both sides have appealed.

> `remedy` [source_located] Remedies opinion (Sept 2, 2025): 'Google will be barred from entering or maintaining any exclusive contract relating to the distribution of Google Search, Chrome, Google Assistant, and the Gemini app'; 'Google will not be barred from making payments ... to distribution partners'; 'Cutting off payments from Google almost certainly will impose substantial—in some cases, crippling—downstream harms to distribution partners'; no Chrome divestiture. 10-K: final judgment Dec 2025 also 'requires us to share certain search data with and offer syndication services to certain competitors'; Google appealed Jan 2026, DOJ and states Feb 2026. (sources: remedy, tenk)

## payoff - The lesson

So how does Google actually make money? Mostly by auctioning your searches, one click at a time, and paying tolls to whoever puts its search box in front of you. YouTube adds a second screen, subscriptions a steady drip, and Cloud has turned into a second engine. The lesson: the most valuable thing Google owns may not be a product. It is a default. And now Alphabet plans to spend more than twice last year's budget to make sure the next default, an AI answer, is its own.
