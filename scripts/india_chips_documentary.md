# India's Chip Plants Don't Make Chips. That's the Plan

_India now has chip plants in commercial production, and none of them makes a chip from scratch. They package and test chips made elsewhere. Why that is not a failure but a deliberate first step, and what India still has to build before it can make chips of its own._

## h_stakes - The plant that does not make chips

That is India in twenty twenty-six. The government says five chip units are in commercial production. But the first ones to open do not make chips from silicon. They take chips made somewhere else and finish them. And that, it turns out, may be exactly the right way to start.

## m1_front - Two halves of a chip

Making a chip happens in two halves. The first half is the front end, the fab. Here, light and chemicals build billions of tiny circuits onto a silicon wafer, layer after layer. It is the hardest part, and the most expensive. One industry study put the cost of a state-of-the-art fab at roughly five billion dollars to twenty billion dollars.

> `value` [source_located] BCG/SIA (Apr 2021, data for 2019): chip design = 50% of the industry's value added; wafer fabrication ≈ 25% of value added and ≈ 65% of industry capex; assembly, packaging and testing = 6% of value added and 13% of capex, 'relatively less capital-intensive and employs more labor than the front-end'; a state-of-the-art fab costs roughly $5 billion (advanced analog) to $20 billion (advanced logic and memory). (sources: bcg_sia)  

## m1_back - Two halves of a chip

The second half is the back end. The finished wafer is cut into individual chips. Each chip is sealed inside a protective package, wired to the outside world, and tested before it ships. In India, this is called assembly, testing, marking and packaging. It is skilled work, but it needs far less money and far fewer exotic machines than a fab.

> `value` [source_located] BCG/SIA (Apr 2021, data for 2019): chip design = 50% of the industry's value added; wafer fabrication ≈ 25% of value added and ≈ 65% of industry capex; assembly, packaging and testing = 6% of value added and 13% of capex, 'relatively less capital-intensive and employs more labor than the front-end'; a state-of-the-art fab costs roughly $5 billion (advanced analog) to $20 billion (advanced logic and memory). (sources: bcg_sia)  
> `units` [source_located] PIB (17 Sep 2026): twelve semiconductor manufacturing units approved under Semicon 1.0 with cumulative investment of over Rs 1.64 lakh crore: one silicon fab, one silicon carbide fab, one GaN micro-LED display fab and nine ATMP/OSAT units; five units have commenced commercial production; 'In 2021, the country had no commercial chip plant.' Converted at 95.32 rupees per dollar (5 May 2026, when the count reached 12): ≈ $17.2 billion. (sources: pib_sep26, pib_may26, fred_inr)  

## m1_draw - Two halves of a chip

Let me draw where the money is. Split the value of the chip industry into slices. Designing the chip is about half of the value. Making the wafer in a fab, about a quarter. Packaging and testing, only six percent. So the back end is the smallest slice. But it is also the one door a newcomer can open first.

## m2_sanand - Sanand

Sanand is an industrial town in the state of Gujarat. In February twenty twenty-six, the American memory maker Micron inaugurated a packaging and test plant there, a project of about two point seven five billion dollars. It now ships memory chips. At the end of March, a second plant, from the Indian company Kaynes, started commercial operation. In July, a third, from a joint venture of CG Power with the Japanese chipmaker Renesas, began production. All three in the same town.

> `sanand` [source_located] Tech Times (Aug 2026): Micron Technology's $2.75 billion assembly and test facility in Sanand, Gujarat, inaugurated on 28 February 2026, ships DRAM and NAND memory; Kaynes Semicon's Sanand plant reached commercial operation on 31 March 2026; CG Power's joint venture with Renesas and Stars Microelectronics began commercial production on 4 July 2026. PIB (Apr 2026): commercial production from two plants (Micron and Kaynes) had commenced. (sources: tt_sites, pib_apr26)  

## m2_inside - Sanand

Inside, the work goes like this. Finished wafers arrive from fabs abroad. Machines cut each wafer into individual dies. Each die is placed in a package, connected to the outside with tiny wires, sealed, and then tested. The machines and most of the materials still come from the United States, Japan, Taiwan and South Korea. But the factory, the jobs and the know-how are now in India.

> `osat_how` [source_located] Tech Times (Aug 2026): OSAT takes finished silicon wafers produced elsewhere, dices them into individual dies, places each die in a protective package, bonds the electrical connections, and tests the finished chips; it is the most accessible entry point because it does not require extreme precision equipment, exotic photochemistry or multi-step photolithography; OSAT uses wire bonders (mainly US and Japan) and packaging materials from Japan, Taiwan and South Korea; front-end chemistry needs contamination control in parts per trillion. (sources: tt_sites)  

## m2_count - Sanand

In total, India has approved twelve chip manufacturing units, with investments worth about seventeen billion dollars. Look at the mix. One silicon fab, two fabs for special materials and displays, and nine packaging and test plants. By September, the government said five of them were in commercial production. In twenty twenty-one, it says, the country had no commercial chip plant at all.

> `units` [source_located] PIB (17 Sep 2026): twelve semiconductor manufacturing units approved under Semicon 1.0 with cumulative investment of over Rs 1.64 lakh crore: one silicon fab, one silicon carbide fab, one GaN micro-LED display fab and nine ATMP/OSAT units; five units have commenced commercial production; 'In 2021, the country had no commercial chip plant.' Converted at 95.32 rupees per dollar (5 May 2026, when the count reached 12): ≈ $17.2 billion. (sources: pib_sep26, pib_may26, fred_inr)

## m3_bill - The import bill

Because it buys an enormous amount of chips. According to the government, India spent almost one hundred and fifty billion dollars on imported chips between twenty sixteen and twenty twenty-five. Imports grew by an average of almost a quarter a year. If that trend continued, the government says, India could be importing two hundred and forty billion dollars worth of chips a year by twenty thirty-five. That is a projection, not a fact. But it explains the hurry.

> `imports` [source_located] PIB (Sep 2026): India spent almost USD 150 billion on semiconductor product imports during FY17–FY25; imports grew at a CAGR of 23%; if the trend continues, annual imports could reach USD 240 billion by 2035; demand projected to reach USD 110 billion by FY2030; India employs nearly 20% of the global semiconductor chip design workforce. (sources: pib_sep26)  

## m3_demand - The import bill

And the need is growing. The government projects that India's own demand for chips will reach one hundred and ten billion dollars a year by twenty thirty, as phones, cars, data centers and factories all fill up with electronics. Again, a projection. And every chip India buys abroad is money that leaves the country.

> `imports` [source_located] PIB (Sep 2026): India spent almost USD 150 billion on semiconductor product imports during FY17–FY25; imports grew at a CAGR of 23%; if the trend continues, annual imports could reach USD 240 billion by 2035; demand projected to reach USD 110 billion by FY2030; India employs nearly 20% of the global semiconductor chip design workforce. (sources: pib_sep26)  

## m3_phones - The import bill

And India has done this before. A decade ago, three in every four mobile phones sold in India were imported. Then factories started assembling phones in India, starting with the final steps. By twenty twenty-five, the government says, almost every phone sold in India was made in India. Packaging chips is the same first step, applied to the chip itself.

> `phones` [source_located] PIB (Apr 2026): mobile phones imported (units) fell from 75% of total demand in 2014-15 to 0.02% in 2024-25; mobile phone production rose from ₹18 thousand crore to ₹5.45 lakh crore. (sources: pib_apr26)  

## m3_design - The import bill

India also starts with a hidden strength. The government says nearly one in five chip designers in the world works in India. Remember the slices. Design is the biggest one, and India already does a large part of it for companies around the world. Packaging, the smallest slice, has now arrived. The missing piece in the middle is the fab.

> `imports` [source_located] PIB (Sep 2026): India spent almost USD 150 billion on semiconductor product imports during FY17–FY25; imports grew at a CAGR of 23%; if the trend continues, annual imports could reach USD 240 billion by 2035; demand projected to reach USD 110 billion by FY2030; India employs nearly 20% of the global semiconductor chip design workforce. (sources: pib_sep26)

## m4_dholera - The missing middle

India's first real chip fab is being built by Tata Electronics in Dholera, also in Gujarat, with technology from the Taiwanese chipmaker PSMC. It is worth about about eleven billion dollars, and it is designed for fifty thousand wafers a month. When construction began in twenty twenty-four, India's IT minister said the first chip would arrive in December twenty twenty-six, at twenty-eight nanometers.

> `dholera` [source_located] PIB (Apr 2026): Tata Electronics is establishing a fab in Gujarat with an investment of Rs 91,526 crore, in technology partnership with PSMC (Taiwan), capacity around 50,000 wafer starts per month (≈ $11.0bn at 82.87 rupees per dollar on the 29 Feb 2024 approval). Tech Times (Jul 2026): the minister said in March 2024 the first chip would come in December 2026 at 28 nm; the fab will now start at 90 nm, with commercial production targeted for mid-2028; construction about 50% complete in April 2026. (sources: pib_apr26, tt_dholera, fred_inr)  

## m4_slip - The missing middle

That has changed. According to Tech Times, the fab will now start with older ninety nanometer chips, and commercial production is targeted for mid twenty twenty-eight. Tata and PSMC say starting with a mature process is normal, and in chipmaking it often is. A new fab has to learn to make good chips before it can make small ones.

> `dholera` [source_located] PIB (Apr 2026): Tata Electronics is establishing a fab in Gujarat with an investment of Rs 91,526 crore, in technology partnership with PSMC (Taiwan), capacity around 50,000 wafer starts per month (≈ $11.0bn at 82.87 rupees per dollar on the 29 Feb 2024 approval). Tech Times (Jul 2026): the minister said in March 2024 the first chip would come in December 2026 at 28 nm; the fab will now start at 90 nm, with commercial production targeted for mid-2028; construction about 50% complete in April 2026. (sources: pib_apr26, tt_dholera, fred_inr)  

## m4_loan - The missing middle

Even the financing shows how new this is. In February twenty twenty-six, the project borrowed seven hundred and thirty-five million dollars from a group of international banks. According to Tech Times, the land pledged as collateral was valued at zero, because nothing had been built on it yet. The banks were betting on the Tata name more than on the factory.

> `loan` [source_located] Tech Times (Jul 2026): a $735 million loan package from HSBC, MUFG, First Abu Dhabi Bank, DBS and ANZ in February 2026, with the project's 163.5-acre Dholera land pledged as collateral at a valuation of zero; lenders relied on Tata Group's reputation. (sources: tt_dholera)  

## m4_rivals - The missing middle

But the competition will not wait. Chinese foundries, backed by state subsidies, are expanding exactly this kind of mature chip production. One of them, Nexchip, is ramping up to one hundred thousand wafers a month. And India carries an old scar. In twenty oh five, a three billion dollar chip project called SemIndia stalled while its imported equipment sat stuck at Indian ports, waiting for customs clearance.

> `china_mature` [source_located] Tech Times (Jul 2026): Chinese foundries SMIC, Hua Hong and Nexchip are expanding mature-node capacity with state subsidies, targeting the 40-90 nm range Dholera will initially serve; Nexchip ramping to 100,000 wafers per month. (sources: tt_dholera)  
> `semindia` [source_located] Tech Times (Aug 2026): in 2005 semiconductor equipment imported for a $3 billion project by SemIndia became stuck at Indian ports awaiting customs clearances from multiple agencies. (sources: tt_sites)  

## m4_draw - The missing middle

Let me draw why a fab is so much harder than a packaging plant. A packaging plant needs machines that many companies sell, and materials with ordinary purity. A fab needs lithography machines from one or two suppliers, gases and chemicals so pure that contamination is counted in parts per trillion, and engineers who have done it before. Every one of those still comes from abroad.

> `semicon2` [source_located] PIB (Sep 2026): the Union Cabinet approved Semicon 2.0 on 15 July 2026 with an outlay of Rs 1,27,500 crore (≈ $13.2bn at 96.26 rupees per dollar), six pillars including machines and materials. Tech Times: a flat 30% incentive on project cost for makers of equipment, specialty chemicals, gases and materials; Kearney's PS Subramaniam: 'a lion share of critical inputs into the value chain will still be imported'. (sources: pib_sep26, tt_sites, fred_inr)

## m5_semicon2 - The second step

In July twenty twenty-six, the government approved a second chip program worth about thirteen billion dollars. For the first time, it pays companies that make the things behind the chips, the machines, the special chemicals and the gases, with an incentive of thirty percent of the project cost. Because, as one consultant at Kearney put it, the lion's share of critical inputs will still be imported, even with new fabs and packaging plants.

> `semicon2` [source_located] PIB (Sep 2026): the Union Cabinet approved Semicon 2.0 on 15 July 2026 with an outlay of Rs 1,27,500 crore (≈ $13.2bn at 96.26 rupees per dollar), six pillars including machines and materials. Tech Times: a flat 30% incentive on project cost for makers of equipment, specialty chemicals, gases and materials; Kearney's PS Subramaniam: 'a lion share of critical inputs into the value chain will still be imported'. (sources: pib_sep26, tt_sites, fred_inr)  

## m5_people - The second step

The new program also pays for more fabs, covering up to forty percent of the cost of a new silicon fab. And it pays for people. Chip design tools are now in more than three hundred Indian universities, and the equipment maker Lam Research plans to help train sixty thousand fab engineers over the next decade. Because a fab is not only machines. It is thousands of people who know how to run them.

> `pillars` [source_located] Tech Times (Aug 2026): Semicon 2.0 offers 40% capex support for CMOS silicon fabs and 35% for compound-semiconductor and display fabs; ₹1,000 crore for research centres in FY2026-27; more than 315 universities equipped with design tools and about 68,000 engineers trained by mid-2026; Lam Research's partnership targets 60,000 fab engineers over the next decade. (sources: tt_sites, pib_may26)  

## m5_trust - The second step

And India is not trying to win on price. Chinese mature chips are cheap and subsidized. According to Tech Times, India's real argument is trust. Companies in America, Europe and Japan want a second source of chips in a country they see as a reliable partner. That is a selling point no subsidy can buy.

> `geo` [source_located] Tech Times (Jul 2026): India's surviving competitive argument against subsidised Chinese mature-node capacity is not price but geopolitics, the security and resilience value of sourcing chips from a democratic, non-adversarial partner. (sources: tt_dholera)  

## m5_ladder - The second step

That is the logic of the whole plan. Start where the door is open, packaging and testing. Learn to run clean factories, train engineers, and attract suppliers. Then move up to a fab, first with older chips. And only then, maybe, the leading edge. Each step is smaller than the dream. But each one is real.

## payoff - The lesson

So are India's chip plants a failure because they do not make chips? No. They are the first rung of a ladder that every chip country has had to climb. The real test comes in twenty twenty-eight, when Dholera is meant to start selling chips of its own. Until then, India is learning the cheapest lesson in chipmaking first, how to finish what others started, before it learns how to start.
