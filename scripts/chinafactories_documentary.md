# Why the World Still Depends on Chinese Factories

_China makes about a quarter of the world's manufactured goods not because of one cheap wage but because of a neighborhood of suppliers, refiners, ports and practiced engineers. Tariffs since 2018 moved the last step of assembly toward Vietnam, Mexico and Taiwan; the deeper layers move far more slowly._

## a1_scale - The everything factory

Start with size. Manufacturing value added is what factories add on top of the parts and materials they buy. By World Bank data, China's factories added about four point eight trillion dollars in twenty twenty-five. That's about twenty-seven percent of the world total, by our calculation. And it sells that output everywhere. It sold so much more than it bought that its goods surplus reached one point one nine trillion dollars.

## a1_workers - The everything factory

## a1_wage - The everything factory

Are they still cheap? A production worker at a larger Chinese company earned roughly eleven thousand two hundred dollars in twenty twenty-five, at the Federal Reserve's average exchange rate. An American factory production worker earned about twenty-nine dollars an hour. Cheaper, clearly. But Chinese factory pay keeps rising, up about six percent last year at private firms. If wages were the whole story, factories would simply chase the lowest pay on the map. Many of them didn't.

At 7.1875 yuan/USD (Fed G.5A 2025): ≈ $11,200; ≈ $10,600; ≈ $15,800 (our conversion). (sources: nbs_wage, fed_g5a)

## a2_ev - Everything is down the street

The deepest clusters are in the newest industries. The International Energy Agency says China made about seventy percent of the world's electric cars in twenty twenty-five, and more than eighty percent of battery cells. For the chemical powders inside those cells, it's higher still. about eighty-five percent of the cathode powder. more than ninety percent of the anode powder.

> `ieaev` [source_located] IEA GEVO 2026: 'China remains the world's largest EV manufacturing hub, accounting for nearly 75% of electric cars produced in 2025'; Ch.7: 'In 2025, China accounted for 70% of electric car production and over 80% of battery cell production, as well as about 85% of global production of cathode active material (CAM) and more than 90% of production of anode active material (AAM)'; 'a tightly clustered supply chain, all the way from critical mineral refining and component production to battery and EV manufacturing'; 'In 2025, battery pack prices in China were 30% lower than in North America, and 35% lower than in Europe'. (sources: iea_gevo)

## a2_solar - Everything is down the street

Solar panels tell the same story. In a twenty twenty-two report, the IEA found that China's share of every manufacturing stage, from polysilicon to wafers, cells and modules, was above eighty percent. The ten biggest makers of the machines that make the panels were Chinese. And making panels there cost twenty percent less than in the United States.

> `ieapv` [source_located] IEA Special Report on Solar PV Global Supply Chains (July 2022): 'China's share in all the manufacturing stages of solar panels (such as polysilicon, ingots, wafers, cells and modules) exceeds 80%'; 'home to the world's 10 top suppliers of solar PV manufacturing equipment'; 'Costs in China are 10% lower than in India, 20% lower than in the United States, and 35% lower than in Europe.' (sources: iea_pv)  

## a2_rare - Everything is down the street

Then there's a step most people never see. Rare earth elements go into the magnets inside electric motors and wind turbines. Digging them up is only the start. They have to be separated and refined. For magnet rare earths, the IEA puts China's share of refining at eighty-five percent in twenty twenty-five, down from ninety percent a year earlier. And in April twenty twenty-five, China put export controls on seven of them.

> `ieare` [source_located] IEA GCMO 2026 (magnet rare earths): 'For refining, China's share dropped from 90% to 85% between 2024 and 2025, with the majority of the lost share being claimed by the United States and the rest by Malaysia'; China's share of mined supply of magnet rare earths around 60% in 2025; 'On 4 April 2025, the Chinese government introduced export controls on seven heavy rare earth elements.' (sources: iea_gcmo)  

## a2_port - Everything is down the street

When it's all built, it has to leave. In twenty twenty-five, the port of Shanghai handled fifty-five million containers, counted in twenty-foot units. The port of Los Angeles handled about ten million. That's more than five times as many through one Chinese port, by our calculation.

## a3_2018 - The tariff wall

Washington's answer was tariffs. In twenty eighteen, the U.S. Trade Representative put a twenty-five percent tariff on about thirty-four billion dollars of Chinese goods a year, then on sixteen billion dollars more. In September, a ten percent tariff hit about two hundred billion dollars, raised to twenty-five percent the next spring. Another round followed in twenty nineteen, and part of it was cut in half under a trade deal in early twenty twenty.

> `ustr18` [source_located] USTR Section 301: additional 25% duty on products of China with annual trade value of approximately $34 billion, effective July 6, 2018; 25% on approximately $16 billion, effective August 23, 2018; 10% on approximately $200 billion effective September 24, 2018, raised to 25% in May 2019; 10% on the '$300 billion action' (Aug 2019), raised to 15% (Aug 30, 2019 notice), reduced from 15% to 7.5% (85 FR 3741, Jan 22, 2020). (sources: fr_301_2018)  

## a3_2024 - The tariff wall

In twenty twenty-four, a review raised tariffs on strategic products. one hundred percent on Chinese electric cars. fifty percent on solar cells. And fifty percent on semiconductors from twenty twenty-five.

> `ustr24` [source_located] 89 FR 76581 (Sept 18, 2024), four-year review: electric vehicles increase rate to 100% in 2024; solar cells (whether or not assembled into modules) 50% in 2024; semiconductors 50% in 2025; lithium-ion EV batteries 25% in 2024; permanent magnets and natural graphite 25% in 2026. (sources: fr_301_2024)  

## a3_2025 - The tariff wall

Then, in twenty twenty-five, new tariffs under an emergency-powers law were stacked on top. In April, the so-called reciprocal tariff on Chinese goods reached one hundred and twenty-five percent, before it was cut back in May. In February twenty twenty-six, the Supreme Court ruled that the law does not authorize tariffs at all. Other laws took over. Since July twenty twenty-six, Chinese goods have faced a twelve and a half percent tariff under one of them.

## a3_who - The tariff wall

Remember who pays. A tariff is collected at the border from the American company that imports the goods. Some of it reaches shoppers. Federal Reserve economists estimated that at least thirty percent of the tariff cost on Chinese imports had shown up in retail prices by the end of twenty twenty-five. The rest was absorbed somewhere along the way.

> `fed_pass` [source_located] FEDS Notes (Mar 5, 2026): pass-through of tariffs to retail prices 'has been at least 30% for goods imported from China' (April-December 2025), a conservative estimate; tariffs are paid by the importer of record at the border. (sources: fed_feds)

## payoff - Why it's hard to leave

So why does the world still depend on Chinese factories? Not because of one cheap wage. Because of a neighborhood. Suppliers down the street, refiners for the hidden materials, ports that never sleep, and engineers who have done it before. Tariffs can move the last step. Rebuilding the whole neighborhood takes far longer. That's why, even after years of trade war, more than a quarter of the world's manufacturing still happens in one country. The next factory may well open in Vietnam, Mexico or India. Its parts list is the hard part to move.
