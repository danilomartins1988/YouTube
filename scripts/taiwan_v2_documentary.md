## m1_conflict - How Taiwan became essential

For a long time, the chip business had one rule. If you designed chips, you also built the factory that made them, called a fab. And fabs were brutally expensive. So only giants could play.

## m1_tsmc - How Taiwan became essential

In 1987, with backing from Taiwan's government and the Dutch company Philips, a new company called TSMC tried something different. It would design nothing. It would only manufacture chips, for anyone. It became the world's first dedicated chip foundry. That one idea rewired the industry. A small team with a great design no longer needed its own factory. Nvidia, founded in 1993, became one of those designers, and so did Apple and AMD. They focus on design and send their blueprints to Taiwan. The timeline turned into a network, and Taiwan sat at the center of it.

> `tsmc_1987` [source_located] TSMC was established in 1987 as the world's first dedicated semiconductor foundry, with backing from Taiwan's government and Philips. (sources: tsmc_about, tsmc_history)  
> `nvda_1993` [source_located] NVIDIA was incorporated in California in April 1993; it designs chips and relies on foundry partners such as TSMC to manufacture them (10-K). (sources: nvda_10k_fy2026)  
> `tsmc_customers` [source_located] TSMC's 2025 annual report shows its largest customer at 19% of revenue and the second at 17%; Nvidia's CEO has said Nvidia is the largest, and the second is widely reported to be Apple. Nvidia's 10-K lists TSMC as a wafer supplier. (sources: tsmc_customers_2025, nvda_10k_fy2026)  
> `apple_tsmc` [source_located] Apple-designed chips are manufactured by TSMC, including at its Arizona fab. (sources: apple_arizona)  
> Nvidia (10-K) and Apple name TSMC as a manufacturer; AMD is a fabless designer that uses TSMC.  

## m1_discovery - How Taiwan became essential

Today TSMC takes about 72 percent of all the revenue earned by the world's top ten contract chipmakers. In twenty twenty-five alone, it brought in 122 billion dollars. And at the end of that year, 77 percent of its wafer revenue came from its most advanced processes, seven nanometers and smaller. Taiwan didn't just join the industry. It took over the hardest part of it.

> `foundry_share` [source_located] TSMC held 72.5% of global foundry revenue among the top ten foundries in the second quarter of 2026 (TrendForce). (sources: trendforce_2q26)  
> `tsmc_revenue_2025` [source_located] TSMC's 2025 revenue was US$122.42 billion, up 35.9% in US dollar terms (4Q25 earnings call). (sources: tsmc_4q25_call)  
> `advanced_q4` [source_located] In Q4 2025, technologies of 7 nanometers and more advanced accounted for 77% of TSMC's wafer revenue (3nm 28%, 5nm 35%, 7nm 14%). (sources: tsmc_4q25)

## m2_process - Why chips are so hard to make

Let me draw what it takes. First, engineers design the chip, billions of tiny switches. Then a fab prints that design onto a silicon wafer, layer after layer, using light. Then the wafer is cut into chips, packaged with memory, and tested. Every step has to be close to perfect.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> General description of the semiconductor value chain (design, fabrication, assembly, packaging and test), as in SIA/BCG.  

## m2_euv - Why chips are so hard to make

Here is the first obstacle. To print the smallest features, a fab needs extreme ultraviolet lithography machines. Only one company in the world makes them, the Dutch firm A S M L. In twenty twenty-five, it shipped 48 of them.

> `asml_only` [source_located] ASML is the only company that makes EUV lithography systems, the machines used to print the smallest chip features; it shipped 48 EUV systems in 2025. (sources: asml_euv, asml_2025)  

## m2_yield - Why chips are so hard to make

And owning the machines is not enough. The real secret is yield, the share of chips on each wafer that actually work. Raising yield takes years of practice, thousands of engineers, and endless learning from mistakes. A brand-new factory, even with the very same machines, can take a long time to match the yields of an experienced one. That know-how is hard to buy, and even harder to copy.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Explanation of manufacturing yield; no figures.  

## m2_capex - Why chips are so hard to make

Then there is the money. TSMC planned to spend more than 50 billion dollars on factories and equipment in twenty twenty-six alone. Across the industry, chipmakers are expected to invest about 2.3 trillion dollars in new fabs between twenty twenty-four and twenty thirty-two.

> `capex_2026` [source_located] In January 2026 TSMC guided 2026 capital spending of US$52-56 billion (later reports say the range was raised). (sources: tsmc_4q25_call)  
> `fab_investment` [source_located] SIA/BCG project about $2.3 trillion of wafer-fab capital expenditure worldwide in 2024-2032. (sources: sia_bcg_2024)  

## m2_discovery - Why chips are so hard to make

Taiwan does not make most of the world's chips. In twenty twenty-two it had 18 percent of all chipmaking capacity, less than China. But for the most advanced logic chips, below ten nanometers, Taiwan had 69 percent. South Korea had 31 percent. The United States had none.

> `total_capacity` [source_located] SIA/BCG: in 2022 Taiwan held 18% of total global wafer-fabrication capacity across all chip types (China 24%, Korea 17%, Japan 17%, US 10%), and South Korea held 52% of DRAM memory capacity. (sources: sia_bcg_2024)  
> `lt10_capacity` [source_located] SIA/BCG: in 2022 Taiwan held 69% of global wafer-fab capacity for logic chips below 10 nm, South Korea 31% and the US 0%; in its 2032 forecast Taiwan has 47% and the US 28%. (sources: sia_bcg_2024)  

## m2_layers - Why chips are so hard to make

And chips are not one market. Memory is a different game, led by South Korea, which had about half of the world's DRAM capacity. Advanced packaging, the step that joins chips and memory, has become a bottleneck of its own. So Taiwan's edge is narrow, but it sits at the very top.

> `total_capacity` [source_located] SIA/BCG: in 2022 Taiwan held 18% of total global wafer-fabrication capacity across all chip types (China 24%, Korea 17%, Japan 17%, US 10%), and South Korea held 52% of DRAM memory capacity. (sources: sia_bcg_2024)  
> "About half" = 52% of DRAM capacity in 2022 (SIA/BCG). Packaging as a bottleneck is described in Episode 06's sources (Nvidia 10-K).

## m5_race - The race to diversify

Let's map the race. America's CHIPS Act offers about 53 billion dollars for semiconductors, 39 billion dollars of it for manufacturing. TSMC plans to invest 165 billion dollars in Arizona. In Japan, its Kumamoto venture costs more than 20 billion dollars.

> `chips_act_figs` [source_located] The 2022 US CHIPS and Science Act provides about $53 billion (CFR; $52.7 billion in other summaries) for semiconductors, including $39 billion in manufacturing incentives. (sources: chips_act)  
> `tsmc_us_165` [source_located] In March 2025 TSMC said it would expand its US investment to $165 billion, for six fabs, two advanced packaging plants and an R&D center in Arizona. (sources: tsmc_165bn)  
> `japan_jasm` [source_located] TSMC's Japanese venture JASM opened its first fab in Kumamoto in February 2024; total investment is to exceed $20 billion. (sources: jasm)  

## m5_europe - The race to diversify

In Germany, a TSMC joint venture is rising in Dresden, and Europe wants 20 percent of global chip production by twenty thirty. South Korea keeps pouring money into memory and its own foundries. And Taiwan keeps building its newest fabs at home.

> `germany_esmc` [source_located] TSMC, Bosch, Infineon and NXP are building ESMC in Dresden, Germany, with production targeted for the end of 2027. (sources: esmc)  
> `eu_target` [source_located] The European Chips Act aims to double the EU's share of global chip production to 20% by 2030. (sources: oecd_chips)  
> `capex_2026` [source_located] In January 2026 TSMC guided 2026 capital spending of US$52-56 billion (later reports say the range was raised). (sources: tsmc_4q25_call)

## m5_limits - The race to diversify

But here is the limit. Even with all this spending, the forecast from the industry's own trade group and Boston Consulting Group still has Taiwan holding 47 percent of advanced logic capacity in twenty thirty-two, with the United States at 28 percent. Diversification reduces the risk. It doesn't remove it.

> `lt10_capacity` [source_located] SIA/BCG: in 2022 Taiwan held 69% of global wafer-fab capacity for logic chips below 10 nm, South Korea 31% and the US 0%; in its 2032 forecast Taiwan has 47% and the US 28%. (sources: sia_bcg_2024)  

## m5_why - The race to diversify

Why so slow? Because the hard part isn't the building. It is the engineers, the suppliers, and the years of yield learning that come with them. Taiwan has spent decades on that. A new fab abroad can be built in a few years. Building the whole ecosystem around it takes much longer.

## payoff - The island

Taiwan matters not because it makes every chip, but because it makes the hardest ones, at a scale nobody else has matched. An island smaller than Switzerland ended up holding one of the most important keys to the global economy. Its strength is also the world's weak point: so much of the cutting edge in one place. And that is why the whole world is watching it.
