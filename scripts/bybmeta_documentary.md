## m3_proxy - What's priced in

So for a company in a building spree, we use a proxy. We take operating cash and subtract depreciation and amortization, about about twenty-five billion dollars a year, as a rough stand-in for the spending needed just to keep things running. That leaves about one hundred and eight billion dollars, a yield of about six and a half percent. It assumes capex eventually falls back toward that level.

> `proxy` [source_located] OUR proxy base for a capex boom (labelled proxy): operating cash flow $133.1bn minus depreciation and amortization ≈ $24.9bn TTM (SEC XBRL cash-flow line; Q3 2026 $7.15bn vs $4.96bn a year earlier) = ≈ $108.2bn (yield ≈ 6.6%). It assumes capex eventually falls back to roughly the depreciation level. (sources: tenq, px)  

## m3_gauge - What's priced in

Now let's run it backwards. What growth in that cash, for ten years, makes today's price work, before slowing to three percent? With an eight percent discount rate, about zero. With ten percent, about three and a half percent. But on today's actual free cash flow, the answer jumps to between about eighteen percent and about twenty-three percent a year.

> `rdcf` [source_located] OUR illustration (not a valuation): market cap $1,639.5bn = 10 years of cash growing at g plus a terminal value at 3%, discounted at r. On the PROXY base $108.2bn: r = 8% gives g ≈ −0.9% a year (about zero); r = 10% gives g ≈ 3.4%. On TTM FREE CASH FLOW $25.1bn: g ≈ 17.7% (8%) / ≈ 23.2% (10%); implied year-10 FCF ≈ $128bn / ≈ $202bn. (sources: tenq, px)  

## m3_mustearn - What's priced in

Less than Microsoft needs, by the same math, but Meta's spending is still climbing.
