# Could AI Make American Factories Cheaper Than China's?

_Mel, who could not buy a toaster that wasn't made in China, decides to build one in Ohio with robots. Her illustrative three-factory experiment shows automation can bring America within a few dollars of China, and which switches flip the winner; then the twist: China installs most of the world's robots. Verdict: AI changes the test, not the winner._

## co_hook - The factory that changes everything

Meet Mel. In our last episode, she tried to buy a toaster that wasn't made in China, and failed. So now she wants to build one herself, in Ohio. Picture her factory on a Sunday afternoon. Robot arms working. Cameras checking every part. Software deciding what to build next. And a price that matches China's. That factory doesn't exist yet. But almost every piece of it already does. So could AI make American factories cheaper than China's? And if it did, would America actually win?

## a3_bill - When labor matters less

And here's why that arm matters less than Mel hoped. In American factories, out of every dollar of sales, only about thirteen cents go to wages and salaries for everyone, from the line to the front office. Materials and parts take by far the biggest bite.

> `asm` [source_located] Census 2021 ASM: shipments $6.1T; cost of materials $3.34T (sum of subsectors) -> 55% (our calculation); annual payroll $785.2bn -> about 13% of shipments (our calculation; all employees, wages and salaries before benefits). (sources: census_asm21)  

## a3_pull - When labor matters less

Watch what happens when I pull labor out. Even if every paycheck vanished, the materials, the machines, the energy and the building would still be there. And the robots that replace those paychecks send a bill of their own. Automation shrinks a slice. It doesn't make the factory free.

## a5_card - China has robots too




## a5_reveal - China has robots too

It is. Last year, factories in China installed more industrial robots than the rest of the world combined. Compared with America, that's roughly nine robots for every American one. In a single year.

> `ifr_inst` [source_located] IFR World Robotics 2026: 603,307 industrial robots installed worldwide in 2025 (+11%, record); China 354,226 (+20%), 59% of the world; US 38,428 (+12%). China/US = 9.2 (our calculation). (sources: ifr_wr26, ifr_pr_global)

## a5_brands - China has robots too

Second surprise. More and more of those robots are made in China. A few years ago, foreign brands sold most of China's new robots. Now Chinese brands sell more than half.

> `ifr_dom` [source_located] IFR (Sep 24, 2026): Chinese domestic suppliers' installations +15% in 2025, 55% of China's installations; share around 30% in 2019-2021 (WR 2026 executive summary); more than half since 2024. (sources: ifr_pr_china, ifr_wr26)  

## a5_density - China has robots too

Now the twist inside the twist. Per factory worker, China isn't the most automated country. America has more robots per worker, and South Korea has several times more than both. China simply has so many factory workers that its robots are spread thin, for now. That's room to grow.

> `ifr_density` [source_located] IFR (Nov 12, 2026), robots per 10,000 manufacturing employees, 2025: Korea 1,066; Singapore 873; Germany 469; Japan 452; US 323 (8th); China 193 (19th; National Economic Census employment base; +147% 2020-2025); world 152. (sources: ifr_density)  

## a5_why - China has robots too

And it has reasons to grow. The federation points to a labor shortage from an aging population, and China's new five-year plan aims AI research at physical uses like robots. Robots don't cancel China's advantages. Plugged into its supplier network, they may multiply them.

> `ifr_cn_plan` [source_located] IFR China release (Sep 24, 2026): labor shortage due to demographic change makes robotic automation a preferred solution; China's 15th Five-Year Plan (2026-2030) robotics strategy emphasises self-reliance and 'aims to focus its AI research on physical applications'. (sources: ifr_pr_china)

## a7_card - The verdict




## a7_where - The verdict

So, could AI make American factories cheaper than China's? For some products, yes. The likeliest are heavy or bulky goods that are costly to ship, products built from many repeated precision steps, and things a country can't risk running out of, like chips, batteries and medical supplies. A toaster is light and cheap to ship, so Mel's choice is harder than she hoped.

## a7_jobs - The verdict

And if Mel does build in Ohio, her factory won't hire the crowds the old plants did. Economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo found that in American local job markets, the more robots arrived, the fewer people were employed and the lower wages went. The new jobs are different. Technicians, programmers, maintenance crews.

> `ar_jobs` [source_located] Acemoglu & Restrepo (JPE 2020): one more robot per thousand workers reduced the employment-to-population ratio by 0.2 percentage points and wages by 0.42% (US commuting zones, 1990-2007). (sources: ar_jpe)  

## a7_china - The verdict

Meanwhile, Wei's factory keeps its suppliers, its ports, its scale, and now the biggest fleet of new robots on Earth, most of them homemade. What could stop an American comeback? Robots sitting idle when demand is weak, missing parts suppliers, expensive money, and trade rules that keep changing.

## a7_verdict - The verdict

So here's our verdict. AI doesn't end China's manufacturing dominance. It changes the test. For decades, the question was who has the cheapest workers. Now it's who can combine intelligent machines, reliable energy, patient capital and a complete supply chain. America can win back competitiveness, product by product, without winning back the same jobs. And China can automate too. Mel's choice won't be decided by the cheapest labor. It'll be decided by the best system.
