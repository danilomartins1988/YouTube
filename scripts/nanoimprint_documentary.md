# The Stamp That Could Break ASML's Monopoly

_Only one company on Earth can build the machines that print the most advanced chips. Canon wants to do the same job with a stamp, China with a secret prototype, and two startups with new kinds of light. Why breaking ASML's monopoly is so hard, and what would have to happen for it to fall._

## h_stakes - The impossible machine

Every one of the most advanced chips in the world, the ones inside AI servers and the newest phones, is printed by machines from a single company, ASML. Its newest machine costs four hundred million dollars. Nobody else can build one. But a camera company, a secret lab and two startups are each trying to break that monopoly in a completely different way.

## m1_print - The machine nobody else can build

First, what the machine actually does. A chip starts as a disc of silicon called a wafer. To make it, you shine light through a pattern and print billions of tiny circuits onto the wafer, layer after layer, like a photograph. The smaller the light's wavelength, the smaller the circuits. And the smallest circuits need extreme ultraviolet light, or EUV. ASML makes that light by firing lasers at tiny drops of molten tin, tens of thousands of times a second.

> `euv_history` [source_located] MIT Technology Review: around 2001 ASML, then a smaller player, bet on EUV; Nikon and Canon were working on it as well but dropped out; it took 16 years and about $10 billion in research; first EUV machines went on the market in 2017 at well over $100 million each; EUV light is made by shooting lasers at molten tin drops tens of thousands of times a second; in 2025 ASML sold nearly 50 EUV machines and had nearly $40 billion in revenue. (sources: mit_asml)  

## m1_bet - The machine nobody else can build

Around twenty oh one, ASML, then a smaller player, bet on EUV. Two Japanese giants, Nikon and Canon, were working on it too. Both dropped out. ASML kept going. It took sixteen years and about ten billion dollars in research before the first machines went on sale in twenty seventeen, at well over one hundred million dollars each.

## m1_money - The machine nobody else can build

Then artificial intelligence arrived, and every AI chip needed EUV. In twenty twenty-five, ASML sold nearly fifty EUV machines and brought in nearly forty billion dollars in revenue. A standard EUV machine costs around two hundred and fifty million dollars. The new high-NA version, which prints even finer lines, costs four hundred million dollars. And because of export rules, no EUV machine has ever been sold to a customer in China.

## m2_how - The stamp

Canon says yes. Its idea is called nanoimprint lithography. Instead of projecting a pattern with light, Canon carves the circuit pattern into a mold made of quartz. Then it presses the mold onto the wafer, coated with a liquid resin, like a stamp. A flash of ultraviolet light from an ordinary mercury lamp, the kind chipmakers used in the nineteen seventies, hardens the resin. The mold lifts, and the pattern stays behind. Canon says it can print lines just fourteen nanometers wide, which it says matches the five nanometer process used for many of today's most advanced chips.

> `nil_how` [source_located] IEEE Spectrum (Jan 2025): NIL writes the pattern on a quartz master mask with an electron beam, makes replica masks, presses a replica onto a wafer coated with liquid resist like a stamp, and hardens the resist with UV light from a mercury lamp, the kind used in the 1970s; it patterns features as small as 14 nm. The Register: Canon says 14 nm line width is equivalent to the 5 nm process; it does not require a light source like EUV. Canon began NIL work in 2004, bought Molecular Imprints (Austin, Texas) in 2014; it took 20 years to reach the market. (sources: ieee_nil, register_nil)  

## m2_road - The stamp

It was not easy. Canon began working on it in twenty oh four, and in twenty fourteen it bought an American pioneer of the technology, Molecular Imprints, in Austin, Texas. It took twenty years to reach the market. In September twenty twenty-four, Canon shipped its first commercial machine to the Texas Institute for Electronics, a research consortium. And the memory maker Kioxia is testing it for prototype memory chips.

> `nil_how` [source_located] IEEE Spectrum (Jan 2025): NIL writes the pattern on a quartz master mask with an electron beam, makes replica masks, presses a replica onto a wafer coated with liquid resist like a stamp, and hardens the resist with UV light from a mercury lamp, the kind used in the 1970s; it patterns features as small as 14 nm. The Register: Canon says 14 nm line width is equivalent to the 5 nm process; it does not require a light source like EUV. Canon began NIL work in 2004, bought Molecular Imprints (Austin, Texas) in 2014; it took 20 years to reach the market. (sources: ieee_nil, register_nil)  
> `nil_ship` [source_located] IEEE Spectrum / The Register: in September 2024 Canon shipped its first commercial NIL system (FPA-1200NZ2C) to the Texas Institute for Electronics for R&D and prototypes; Kioxia has tested NIL for years and is evaluating it for prototype memory chips. (sources: ieee_nil, register_nil)  

## m2_pitch - The stamp

The pitch is all about cost. Canon's chief executive, Fujio Mitarai, said the price will have one digit less than ASML's EUV machines. In other words, roughly a tenth, although Canon has not announced a final price. With no giant light source, Canon estimates its machine uses about one tenth of the energy. And it is smaller. An EUV machine is about the size of a double-decker bus, about two hundred cubic meters. Four of Canon's machines fit in less than half that space.

> `nil_price` [source_located] Bloomberg via The Japan Times (6 Nov 2023): Canon CEO Fujio Mitarai: 'The price will have one digit less than ASML's EUVs'; a final pricing decision hasn't been made. Tom's Hardware: Mitarai: 'I do not expect nanoimprint technology to overtake EUVs, but I'm confident this will create new opportunities'. (sources: jt_mitarai, thw_nil_price, tf_nil)  
> `nil_energy` [source_located] IEEE Spectrum: Canon estimates NIL consumes one-tenth the energy of an EUV system with a 250-watt light source; EUV systems are as big as double-decker buses, about 200 cubic meters; a cluster of four NIL systems takes less than half that volume (plus a 50 m3 mask-replication tool). (sources: ieee_nil)

## m3_touch - The catch

The problem is the touch itself. Light never touches the wafer. A stamp does. So a single speck of dust caught between the mold and the wafer can ruin the chips underneath it. Canon had to invent ways to stop tiny air bubbles, to drop the resin precisely with inkjet printing, and to line up each new layer with the ones already printed, at a scale of a few nanometers. The analyst firm Gartner warned that there is a big gap between working in a lab and producing chips in high volume.

> `nil_problems` [source_located] Tom's Hardware: NIL tends to pick up defects because the mold touches the wafer; any particle can introduce flaws. IEEE Spectrum: Canon had to stop air bubbles and control overlay between layers. The Register: Gartner's Gaurav Gupta saw 'a big gap between research and development at leading-edge nodes versus high-volume execution'. (sources: thw_nil_price, ieee_nil, register_nil)  

## m3_draw - The catch

Let me draw the speed problem. An EUV machine prints about two hundred wafers an hour. Canon's comparison uses a machine printing eighty wafers an hour, with a target of one hundred. And every mold wears out. One copy of the mold prints one layer on about two thousand wafers, then it has to be replaced. For a giant factory making millions of chips, every slow step and every worn mold costs money.

> `nil_speed` [source_located] IEEE Spectrum: each replica mask can make up to 80 lots of 25 wafers, i.e. one circuit layer for 2,000 wafers; Canon's comparison uses an NIL system at 80 wafers per hour and targets 100 wph. MIT Technology Review: ASML's EUV machines crank out about 200 wafers an hour. (sources: ieee_nil, mit_asml)  
> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Comparison built from two sources (IEEE Spectrum, MIT Technology Review); not a like-for-like benchmark, said with 'about'.  

## m3_niche - The catch

Canon itself is careful. Mitarai said he does not expect nanoimprint to overtake EUV, only to create new opportunities. Its target is to sell ten to twenty machines a year within five years. That is a goal, not a sale. Compare that with the nearly fifty EUV machines ASML sold in twenty twenty-five alone. Canon is not trying to take the throne. It wants the chips that do not need it, like memory, where the same patterns repeat again and again.

## m4_lab - The secret lab

In December twenty twenty-five, Reuters reported that Chinese scientists had built a prototype EUV machine in a high-security laboratory in Shenzhen. According to two people with knowledge of the project, it was built by former ASML engineers who reverse-engineered ASML's machines, partly with parts from older machines bought on secondary markets. It fills nearly an entire factory floor. And it already makes EUV light. But it has not made a single chip.

> `cn_euv` [source_located] Reuters (17 Dec 2025): a prototype EUV machine in a high-security Shenzhen lab, completed in early 2025 and under testing, fills nearly an entire factory floor, built by former ASML engineers who reverse-engineered ASML's machines (two people with knowledge of the project); parts from older ASML machines came from secondary markets; goal of working chips by 2028. Engadget/Reuters: it generates EUV light but is not yet making chips; experts see 2030 as more likely. ASML told Reuters its first EUV prototype was built in 2001 and it took nearly two decades before chips in 2019. (sources: reuters_cn_euv, engadget_cn_euv)  

## m4_gap - The secret lab

The distance is still huge. According to Tom's Hardware, one Chinese team's light source reaches around one hundred watts of EUV power. ASML's production machines deliver roughly six hundred. The government's goal is working chips by twenty twenty-eight. People close to the project told Reuters that twenty thirty is more realistic. Remember, ASML built its first EUV prototype in twenty oh one, and it took nearly two decades to make chips with it.

## m4_draw - The secret lab

Let me draw why China might not care about speed. A Western chipmaker needs a machine that earns money, hundreds of wafers an hour. A country cut off from the machine only needs one that works. As one former ASML employee, now an analyst, put it, China would be very happy with a tool that makes one wafer per hour, even if it costs a fortune, and would build a factory with a thousand of them.

> `china_wafer` [source_located] MIT Technology Review: Jeff Koch, a former ASML employee and SemiAnalysis analyst: China 'would be very happy to have a tool that does one wafer per hour and it costs them a fortune to run'; they would build a fab with a thousand of those. (sources: mit_asml)

## m4_duv - The secret lab

Even the older kind of machine is a struggle. China's own makers of deep ultraviolet machines, the generation before EUV, are expected to deliver about five machines this year, according to Tom's Hardware. ASML deploys about one hundred and thirty of its comparable machines in a typical year. Many key parts for the Chinese machines still come from Japan. So for now, China squeezes everything it can out of the older machines it already owns, printing the same layer several times to fake a finer line.

> `cn_light` [source_located] Tom's Hardware (Aug 2026): one Chinese light-source team (Harbin Institute of Technology) has reached around 100 W of EUV output against the roughly 600 W that ASML's production sources deliver; the prototype generates EUV photons but has not yet exposed a wafer. MIT Technology Review: China will lean hard on DUV with multi-patterning. (sources: thw_cn_tools, mit_asml)

## m5_substrate - New kinds of light

In San Francisco, a startup called Substrate wants to print chips with X-rays, made by a compact particle accelerator. It does not plan to sell machines at all. It wants to build its own factory and produce chips at scale by twenty thirty. That is its goal. It even predicts it can make a finished wafer for ten thousand dollars, about a tenth of where it says the industry is heading. But it has shared few details. One author who has written a book on ASML called the plan probably unachievable.

> `substrate` [source_located] MIT Technology Review: Substrate, a San Francisco startup founded four years ago, works on lithography with x-ray light from a compact particle accelerator; goal: chips at scale by 2030 in its own fab; it predicts finished wafers at $10,000, a tenth of where its CEO says the industry is heading (toward $100,000). Marc Hijink thinks mastering both a new lithography and fab techniques is probably 'unachievable and impossible'. (sources: mit_asml)  

## m5_lace - New kinds of light

In Norway, a startup called Lace Lithography skips light completely. It fires a beam of helium atoms through the pattern. It says the atoms offer a precision of a tenth of a nanometer, and it aims to have machines ready to sell by twenty twenty-nine or twenty thirty. Again, a goal. Meanwhile, ASML is not standing still. Its high-NA machines are already shipping to factories.

> `lace` [source_located] MIT Technology Review: Lace Lithography (Norway) uses a beam of helium atoms instead of light, with a precision of 0.1 nm against EUV's 13.5 nm wavelength; it aims to have machines ready to sell by 2029 or 2030. (sources: mit_asml)  
> `high_na` [source_located] MIT Technology Review (Jun 2026): ASML's new high-NA EUV machines (8 nm resolution) are shipping to fabs at $400 million each. (sources: mit_asml)  

## payoff - The lesson

So could a stamp break ASML's monopoly? Not soon. Canon's machine works, but for now it is a tool for the chips that do not need the very best. China's prototype makes light, not chips. The startups have goals, not factories. And that is the real lesson. ASML's monopoly was never just a machine. It was a bet held for sixteen years, while everyone else gave up. Breaking it will take the same thing. Not a cheaper trick, but years of patience nobody else has yet shown.
