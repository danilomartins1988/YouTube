## m1_total - The bill

Start with the whole bill. In twenty twenty-four, the United States spent five point three trillion dollars on health care. That is fifteen thousand four hundred and seventy-four dollars for every man, woman and child, and eighteen percent of everything the American economy produced. And it grew more than seven percent in a single year.

> `nhe` [source_located] CMS: 'NHE grew 7.2% to $5.3 trillion in 2024, or $15,474 per person, and accounted for 18.0% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP).' 'Hospital expenditures grew 8.9% to $1,634.7 billion in 2024'; 'Prescription drug spending increased 7.9% to $467.0 billion in 2024'. (sources: cms)  

## m1_parts - The bill

The biggest piece is hospitals, at about one point six trillion dollars a year. Prescription drugs at the pharmacy add another four hundred and sixty-seven billion dollars. Doctors, nursing homes, insurance and everything else make up the rest. Hospital spending alone grew almost nine percent in a single year, and drug spending almost eight.

On the same basis, health care took seventeen point two percent of the American economy, against eleven point two percent on average elsewhere.

## m2_usage - Less care, higher prices

Americans actually use less health care than people in many rich countries. They have about half as many in-person doctor consultations per person. In other rich countries, hospitals discharge about fifty-seven percent more patients per person. And an average hospital stay lasts six and a half days in the US, against almost ten days elsewhere.

> `usage` [source_located] Peterson-KFF / KFF: 'higher prices – as opposed to higher utilization – explain the United States' high health spending'; 'Compared to its peers, the U.S. has about half as many in-person doctor consultations per person'; 'In 2021, comparable countries averaged 57% more discharges per capita than the U.S.'; 'In 2022, peer nations had an average inpatient hospital stay of 9.7 days … compared to 6.5 days in the U.S.' (sources: pkff_prices)  

## m2_finding - Less care, higher prices

Researchers have looked at this for years, and the conclusion keeps coming back the same. In the words of the Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker, it is higher prices, as opposed to higher use, that explain the high spending.

> `usage` [source_located] Peterson-KFF / KFF: 'higher prices – as opposed to higher utilization – explain the United States' high health spending'; 'Compared to its peers, the U.S. has about half as many in-person doctor consultations per person'; 'In 2021, comparable countries averaged 57% more discharges per capita than the U.S.'; 'In 2022, peer nations had an average inpatient hospital stay of 9.7 days … compared to 6.5 days in the U.S.' (sources: pkff_prices)  

## m2_example - Less care, higher prices

Take a heart bypass operation. Even in Medicare, the US government's own program, the average cost was forty-four thousand one hundred and forty-nine dollars. In public insurance schemes in comparable countries, it was about twenty-four thousand eight hundred and forty-seven dollars. Almost twice as much, for the same operation, even when the government is the one paying. For a procedure to open a blocked artery, the Medicare cost was almost three times the peer average.

> `prices` [source_located] Peterson-KFF / KFF: 'the average cost per inpatient CABG in the U.S. traditional Medicare program was $44,149' compared with '$24,847 among public insurance schemes in peer nations'; for angioplasty 'the average cost within the U.S. traditional Medicare program was 2.9 times the average cost among peer nations'; 'The cost per MRI among U.S. private health plans was more than three times the U.S. Medicare cost'; 'Medicare-negotiated prices are 2.8 times the average of drug prices in the 11 comparable countries.' (sources: pkff_prices)

## m3_two - Price one: the hospital

An American hospital does not have one price. It has many. For older Americans on Medicare, the government sets what it will pay. For everyone with private insurance, the price is negotiated between the hospital and each insurer. And those prices are much higher.

> `hospitals` [source_located] RAND Round 5.1: 'In 2022, across all hospital inpatient and outpatient services ... employers and private insurers paid, on average, 254 percent of what Medicare would have paid for the same services at the same facilities.' Outpatient facility services averaged 279 percent. (sources: rand_hosp)

## m3_rand - Price one: the hospital

The RAND Corporation studied what employers and private insurers actually paid hospitals. In twenty twenty-two, they paid on average two hundred and fifty-four percent of what Medicare would have paid, for the same services, at the same hospitals. For outpatient services, it was two hundred and seventy-nine percent. In other words, the same hospital, for the same care, charged private plans about two and a half times what it accepted from Medicare.

> `hospitals` [source_located] RAND Round 5.1: 'In 2022, across all hospital inpatient and outpatient services ... employers and private insurers paid, on average, 254 percent of what Medicare would have paid for the same services at the same facilities.' ## m4_brand - Price two: the pharmacy

RAND also compared drug prices in America with thirty-three other rich countries. Across all drugs, US prices were two hundred and seventy-eight percent of prices elsewhere. For brand-name drugs, the ones still protected by patents, they were four hundred and twenty-two percent. More than four times as much.

> `drugs` [source_located] RAND (2022 data): 'In 2022, U.S. prices across all drugs were 278 percent of prices in the 33 OECD comparison countries.' 'U.S. prices for brand-name originator drugs were 422 percent of prices in comparison countries.' 'U.S. prices were 67 percent of prices of non-U.S. countries for unbranded generics.' After rebates, brand-name drugs were 308 percent. (sources: rand_drugs)  

## m4_generic - Price two: the pharmacy

But here is the twist. For cheap generic drugs, the copies made after patents expire, American prices were lower, about sixty-seven percent of prices abroad. The problem is not every pill. It is the newest, patented ones.

## m5_card - Price three: the paperwork




## m5_admin - Price three: the paperwork

Someone has to run all those insurance plans, process every claim, and argue over every bill. A major study in the Journal of the American Medical Association found that administration took about eight percent of US health spending, against one to three percent in other rich countries. That was for twenty sixteen. More recent figures put the cost of running insurance and public programs at about seven percent of national health spending.

> `admin` [source_located] JAMA (2018, 2016 data): administrative costs 'accounted for 8% in the US vs a range of 1% to 3% in the other countries.' Peterson-KFF (Jan 2026): the net cost of health insurance and program administration 'represented 7.0% of total national health expenditures' in 2024. (sources: jama, pkff_time)  

## m5_premium - Price three: the paperwork

All three prices end up in the same place. The insurance premium. In twenty twenty-six, a family plan through an employer cost on average twenty-eight thousand three hundred and three dollars a year, up five percent in a single year. Workers themselves paid about seven thousand four hundred and seventy-one dollars of that, nine percent more than the year before. The employer paid the rest, as part of the cost of employing each worker.

## m5_hard - Price three: the paperwork

Why are these prices so hard to bring down? Because every one of them is somebody's income. A hospital's budget, a drug maker's profit, an insurer's revenue. Lowering the price means someone gets paid less, and each of them has every reason to fight it.

## payoff - The lesson

So why do Americans pay so much for health care? Not because they go to the doctor more. They go less. They pay more for each visit, each scan, each pill, and each claim. Health care in America is not expensive because of how much care people get. It is expensive because of what each piece of care costs.
