# The Tiny Island Getting Rich From AI

_A tiny Caribbean island was handed two letters by chance in the nineteen nineties. Today those letters, .ai, bring in almost half of its government's money. How it works, how big it got, what it pays for, and whether it can last._

## h_question - The island

What if a tiny island got rich from artificial intelligence, without building a single AI? No factories, no data centers, no tech giants. Just two letters.

## h_stakes - The island

It's happening. Anguilla, a Caribbean island of about 16,000 people, now earns 80 million dollars in just seven months of twenty twenty-six from two letters: dot A I. That is 47.5 percent of all the money its government collects. More than all the taxes paid by the island's businesses.

> `island` [source_located] AP (Oct 2024): Anguilla has a population of about 16,000 and covers just 35 square miles (91 square kilometers). (sources: ap_2024)  
> `rev_2026` [source_located] The Gleaner (Oct 2026): .ai fees brought in EC$217 million (US$80 million) in the first seven months of 2026, 47.5 per cent of all government revenue to date, more than the EC$203 million in tax revenue from the territory's entire productive sector, according to Premier Cora Richardson Hodge. (sources: gleaner_2026, eccb_peg)

## m1_what - The lucky letters

First, what is a domain name? Every website lives at a number, a bit like a phone number, that computers use to find it. A domain name is the easy name that points to that number. And the last part, after the final dot, is called the ending.

## m1_codes - The lucky letters

Let me draw it. Every country on the internet gets its own two-letter ending. Brazil is dot B R. Japan is dot J P. The letters come from each country's official code, not from what they might mean one day. And Anguilla's code is A I.

## m1_assigned - The lucky letters

In 1995, Anguilla was assigned dot A I. Back then, artificial intelligence was a niche idea in labs, and the letters were worth almost nothing. Its official manager today is the Government of Anguilla. Nobody on the island could have guessed what those two letters would one day be worth.

> `assigned` [source_located] Anguilla was assigned the .ai domain in 1995 (Premier Webster via AP; Afnic: available since 1995); IANA lists the Government of Anguilla as its manager. (sources: ap_2024, afnic_2025, iana_ai)  

## m1_cate - The lucky letters

The early years were improvised. A software developer called Vince Cate, who moved to the island in nineteen ninety-four, says he was asked by one of the internet's founders whether he wanted to run dot A I. For a while the domain was even handed to a company in Taiwan that later disappeared, and it had to be recovered. For years, Cate ran the sales for the government. Later, he made the government itself the official contact for the domain.

> `cate` [source_located] IEEE Spectrum (Jan 2024): software developer Vince Cate, who came to Anguilla in 1994, ran .ai sales for the government and later changed the IANA administrative contact to the government; he recounts that Jon Postel asked him whether he wanted to run .ai, that it was later handed to a company in Taiwan which disappeared, and that the domain was recovered. (sources: ieee_2024)  
> Cate's own account in IEEE Spectrum; attributed to him in the narration ("says").

## m2_draw - The money machine

Let me draw the money machine. A start-up anywhere in the world wants a name that ends in dot A I. It buys it from a website that sells domain names. That seller passes the order to the registry, the system that keeps the master list of every dot A I name. And the registry pays the Government of Anguilla.

> `manager` [source_located] AP (Oct 2024): Anguilla signed a deal with U.S.-based Identity Digital (which also manages Australia's .au) to manage .ai registrations, which the government said would mean more revenue and better resilience and security. (sources: ap_2024, iana_ai)  
> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> General description of the domain registration chain; the registry operator is from AP.  

## m2_price - The money machine

The price is simple. A new name costs about 140 dollars for two years. Then you pay again to keep it, every time it comes up for renewal. And the best names are auctioned. In twenty twenty-three, litigate dot A I went for 13,000 dollars. And when a name expires and nobody renews it, it can be sold again, and the island is paid once more.

> `price` [source_located] IMF (May 2024): the pricing model is US$140 for a two-year registration; litigate.ai was auctioned for US$13,000 in 2023. AP: the fee of $140 for two years won't change under the new manager. (sources: imf_2024, ap_2024)  

## m2_renew - The money machine

Here is the clever part. A domain name is never really bought. It is rented. Stop paying, and you lose it. So every name sold today comes back as money two years later, and two years after that. For Anguilla, the AI boom became a subscription.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Interpretation of the renewal model described by the IMF and AP; no figures.  

## m2_why - The money machine

Why pay? Because for an AI company, a name that ends in dot A I tells the world what it does in two letters. It works like a brand. And with so many new AI start-ups, everyone wants one.

In twenty twenty-four, as the rush grew, Anguilla signed a deal with an American company, Identity Digital, to run the registry. The government said it would bring more money, and make the system safer. The same company also runs Australia's own internet ending, dot A U.

## m3_regs - The boom

In twenty twenty-two, there were 144,000 registered dot A I names. A year later, 354,000. By the start of twenty twenty-five, about 600,000. And in January twenty twenty-six, the government announced that dot A I had passed one million names.

> `regs` [source_located] IMF: registrations rose from 144,000 in 2022 to 354,000 in 2023. Domain Name Wire: the official count was 598,007 on 2 January 2025, and the Government of Anguilla announced in January 2026 that .ai passed one million domain names. (sources: imf_2024, dnw_2026)  

## m3_cate - The boom

Vince Cate watched it happen from his office. At the start of twenty twenty-four he said sales had gone up almost four times, to about 3 million dollars a month. That year, the island's premier said the money could even double. By twenty twenty-five, it had nearly tripled.

> `cate_sales` [source_located] IEEE Spectrum (Jan 2024), Vince Cate: 'It's about US $3 million per month' and 'our sales went up by almost a factor of four'. (sources: ieee_2024)  
> `rev_2023` [source_located] IMF: in 2023 .ai registrations generated EC$87 million (approximately US$32 million), just over 20 percent of the government's total revenue; before the surge it hovered around 5 percent. (sources: imf_2024, eccb_peg)  
> `rev_2025` [source_located] Anguilla Focus (Mar 2026): Anguilla earned EC$230 million (US$85.3m) from .ai registrations in 2025, far exceeding the government's projection (EC$132 million in the draft estimates). (sources: focus_2025, eccb_peg)  

## m3_revenue - The boom

The money followed. Before the boom, the domain brought in around 5 percent of the government's revenue. In twenty twenty-three, it was about 32 million dollars, just over 20 percent of everything the government collected. In twenty twenty-five, about 85 million dollars, far more than the government itself had planned.

> `rev_2023` [source_located] IMF: in 2023 .ai registrations generated EC$87 million (approximately US$32 million), just over 20 percent of the government's total revenue; before the surge it hovered around 5 percent. (sources: imf_2024, eccb_peg)  
> `rev_2025` [source_located] Anguilla Focus (Mar 2026): Anguilla earned EC$230 million (US$85.3m) from .ai registrations in 2025, far exceeding the government's projection (EC$132 million in the draft estimates). (sources: focus_2025, eccb_peg)

## m4_uses - What the money buys

The government says dot A I money has helped pay for the airport, new roads, tax relief for locals, and more health services for the young and the elderly, including free medical care for senior citizens. ## m4_budget - What the money buys

## m4_tourism - What the money buys

For an island like this, that changes everything. Tourism makes up 37 percent of the economy, and it has been hit hard, by a powerful hurricane and then by the pandemic. The domain gives the government a second engine, one that no storm can blow away.

## m5_quote - Can it last

In twenty twenty-four, the island's premier said: "You can't predict how long this is going to last." And, "we can't rely on it solely." At the time, the government expected the domain to settle at around 15 percent of its revenue. Instead, it shot far higher.

> `risk_quote` [source_located] AP (Oct 2024): Premier Ellis Webster said 'You can't predict how long this is going to last' and 'we can't rely on it solely'. IMF: the government expected .ai revenue to stabilize at around 15 percent of revenue. (sources: ap_2024, imf_2024)  

## m5_renewals - Can it last

The real test is renewals. Many names were bought in the rush. If AI start-ups close, or the hype fades, owners stop renewing, and the money shrinks two years later. The boom could turn into a slow leak.

## payoff - The lucky island

So, Anguilla didn't invent artificial intelligence. It didn't build a single data center. It simply owned the right two letters at the right moment in history. And every time someone, somewhere, names a new AI company, a little piece of that idea travels across the ocean, to a small island in the Caribbean. The lesson is not only about luck. It is about what you do when luck arrives: spend it, or turn it into something that lasts. From an improvised registry run by one software developer to more than a million names, it is one of the strangest windfalls in the world economy.
