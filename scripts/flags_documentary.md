# Why Almost Half the World's Ships Fly Three Small Flags

_Greek, Chinese and Japanese owners control the biggest fleets, yet almost half of the world's shipping capacity flies the flags of Liberia, Panama and the Marshall Islands. How it started, why owners do it, what the small countries earn, and who pays when things go wrong._

What if most of the giant ships that carry your phone, your clothes and your coffee were registered in countries they almost never visit? And what if that choice of flag decided the rules on board, the taxes the owner pays, and who protects the crew?

## h_stakes - The flags

They are. According to the United Nations' trade agency, just three flags, Liberia, Panama and the Marshall Islands, carry 45 percent of the world's shipping capacity. Yet the biggest ship owners are Greek, Chinese and Japanese.

> `top3` [source_located] UNCTAD (RMT 2025): as of 1 January 2025 the top three flag States, Liberia, Panama and the Marshall Islands, together accounted for 45.1 per cent of world ship carrying capacity; Liberia alone 17.4 per cent. (sources: unctad_2025)

## m1_flag - Whose ship is it

Every ship must be registered in one country, its flag state. That flag decides which country's laws apply on board: safety rules, labor rules and taxes. It works like the ship's passport. In theory, the flag state is also the one that must inspect the ship and make sure those rules are followed, wherever it sails.

## m1_evergiven - Whose ship is it

Remember the giant ship that got stuck in the Suez Canal in twenty twenty-one, the Ever Given? It was owned by a Japanese company, operated by a Taiwanese shipping line, flagged in Panama, and stuck in Egypt. One ship, four countries. As the Associated Press put it at the time, matters quickly became an international morass.

> `evergiven` [source_located] AP (March 2021): the Ever Given that blocked the Suez Canal was owned by a Japanese firm, operated by a Taiwanese shipper, flagged in Panama and stuck in Egypt, so 'matters quickly become an international morass'. (sources: ap_evergiven)  

## m1_owners - Whose ship is it

And it is not an exception. Greek owners control the biggest share of the world's fleet, about 16 percent of all carrying capacity. Asian companies own more than half of it, European companies about a third. But look at the flags, and the top three are small countries most people could not find on a map. That is true across a world fleet of about 116,000 vessels. Greece, China and Japan together own more than 40 percent of the world's carrying capacity.

> `owners` [source_located] UNCTAD (RMT 2025): the top three ship-owning nations, Greece, China and Japan, accounted for over 40.7 per cent; Greece was the top ship-owning nation with 16.4 per cent of global fleet capacity. (sources: unctad_2025)  
> `regs_2026` [source_located] UNCTADstat (June 2026): at the start of 2026 Liberia (447 million dwt), Panama (350 million dwt) and the Marshall Islands (318 million dwt) were the largest registries; world fleet capacity 2.5 billion dwt, around 116,000 vessels of at least 100 GT; Greece owns 397 million dwt, China 377 million, Japan 243 million; Asian companies own 56% of capacity, European 34%, North American 4%. (sources: unctadstat_2026)

## m2_prohibition - How it started

In the nineteen twenties, alcohol was banned in the United States. Ship owners who wanted to serve drinks to their passengers found a way out: they registered their ships in Panama. Panama became the first open registry, a flag that anyone, from any country, could fly. And as American shipping laws grew stricter, more and more owners followed them there.

> `panama_1920s` [source_located] Windward: Panama was the first open registry; facing tightening shipping laws in the United States, many owners registered there; during Prohibition in the 1920s ship owners who wanted to serve alcohol registered their ships in Panama. (sources: windward_foc)  

## m2_liberia - How it started

After the Second World War, a historian found, Panama's officials started demanding so much in private payments that ship owners wanted an alternative. In 1948, a former American Secretary of State, Edward Stettinius, worked with the government of Liberia to open a new registry. And to this day, the Liberian registry is run from an office in Virginia, in the United States. That registry would grow into the biggest in the world.

> `liberia_1948` [source_located] Windward: in 1948 former U.S. Secretary of State Edward Stettinius worked with the Liberian government to open its registry, which is operated out of Virginia. Carlisle (1980) describes a flag of convenience as one 'whereunder a nation exploits the need of operators for a haven from high standards'; Panamanian officials came to demand such private tribute after World War II that a new flag of convenience was needed; Stettinius's development company provided it under the flag of Liberia in the late 1940s. (sources: windward_foc, carlisle_1980)  
> `top3` [source_located] UNCTAD (RMT 2025): as of 1 January 2025 the top three flag States, Liberia, Panama and the Marshall Islands, together accounted for 45.1 per cent of world ship carrying capacity; Liberia alone 17.4 per cent. (sources: unctad_2025)  

## m2_term - How it started

The idea spread so fast that it got its own name. In the nineteen fifties, people started calling it a flag of convenience: a flag chosen not for where the ship comes from, but for what it saves. One historian described it more bluntly: a haven from high standards.

> `term_1950s` [source_located] Windward: the term 'flag of convenience' was coined in the 1950s. (sources: windward_foc)

(sources: windward_foc, carlisle_1980)

## m3_reasons - Why owners do it

According to the maritime analytics firm Windward, owners choose a flag of convenience to escape stricter rules at home, to cut operating costs, to avoid high taxes, and, in the worst cases, to get around the laws that protect sailors' wages and working conditions. Panama, for example, offers registration online and lets owners hire low-cost crews from anywhere.

> `definition` [source_located] Windward: a flag of convenience means a ship is registered in a different country than the one where its owner resides; owners choose it to avoid strict rules and safety standards, reduce operating costs, avoid high taxes and bypass laws that protect workers' wages and conditions. (sources: windward_foc)  

## m3_scale - Why owners do it

And it works at an enormous scale. In twenty twenty-six, Liberia became the first registry in history to pass 300 million tons of ships, with about 6,000 ships flying its flag. Liberia alone carries 17 percent of the world's shipping capacity. It says it flies almost a quarter of all the world's container ships, the ones that carry the boxes with your online orders.

> `liberia_2026` [source_located] FrontPage Africa (June 2026): the Liberian registry became the first to surpass 300 million gross tons, reaching 307.3 million GT as of June 8 (Clarksons), with 6,092 vessels (IHS). (sources: fpa_2026)  
> `top3` [source_located] UNCTAD (RMT 2025): as of 1 January 2025 the top three flag States, Liberia, Panama and the Marshall Islands, together accounted for 45.1 per cent of world ship carrying capacity; Liberia alone 17.4 per cent. (sources: unctad_2025)  
> `liberia_new` [source_located] LISCR (Sept 2026 data, Clarksons/IHS): 25.94% of the world's newbuilding fleet is flagged with the Liberian Registry; Liberia has over 23.96% of the world's container ships. (sources: liscr_2026)  

## m3_money - Why owners do it

So what does Liberia earn? Registration fees, yearly tonnage taxes, inspections and certificates. A local researcher estimates the country's maritime revenue at about 30 million dollars in twenty twenty-six. For a country like Liberia, that matters. But it is a tiny slice of an industry that carries the world's trade. And exactly how much? The researcher says only the registry and Liberia's maritime authority hold the full records, which is why he is calling for more transparency.

> `liberia_rev` [source_located] AllAfrica (July 2026): maritime revenue is an important contributor to Liberia's national budget, estimated at approximately US$30 million in 2026; income includes registration fees, recurring annual tonnage taxes and inspection and certification fees (researcher George Tengbeh). Only the Liberia Maritime Authority and LISCR hold full financial records; he calls for transparency. (sources: allafrica_2026)

## m4_abandon - The dark side

It happens more and more. According to the International Transport Workers' Federation, about 3,100 sailors were abandoned by their ship owners in twenty twenty-four, up from about 1,700 the year before. Left on board, far from home, often without pay, food or a way back. They were owed more than 20 million dollars in unpaid wages.

> `abandon` [source_located] ITF via gCaptain (Jan 2025): flags with most abandonments: Panama (43), Palau (37), Tanzania (30), Comoros (29), Cameroon (20), Bahrain (16). About 3,133 seafarers were abandoned by shipowners in 2024 versus 1,676 in 2023; 312 vessels (132 in 2023); 81 percent (253) of abandoned vessels sailed under a flag of convenience; Panama had the most (43); US$20.1 million owed in unpaid wages. (sources: itf_2024)  

## m4_ships - The dark side

## m4_falseflags - The dark side

Some ships go further, and fly flags that are not real at all. Researchers at the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air counted 113 Russian vessels flying a false flag in the first nine months of twenty twenty-five, often to dodge sanctions on oil. And the shadow fleet keeps finding new homes: six registries that had never flagged a Russian ship before the invasion of Ukraine were flying 162 shadow vessels between them by September.

> `falseflags` [source_located] Al Jazeera (Nov 2025), citing the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (CREA): a total of 113 Russian vessels flew a false flag in the first nine months of 2025; 96 sanctioned vessels had flown a false flag at least once by the end of September; six registries that had not flagged a Russian ship before February 2022 had at least 10 such vessels each, 162 shadow vessels in total. (sources: crea_2025)

## m5_everyone - Why it survives

Because almost everyone gains something. Owners save money. Small countries earn fees. Shipping stays cheap, and so do the goods it carries, all the way to your local store. The world's fleet now carries 2.5 billion tons of cargo capacity, and most of it moves under a flag of choice. Changing that would mean higher costs for owners, less income for small countries, and, in the end, higher prices for everyone.

> `regs_2026` [source_located] UNCTADstat (June 2026): at the start of 2026 Liberia (447 million dwt), Panama (350 million dwt) and the Marshall Islands (318 million dwt) were the largest registries; world fleet capacity 2.5 billion dwt, around 116,000 vessels of at least 100 GT; Greece owns 397 million dwt, China 377 million, Japan 243 million; Asian companies own 56% of capacity, European 34%, North American 4%. (sources: unctadstat_2026)  
> "Most of it" summarises the UNCTAD finding that flag and owner usually differ; no new figure.  

## m5_quality - Why it survives

And the big registries compete hard to look like the safe choice. Liberia says about a quarter of the world's newly built ships are joining its flag. But the list of abandoned ships tells a mixed story: Panama tops it, followed by smaller flags like Palau, Tanzania, Comoros, Cameroon and Bahrain.

## m5_lesson - Why it survives

The real question is not where a ship is registered. It is whether anyone, anywhere, is truly responsible for it. Because a flag is supposed to be a promise: that someone will check the ship, protect the crew, and answer when things go wrong.
