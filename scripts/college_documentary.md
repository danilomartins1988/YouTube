## m1_case - The settlement

It started with a class action by college athletes against the NCAA and the biggest conferences. The case was about money from the athletes' names, images and likenesses, including the television deals built on their games. Most of the damages, about two billion dollars, were set aside for exactly that, the athletes' share of name and image money from the past.

> `deal` [source_located] Final approval order (Jun 6, 2025, Judge Wilken): damages '$2.576 billion in total', 'paid yearly over the course of ten years' ($1.976bn NIL fund + $600M); class of 389,700; schools may share 'up to 22% of the Power Five schools' average athletic revenues each year, subject to specified yearly increases'; plaintiffs' expert estimate: more than $20M per school in 2025-26, $32.9M by 2034-35, and 115,000+ additional scholarships (estimates). Schools 'free to allocate' in a Title IX-compliant way; Title IX claims not released. Appeals pending. (sources: order)

## m3_roster - Fewer seats

The old system limited how many scholarships a team could give. The new one limits how many athletes a team can have at all. Football rosters are capped at one hundred and five. Basketball at fifteen. Baseball at thirty-four. Softball at twenty-five. Golf at nine. The plaintiffs' expert estimated that removing the old scholarship caps could open more than one hundred and fifteen thousand new scholarships across the country, another estimate, not a count.

> `roster` [source_located] NCAA (Jun 23, 2025): Division I rules 'no longer include sport-specific scholarship limits'; roster limits from July 1, 2025 - football 105, basketball 15, baseball 34, softball 25, golf 9; athletes cut or who would have been cut are exempt for their careers. (sources: ncaa)  

## m3_exempt - Fewer seats

Any school may now give a scholarship to every athlete on its roster, if it can afford to. But for walk-ons, the players who joined teams without scholarships, the new limits could mean losing a spot. Athletes who were cut, or would have been, are exempt from the limits for the rest of their college careers.

## m4_nil - The referee

Athletes can still sign deals with outside companies for their name, image and likeness, known as NIL. ## m5_johnson - Students or employees

In twenty twenty-four, a federal appeals court ruled in a case called Johnson versus the NCAA. It said athletes cannot be barred from claiming wages just because they are called amateurs. Courts must look at the economic reality of what they do. The court sent the case back to a lower court to do exactly that, and the question is still open.

## m5_congress - Students or employees

Washington is trying to write the rules. In April twenty twenty-six, the President signed an executive order asking for revenue sharing that preserves or expands women's and Olympic sports. Its main sections took effect in August. An executive order can push the schools, but it cannot rewrite the settlement or the labor laws on its own. In September, the Senate passed a bill called the Protect College Sports Act, seventy-seven to twenty-two. The House has not voted on it, so it is not law.

## payoff - The lesson

So how did college sports get a twenty million dollar payroll? Because a court decided the old rules could not stand, and the schools agreed to pay. The money is real, but it comes from the same budgets that fund every other team. The rich programs can carry it. The rest are deciding which seats to remove. For the athletes, the money is real. For the smaller sports, the question is whether there will still be a team to play on. And the courts and Congress have not yet said who the players really are.
