## m4_pjm - The data center rush

Nowhere is this clearer than in PJM, the grid operator for a large part of the eastern United States, including Virginia, the world's biggest cluster of data centers. On July second, twenty twenty-six, PJM's demand peaked at one hundred and sixty-two point six gigawatts. Every year PJM holds an auction to pay power plants to be ready for peak demand. ## m5_card - The utility deal




## m5_model - The utility deal

Most American homes buy power from a regulated utility. The utility cannot set its own prices. A state commission does. Texas rules put it simply. The cost of service has two parts, allowable expenses, and a return on invested capital.

> `model` [source_located] Texas PUC §25.231: 'The two components of cost of service are allowable expenses and return on invested capital.' 'The return on invested capital is the rate of return times invested capital.' CPUC SB 695 report: rate base assets are those 'for which they may earn a profit. Other things being equal, a larger rate base results in higher net income for utilities'; fuel and purchased power 'are pass-through expenses; the utility receives no mark up or profit on these costs.' CPUC D.25-12-043: 'A Test Year 2026 ROE of 9.98% ... for PG&E'. (sources: puct, cpuc)  

## m5_incentive - The utility deal

And here is the key detail. California regulators explain that utilities earn no markup on fuel. It is passed straight through. But on poles, wires and substations, the rate base, they can earn a profit. Other things being equal, a larger rate base means higher income for the utility.

## payoff - The lesson

So why are America's electricity bills rising? Not because electricity got harder to make. Because the system that delivers it is being rebuilt, the fuel became volatile, and a new generation of machines is competing with your home for the same power. The light switch looks the same as ever. The economics behind it have changed.
