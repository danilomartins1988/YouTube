# The Day Volkswagen Became the World's Most Valuable Company

_In October 2008, in the middle of a financial crisis, a struggling carmaker briefly became the most valuable company on Earth. Not because it sold more cars, but because too many investors had bet against it, and there were not enough shares left to buy back._

## h_question - The impossible day

What if, in the worst week of a financial crisis, a carmaker in trouble suddenly became the most valuable company in the world? Not for a year. Not for a month. For about a day.

## h_stakes - The impossible day

That happened to Volkswagen in October twenty oh eight. In two trading days, its share price jumped from about two hundred and sixty-six dollars to about one thousand two hundred and fifty dollars. For a brief moment, the whole company was worth about three hundred and seventy billion dollars, more than ExxonMobil, the world's biggest company at the time. Nothing about its cars had changed. What changed was a simple question of arithmetic. How many shares were left?

## m1_draw - The bet against Volkswagen

Let me draw how you bet against a share. It is called short selling. First, you borrow a share from someone who owns it. Then you sell it right away, at today's price. Later, you buy a share back on the market and return it to the lender. If the price has fallen, you keep the difference. But here is the catch: if the price rises instead, you still have to buy it back. And a price can rise without any limit.

> `float` [source_located] International Banker: with Lower Saxony's 20%, less than 6% of VW voting stock was available in the market, while around 12% of VW's outstanding shares had been sold short, so it was impossible for every short seller to buy back their shares. Risk.net: the surge is widely attributed to short sellers trying to close out positions from a smaller pool of shares than they previously thought were available. (sources: ib_squeeze, risk_options)  
> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> General explanation of short selling, as described by International Banker; the "no limit" point is the standard risk of a short position.  

## m1_why - The bet against Volkswagen

In twenty oh eight, Volkswagen looked like an easy target. It was a carmaker carrying a lot of debt. Many hedge funds thought its shares had become too expensive. And then the financial crisis hit, and demand for new cars collapsed. If you wanted to bet that a share would fall, Volkswagen seemed like a good one.

> `march` [source_located] International Banker: by March 2008 Porsche owned 31% of Volkswagen and said it was not looking to go to 75%: 'In view of the fact that Lower Saxony as second largest shareholder owns 20 percent of VW, the probability of acquiring the necessary shares in freefloat is extremely low.' VW was a 'troubled carmaker, which had amassed substantial debt'; many hedge funds held the view the stock had become overvalued and increased short positions; as the crisis unfolded, demand for new cars collapsed. (sources: ib_squeeze)  

## m1_porsche - The bet against Volkswagen

But there was one buyer who kept buying. Porsche, the sports car maker from Stuttgart, much smaller than Volkswagen, had been building up a stake in its giant neighbour. By March twenty oh eight, Porsche owned thirty-one percent of Volkswagen. Would it go all the way to seventy-five percent? Porsche said that was very unlikely. The state of Lower Saxony, Volkswagen's second largest shareholder, owned twenty percent. So there simply weren't enough shares out there.

> `march` [source_located] International Banker: by March 2008 Porsche owned 31% of Volkswagen and said it was not looking to go to 75%: 'In view of the fact that Lower Saxony as second largest shareholder owns 20 percent of VW, the probability of acquiring the necessary shares in freefloat is extremely low.' VW was a 'troubled carmaker, which had amassed substantial debt'; many hedge funds held the view the stock had become overvalued and increased short positions; as the crisis unfolded, demand for new cars collapsed. (sources: ib_squeeze)

## m2_sunday - The Sunday announcement

On Sunday, October twenty-sixth, twenty oh eight, Porsche published a press release. It said that it held forty-two point six percent of Volkswagen's ordinary shares. And on top of that, thirty-one point five percent more in so-called cash-settled options, a kind of financial contract linked to Volkswagen's share price. Together, seventy-four point one percent. Its aim, it said, was to reach seventy-five percent in twenty oh nine. And the Porsche and Piëch families, who own all of Porsche's voting shares, backed the plan to take control of Volkswagen.

> `stake` [source_located] Porsche SE (26 Oct 2008): at the end of last week it held 42.6% of the Volkswagen ordinary shares and in addition 31.5% in so-called cash-settled options relating to VW ordinary shares, to hedge against price risks, a total of 74.1%; on settlement Porsche receives in cash the difference between the share price and the strike price; the aim is to increase to 75% in 2009, paving the way to a domination agreement; the families Porsche and Piëch, who own all Porsche ordinary shares, expressed their unconditioned backing and support for a domination of the Volkswagen group by Porsche. Risk.net: this was more than the 35.14% Porsche had disclosed on 16 September. (sources: porsche_oct26, risk_options)  

## m2_options - The Sunday announcement

Here is how they worked. When an option was settled, Porsche would receive in cash the difference between Volkswagen's share price and an agreed price, called the strike. Those options were the surprise. Porsche said they were there to protect it against rising prices. Only a few weeks earlier, it had disclosed a stake of about thirty-five percent. Now the market could see the full picture, and it looked very different from what many short sellers had believed.

## m2_draw - The Sunday announcement

Let me draw the problem with a hundred shares. Porsche: about seventy-four, counting its options. Lower Saxony: twenty. What is left for everyone else? Less than six. Now, how many shares had been sold short? Around twelve. Twelve shares owed, and fewer than six for sale. Not every short seller could buy back. That is a short squeeze.

## m3_spike - The squeeze

The price explodes. On Friday, a Volkswagen share had cost about two hundred and sixty-six dollars. By Tuesday, October twenty-eighth, it touched about one thousand two hundred and fifty dollars. Short sellers were not buying because they liked the company. They were buying because they had to. Every purchase pushed the price higher, which made the losses of the next short seller even bigger.

> `price` [source_located] Risk.net: VW's share price rose to €1,005.01 on 28 October from €210.52 on 24 October, then closed at €512 on 29 October. Porsche SE (29 Oct): the price at one stage exceeded €1,000. Converted at the Fed H.10 rate of each day: 24 Oct 1.2639 → ≈ $266; 28 Oct 1.2476 → ≈ $1,254; 29 Oct 1.2851 → ≈ $658. (sources: risk_options, porsche_oct29, fed_h10)  

## m3_top - The squeeze

And for a brief moment, the numbers became absurd. At that price, Volkswagen's voting shares were worth about three hundred and seventy billion dollars. ExxonMobil, the giant oil company and the world's most valuable firm, was worth three hundred and forty-three billion dollars. A carmaker in the middle of a crisis was, on paper, the most valuable company on the planet.

> `top` [source_located] International Banker: Volkswagen became the world's largest company by market value on 28 October, very briefly; at up to €1,005 per share VW's voting stock was worth €296bn, exceeding the $343bn market capitalisation of the world's incumbent top company, ExxonMobil. Converted at the 28 Oct 2008 H.10 rate (1.2476): ≈ $369bn. (sources: ib_squeeze, fed_h10)  

## m3_tears - The squeeze

On the trading floors, it was chaos. One analyst in London said he had hedge fund managers literally in tears on the phone. And Germany's financial regulator, BaFin, said the same day that it was examining the trading for signs of insider trading or market manipulation. Porsche denied all of it.

> `bafin` [source_located] International Banker: BaFin announced on the same day (28 Oct) that it was examining the VW share-price activity for evidence of insider trading or market manipulation; Porsche issued a blanket denial. Risk.net (7 Nov 2008): BaFin launched a formal investigation into market manipulation of VW shares. Porsche SE (29 Oct): Porsche rejects responsibility for the price movements; BaFin was informed in advance about the settlement measure. (sources: ib_squeeze, risk_options, porsche_oct29)  
> `fall` [source_located] International Banker: after the peak on the 28th, the stock plunged 58 percent in just four days; a month later it was down 70 percent from its peak. Hedge funds that had shorted VW stock lost an estimated $30 billion. 'I have had hedge fund managers literally in tears on the phone,' said a London-based analyst on the 29th. (sources: ib_squeeze)

## m4_porsche - Letting the air out

The first move came from Porsche itself. On October twenty-ninth, it said that short sellers were responsible for the extreme prices. To avoid more market distortions, it would settle options covering up to five percent of Volkswagen's shares, putting more shares within reach of those who needed them. Porsche added that it had informed the regulator in advance.

> `settle` [source_located] Porsche SE (29 Oct 2008): short sellers had to buy VW ordinary shares to fulfil their delivery obligations, which led to a massive increase in the price, at one stage above €1,000; to avoid further market distortions, Porsche intends, depending on the state of the market, to settle hedging transactions of up to 5% of VW ordinary shares; it still aims for up to 75%. International Banker: the following day Porsche unloaded around 5% of its VW holdings to the short sellers. (sources: porsche_oct29, ib_squeeze)  
> `bafin` [source_located] International Banker: BaFin announced on the same day (28 Oct) that it was examining the VW share-price activity for evidence of insider trading or market manipulation; Porsche issued a blanket denial. Risk.net (7 Nov 2008): BaFin launched a formal investigation into market manipulation of VW shares. Porsche SE (29 Oct): Porsche rejects responsibility for the price movements; BaFin was informed in advance about the settlement measure. (sources: ib_squeeze, risk_options, porsche_oct29)  

## m4_index - Letting the air out

The second move came from the stock exchange. Deutsche Börse runs the DAX, the index of Germany's top companies. After Tuesday's close, Volkswagen alone made up twenty-seven percent of the whole index. So the exchange brought forward a rule, and capped Volkswagen's weight at ten percent. Normally, that rule was only applied on fixed dates, and the next one was not until December twenty-second. This time, it would apply from Monday, November third.

> `dax_cap` [source_located] Deutsche Börse (28 Oct 2008): it decided to extraordinarily adjust the DAX weightings; the 10 percent capping rule is brought forward for Volkswagen as of the start of trading on Monday 3 November; at Tuesday's close Volkswagen was weighted with 27 percent; the next regular adjustment date was 22 December. (sources: dbg_cap)  

## m4_draw - Letting the air out

Let me draw why that mattered. An index is like a basket of companies. Many funds simply copy the basket, holding each company at its weight. When one company becomes a giant slice of the basket, those funds need more of it, at the worst possible moment. Cap the slice at ten percent, and that pressure eases.

> `dax_cap` [source_located] Deutsche Börse (28 Oct 2008): it decided to extraordinarily adjust the DAX weightings; the 10 percent capping rule is brought forward for Volkswagen as of the start of trading on Monday 3 November; at Tuesday's close Volkswagen was weighted with 27 percent; the next regular adjustment date was 22 December. (sources: dbg_cap)

## m4_fall - Letting the air out

And the price came down as fast as it went up. On Wednesday, the share closed at about six hundred and sixty dollars, about half its peak of the day before. Within four days of the peak, it had fallen fifty-eight percent. A month later, it was down seventy percent. But for many short sellers, it was too late. They had already bought back at sky-high prices. Hedge funds that had bet against Volkswagen lost an estimated thirty billion dollars.

## m5_gain - The hunter and the hunted

On paper, Porsche looked brilliant. For its business year twenty oh seven to twenty oh eight, it reported a gain of about ten billion dollars on its Volkswagen options alone. A year earlier, it had already made about four point nine billion dollars this way. That was most of its profit of about twelve point seven billion dollars. A large part of a sports car maker's profit came from financial contracts on another company's shares.

> `options_gain` [source_located] Risk.net (7 Nov 2008): Porsche made a €6.83bn gain in its 2007/08 business year on cash-settled options on VW shares (previous year €3.59bn); group profit €8.6bn in 2007/08. Converted at the 2008 H.10 average (1.4726): ≈ $10.1bn and ≈ $12.7bn; the previous year's €3.59bn at the 2007 average (1.3711): ≈ $4.9bn. (sources: risk_options, fed_h10)  

## m5_merger - The hunter and the hunted

But the plan was to take over Volkswagen, and the crisis made that much harder. Less than a year later, the roles were reversed. In August twenty oh nine, the two companies agreed to merge. Volkswagen would first buy forty-two percent of Porsche for four point seven billion dollars, and Porsche would become a brand inside the Volkswagen group. Volkswagen's chief executive, Martin Winterkorn, would run both companies. And the Gulf state of Qatar was expected to take a direct stake in Volkswagen, by buying options that Porsche held. The news agency AFP called it the failure of a gamble to take over the far larger Volkswagen. The hunter had become the hunted.

> `merger` [source_located] AFP (13 Aug 2009): VW and Porsche approved a plan to merge by 2011; VW will initially buy 42% of Porsche by the end of 2009 for €3.3bn (US$4.7bn), valuing Porsche at €12.4bn; Porsche to be integrated as VW's 10th brand; VW CEO Martin Winterkorn becomes head of both; 'the failure of a gamble begun by Porsche's management last year to take over the far larger VW'; a clear victory for VW chairman Ferdinand Piëch; Winterkorn had previously announced that Qatar would take a direct 17% stake in VW by acquiring stock options owned by Porsche, and negotiations with Qatar would continue. Valuation converted at the 2009 H.10 average (1.3935): ≈ $17bn. (sources: afp_merger, fed_h10)  

## m5_court - The hunter and the hunted

And the courts? Prosecutors in Stuttgart charged Porsche's former chief executive, Wendelin Wiedeking, and its former finance chief, Holger Härter, with market manipulation. In March twenty sixteen, the court acquitted them on all charges. It found that their statements in twenty oh eight were neither false nor misleading. The prosecutors dropped their appeal, and the verdict became final.

> `acquittal` [source_located] Porsche SE (28 Jul 2016): the Stuttgart Regional Court acquitted former Porsche SE executive board members Wendelin Wiedeking and Holger Härter on 18 March 2016 of alleged information-based market manipulation on all charges; the six indicted statements (10 March to 26 October 2008) were neither false nor misleading, and it was not proven they influenced the price; the prosecutor withdrew its appeal, making the acquittal legally binding; the motion for a fine against Porsche SE was finally rejected. (sources: porsche_2016)  

## m5_draw - The hunter and the hunted

Let me draw the lesson of this squeeze. A short seller can lose more than they bet, because a price has no ceiling. When too many people bet the same way, the exit gets crowded. And when someone holds positions the market cannot fully see, the number of shares that are really for sale can be much smaller than everyone thinks.

## payoff - The lesson

So, how did Volkswagen become the world's most valuable company? Not by selling more cars. Not by inventing anything. It happened because, for a day, its shares were the scarcest thing in the market. A price is not only what a company is worth. Sometimes it is just what the last desperate buyer has to pay.
