# The Bank That Accidentally Sent $900 Million

_In August twenty twenty, Citibank meant to send about seven point eight million dollars. It sent almost nine hundred million. Some lenders refused to give it back, and a federal judge said they could keep it. How three checkboxes, six eyes and one old legal rule turned a typo into a two-year court fight._

## h_stakes - The nine-hundred-million-dollar click

On August eleventh, twenty twenty, Citibank meant to pay about seven point eight million dollars of interest on behalf of the cosmetics company Revlon. Instead, it sent almost nine hundred million dollars of its own money. A federal judge later called it one of the biggest blunders in banking history. And then he ruled that much of the money did not have to come back.

## m1_loan - The middleman

In twenty sixteen, Revlon borrowed one point eight billion dollars through a syndicated loan. That means the money did not come from one bank, but from dozens of lenders, mostly investment funds. Someone has to sit in the middle, collect Revlon's payments and pass them on to every lender. That job is called the administrative agent. And Revlon's agent was Citibank.

> `loan` [source_located] Furman opinion: in 2016, Revlon took out a seven-year, $1.8 billion syndicated loan; Citibank is the Administrative Agent, which receives payments from Revlon and passes them on to the lenders. Kelley Drye: the loan was not due until 2023. (sources: furman, kelleydrye)  

## m1_draw - The middleman

When an interest payment is due, Revlon sends the money to Citibank, and Citibank splits it and sends each lender its share. The agent does not lend. It only moves the money. But that means the agent's computers are connected to the bank's own vault. If the agent sends too much, the extra comes out of Citibank's own pocket.

## m1_trouble - The middleman

By twenty twenty, Revlon was in deep trouble. In May, it raised more than eight hundred million dollars in new financing, partly by moving assets that had protected the old loan into a new deal. Many lenders were furious. On the market, the old loan was trading for less than thirty cents on the dollar. In other words, investors doubted they would ever be paid back in full.

> `distress` [source_located] Furman opinion: in May 2020 Revlon completed a transaction that obtained over $800 million in new financing and moved collateral that had secured the 2016 loan to a new facility; some lenders challenged it, and on August 12, 2020 lenders authorized UMB Bank to sue Revlon and Citibank over it; Revlon was backed by Ronald Perelman's MacAndrews & Forbes, which owned approximately 85% of Revlon. Kelley Drye: Revlon was in severe financial distress, with the loan trading for less than 30 cents on the dollar; Revlon has since filed for chapter 11. (sources: furman, kelleydrye)

## m2_task - Three checkboxes

The task looked simple. As part of a deal with some of its lenders, Revlon had agreed to pay interest to all of its old lenders early. Citibank's payment software, called Flexcube, had an awkward quirk. The easiest way to send only the interest was to enter the transaction as if Revlon were paying off the entire loan, and then redirect the principal into an internal account, called a wash account, so that the money never left the bank.

> `checkbox` [source_located] Furman opinion: Citibank's payments ran on Flexcube; the easiest way to pay the interest was to enter a full payoff and send the principal to an internal 'wash account'; the manual said 'ALL of the below field[s] must be set to the wash account: FRONT[;] FUND[; and] PRINCIPAL'; between 5:15 and 5:45 p.m. the maker checked only PRINCIPAL; the 'six-eye' procedure requires three people to review and approve; the checker and the senior approver ('Looks good, please proceed. Principal is going to wash.') also believed it was correct; a 'stop sign' warned 'Account used is Wire Account and Funds will be sent out of the bank. Do you want to continue?' without showing the amount. Several team members were Wipro employees in India working exclusively for Citibank. (sources: furman, reg_2021)  

## m2_boxes - Three checkboxes

According to the court, Citibank's own manual said that to keep the principal inside, three fields on the screen all had to be set to the wash account. They were called front, fund, and principal. The employee who built the payment, part of a team in India working for Citibank through the outsourcing firm Wipro, ticked only one. Principal.

## m4_rule - Finders keepers

Usually, yes. Under New York law, a payment made by mistake generally has to be returned. But there is an old exception, called discharge for value. If you are owed money, and someone pays you that exact amount, and you had no reason to suspect a mistake, you can keep it. As New York's highest court once put it, you should be able to treat the payment as final.

> `ruling_2021` [source_located] Furman opinion (16 Feb 2021, after a December 2020 bench trial): New York law generally requires mistaken payments to be returned, but the 'discharge-for-value' defense lets a creditor keep money it is owed if it had no notice of the mistake (Banque Worms, 1991: such a beneficiary 'should be able to consider the transfer of funds as a final and complete transaction'); 'the Court holds that Citibank is not entitled to its money back. Instead, Defendants' clients are entitled to keep the money.' Banking Dive: Citi said it would appeal. (sources: furman, bankingdive)  

## m4_draw - Finders keepers

If the answer to all three is yes, the arrow leads to keeping it. If any answer is no, the arrow leads back to the sender. The whole case came down to that third box.

## m5_reverse - The appeal

On September eighth, twenty twenty-two, the federal appeals court in New York reversed the ruling. First, the loan was not due until twenty twenty-three. So the lenders were not owed that money yet, and the old exception did not protect them.

> `ruling_2022` [source_located] Torys / Kelley Drye (Sep-Oct 2022): the Second Circuit reversed and remanded on September 8, 2022; the lenders were not bona fide creditors because the debt was not yet due; they were on inquiry notice given four red flags: no prepayment notice from Revlon; Revlon paying off the loan three years early despite poor finances; it could have bought the debt for under 30 cents on the dollar; and an exchange offer days earlier designed to prevent acceleration of the loan. Torys: the market had already added erroneous-payment provisions drafted by the Loan Syndication and Trading Association. (sources: torys, kelleydrye)

## m5_flags - The appeal

And days earlier, it had launched a deal designed to stop the loan from coming due early.

## payoff - The lesson

So what does this story say about the money system? That the biggest transfers in the world still pass through a few people looking at a screen. Six eyes are only safe if each pair is really looking, not trusting the other two. A warning that does not show the amount is not much of a warning. And when money arrives that seems too good to be true, the law may one day ask a simple question. Why didn't you call?
