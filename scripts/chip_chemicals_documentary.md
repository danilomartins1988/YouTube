# The Chemicals Nobody Talks About That Decide Who Makes Chips

_Everyone talks about the machines that make chips. But the most advanced factory in the world can be stopped by a bottle of liquid, a tank of gas or a silver metal. Who makes those materials, and how three countries have already used them as weapons._

## h_question - The bottle

What if the most advanced chip factory on Earth could be stopped, not by a broken machine, but by a missing bottle of liquid?

## h_stakes - The bottle

It has almost happened, more than once. A chemical from Japan, a gas from Ukraine, a metal from China. Each time, the chip industry discovered that its weakest link was not a machine worth hundreds of millions of dollars, but something that fits in a truck.

## m1_resist - The liquid that draws circuits

A strong candidate is photoresist. It is a light-sensitive liquid spread across the silicon wafer. When the lithography machine shines its pattern of light, the resist changes wherever the light hits it. Wash it, and the circuit design is left behind on the wafer, ready to be etched. No resist, no pattern. No pattern, no chip.

> `resist` [source_located] Tom's Hardware: photoresists are the light-sensitive materials that transfer circuit designs onto silicon wafers; they must be reformulated for each new process node; each new node requires hundreds of iterative test cycles between resist supplier and foundry. Reuters (2019): Tokyo Ohka Kogyo says it takes up to two years to develop new resists. (sources: thw_jsr, aj_2019)  

## m1_recipe - The liquid that draws circuits

And resist is not a commodity. Every new generation of chips needs a new recipe, tuned to the light, the machine and the chemistry of one factory. That takes hundreds of test cycles between the resist maker and the chipmaker. One of the biggest suppliers says developing a new resist can take up to two years.

> `resist` [source_located] Tom's Hardware: photoresists are the light-sensitive materials that transfer circuit designs onto silicon wafers; they must be reformulated for each new process node; each new node requires hundreds of iterative test cycles between resist supplier and foundry. Reuters (2019): Tokyo Ohka Kogyo says it takes up to two years to develop new resists. (sources: thw_jsr, aj_2019)  

## m1_japan - The liquid that draws circuits

For the most advanced chips, made with extreme ultraviolet light, the market belongs to Japan. According to the research firm QYResearch, three Japanese companies, Tokyo Ohka Kogyo, JSR and Shin-Etsu Chemical, hold about ninety percent of the world market for EUV resist. It is an estimate. But it means the most advanced factories in Taiwan, Korea and America all depend on a few chemical plants in Japan. And this resist sells for about one thousand eight hundred dollars a gallon.

> `euv_share` [source_located] Digital Today citing QYResearch: three Japanese companies, Tokyo Ohka Kogyo (TOK), JSR and Shin-Etsu Chemical, accounted for about 90% of the global EUV photoresist market; the average price of EUV photoresist is about $1,800 per gallon. Tom's Hardware: JSR holds roughly a quarter of the global photoresist market; TOK and Shin-Etsu are the first- and third-largest suppliers. (sources: dt_japan, thw_jsr)  

## m1_layers - The liquid that draws circuits

And the dependence grows with every generation. Moving from three nanometer chips to two nanometer chips adds more than thirty percent more layers printed with extreme ultraviolet light, according to Korean industry figures. Each layer needs its own coat of resist, and its own mask, printed on blank plates from yet another Japanese specialist, Hoya. The smaller the chip, the more of these materials it eats.

> `layers` [source_located] Digital Today (2026): compared with 3 nm, the number of EUV-applied layers in 2 nm processes increases by more than 30%; as layers increase, material consumption rises; Hoya dominates the market for EUV blank masks, the base plates for photomasks. (sources: dt_japan)  

## m1_draw - The liquid that draws circuits

Let me draw why a tiny market can hold so much power. The machine that prints the chip is worth hundreds of millions of dollars. The resist on a wafer costs very little by comparison. But the factory needs both. If either one is missing, the whole line stops. So the cheapest ingredient can carry the same power as the most expensive machine.

## m2_curbs - The test

On July first, twenty nineteen, in a decades-old dispute with South Korea over wartime forced labour, Japan announced it would restrict exports of three materials to Korea. Fluorinated polyimide, used in phone screens. Photoresist. And ultra-pure hydrogen fluoride, used to clean and etch wafers. Korea is home to two of the world's biggest memory makers, Samsung and SK hynix. And Japan supplied, by one estimate, about seventy percent or more of these materials to the whole world.

> `korea2019` [source_located] IHS (Jul 2019): on July 1, Japan's METI announced it would restrict exports of fluorinated polyimide, resist and hydrogen fluoride, used for semiconductor and display production, to South Korea. OLED-Info: Japan produces about 90% of fluorinated polyimide and resist. Reuters (Aug 2019): Japan provides about 70% or more of the restricted products to the world; Samsung stepped up testing of non-Japanese photoresists and hydrogen fluoride; Soulbrain aimed to match the purity of Japanese hydrogen fluoride. (sources: ihs_2019, oled_2019, aj_2019)  

## m2_scramble - The test

Korea scrambled. Samsung stepped up testing of resists and hydrogen fluoride from other suppliers. A Korean chemical maker, Soulbrain, raced to match the purity of the Japanese gas. Shipments from Japan now needed case-by-case permission. For the first time, the chip industry saw a supply of chemicals turned into a lever.

(sources: ihs_2019, oled_2019, aj_2019)  

## m2_after - The test

Did Korea break free? Partly. According to Korean industry reports, it now makes more of the ordinary materials at home. But for the most advanced chips, the pattern repeats. The finer the circuits, the more Korea depends on Japanese materials again. Every new generation of chips moves the finish line.

> `korea_after` [source_located] Digital Today (2026): after Japan's export restrictions in 2019, South Korea's localisation rate for general-purpose materials increased, but as processes become more advanced, dependence on Japan rises again; for High-NA EUV it is still difficult to secure yields without Japanese materials. (sources: dt_japan)

## m3_neon - The gas

Because of neon. Chipmakers use ultra-pure neon gas inside the lasers of their lithography machines. And in early twenty twenty-two, two Ukrainian companies, Ingas in Mariupol and Cryoin in Odessa, produced, by Reuters' calculation, forty-five to fifty-four percent of the world's neon for chipmaking. When Russia invaded, both shut down.

> `neon` [source_located] The Register citing Reuters (11 Mar 2022): Ingas (Mariupol) and Cryoin (Odessa) produce 45 to 54 percent of the world's neon for chip fabrication (Reuters calculation from company and Techcet data); both shut down amid Russian attacks; neon is needed as a buffer gas for lasers used in chipmaking; big chipmakers had safety stocks; moving production could take nine months to two years. CNBC (25 Mar 2022): neon is required for the lasers used in lithography; global chip neon consumption roughly 540 metric tons in 2021 (Techcet); historically up to 90% of chip-industry neon was a by-product of Russian steel manufacturing; Bain's Peter Hanbury estimated only about two-fifths of the neon used in chip production is now sourced from Russia and Ukraine; the industry took steps to limit neon risks after Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014. (sources: reg_neon, cnbc_neon)  

## m3_steel - The gas

Why Ukraine? Because neon is a by-product. It is collected from steelmaking, and historically up to ninety percent of the neon for the chip industry came as a by-product of Russian steel plants, with companies in Ukraine turning it into the ultra-pure gas chipmakers need. The whole chip industry used about about five hundred and forty tons of it in twenty twenty-one.

## m3_stocks - The gas

The big chipmakers survived. They had learned from an earlier scare in twenty fourteen, when Russia annexed Crimea, and kept safety stocks. But replacing the lost supply was slow. Building new neon purification lines elsewhere could take nine months to two years. Neon showed that a chokepoint does not need a government to switch it off. A war is enough.

## m3_now - The gas

In fact, after the scare of twenty fourteen, the industry had already started to spread its bets. By one consultant's estimate at Bain, only about two-fifths of the neon used for chips still came from Russia and Ukraine by the time of the invasion. The lesson had been learned once. It had to be learned again.

## m4_gallium - The metal

Meet gallium, a soft silver metal used in special chips for defense systems, telecom equipment and LEDs. In the United States, chips use about four in five of all the gallium consumed. And according to the US Geological Survey, China produced ninety-nine percent of the world's raw gallium in twenty twenty-four.

> `gallium` [source_located] USGS (Jan 2025): China accounted for 99% of worldwide primary low-purity gallium production; in August 2023 China implemented gallium export controls requiring licences; in December 2024 China banned all exports of gallium to the United States; primary gallium prices in China averaged $380/kg in June 2024, up 58% from $240/kg in June 2023, and $420/kg by October 2024; integrated circuits accounted for 79% of U.S. gallium consumption. (sources: usgs_ga)  

## m4_controls - The metal

In August twenty twenty-three, China began requiring licences for every gallium export. Prices jumped. Raw gallium in China went from about two hundred and forty dollars a kilogram in mid twenty twenty-three to four hundred and twenty dollars by October twenty twenty-four. Then, in December twenty twenty-four, China banned gallium exports to the United States altogether.

> `gallium` [source_located] USGS (Jan 2025): China accounted for 99% of worldwide primary low-purity gallium production; in August 2023 China implemented gallium export controls requiring licences; in December 2024 China banned all exports of gallium to the United States; primary gallium prices in China averaged $380/kg in June 2024, up 58% from $240/kg in June 2023, and $420/kg by October 2024; integrated circuits accounted for 79% of U.S. gallium consumption. (sources: usgs_ga)  

## m4_pause - The metal

A year later, after talks between the American and Chinese presidents, China suspended that ban. But it only paused it. Exporters still need a licence from Beijing for every sale, and the pause is set to expire on November twenty-seventh, twenty twenty-six. In other words, the tap is open, but the hand is still on it.

> `ga_suspend` [source_located] Evertiq / Reuters (10 Nov 2025): China suspended its ban on gallium and germanium exports to the US; the suspension remains in effect until November 27, 2026; exporters must still comply with licensing under China's dual-use export control list. (sources: evertiq_ga)  

## m4_draw - The metal

Let me draw three kinds of chokepoints. The first is skill: Japan makes resist that others cannot yet copy. The second is accident: neon ended up in Ukraine because that is where the steel was. The third is policy: China produces almost all the raw gallium and decides who gets it. Each one is fixed in a different way, and none of them quickly.

## m5_jsr - Owning the bottle

Japan answered that question with a purchase. In twenty twenty-four, a government-backed fund, the Japan Investment Corporation, bought JSR, one of the three big resist makers, in a deal worth about about six point four billion dollars. JSR left the stock market. A private chemical company became, in effect, a national asset. JSR had already bought an American start-up, Inpria, for five hundred and fourteen million dollars, to own the next generation of EUV resist.

> `jsr` [source_located] Tom's Hardware: Japan Investment Corporation (JIC), a government-backed fund, completed a tender offer for JSR in April 2024; JSR delisted on June 25, 2024; the deal valued the company at roughly ¥909 billion ($6.4 billion); JSR bought Inpria (metal oxide resist) for $514 million in 2021; at TSMC's request JSR set up a joint venture to build its first Taiwan photoresist plant in Yunlin, online as early as 2028; TOK and Shin-Etsu already produce in Taiwan. (sources: thw_jsr)  

## m5_mor - Owning the bottle

That next generation is called metal oxide resist. Instead of plastic-like molecules, it uses tin oxide, which, according to Inpria, absorbs extreme ultraviolet light about roughly five times more efficiently. JSR is now ramping the world's first large-scale factory for it, in South Korea. Japan is not only defending today's recipe. It is buying tomorrow's.

> `mor` [source_located] Tom's Hardware: JSR is ramping the world's first production-scale facility for metal oxide resist (MOR) in South Korea, a next-generation EUV chemistry acquired with Inpria; tin-oxide MOR absorbs EUV photons roughly five times more efficiently than organic resists, according to Inpria. (sources: thw_jsr)

## m5_taiwan - Owning the bottle

And the materials are moving closer to the factories. At the request of TSMC, JSR is building its first photoresist plant in Taiwan, expected to open as early as twenty twenty-eight. Its rivals Tokyo Ohka Kogyo and Shin-Etsu already produce on the island. The reason is speed. Every new chip generation needs hundreds of test cycles, and shipping samples across the sea for each one costs weeks.

## m5_lesson - Owning the bottle

But notice what that means. Taiwan gets the resist plants, and Japan keeps the recipes. Korea makes more of its own materials, but still buys the most advanced ones. America looks at restarting gallium production, but China still produces almost all of it. Every country is building a little more of the chain. Nobody owns all of it.

## payoff - The lesson

So who decides who makes chips? The machine makers, yes. But also the companies that make a liquid most people have never heard of, the steel plants that happen to collect a gas, and the country that produces a silver metal. The most powerful thing in a chip factory is not always the biggest. Sometimes it is the bottle on the shelf that nobody else knows how to fill.
