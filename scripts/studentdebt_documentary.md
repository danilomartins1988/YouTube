## m1_tuition - The price of a degree

Start with the price tag. According to the College Board, the average published tuition and fees at a public four-year college, for students from the same state, was about six thousand three hundred and twenty dollars in the school year that began in nineteen ninety-six, measured in today's dollars. This year, it is twelve thousand two hundred and ninety dollars. At private nonprofit colleges, it went from about twenty-seven thousand five hundred to forty-six thousand three hundred and fifty dollars. Those numbers are already adjusted for inflation.

> `tuition` [source_located] College Board 2026: 'Between 1996-97 and 2026-27, the average published tuition and fees increased ... from $6,320 to $12,290 for public four-year in-state students, and from $27,530 to $46,350 for private nonprofit four-year students, after adjusting for inflation.' 'Over the decade between 2016-17 and 2026-27 ... declined by 8% for public four-year in-state students'. Net tuition public four-year 'peaked in 2012-13 at $4,600 (in 2026 dollars) and declined to an estimated $1,920 in 2026-27.' ## m1_twist - The price of a degree

But there is a twist the headlines often miss. Over the last ten years, the inflation-adjusted sticker price at public four-year colleges actually fell by eight percent. And after grants and scholarships, the average net tuition paid by first-time, full-time in-state students there dropped from a peak of about four thousand six hundred dollars to an estimated one thousand nine hundred and twenty this year. The sticker price and the real price are not the same thing.

## m1_grads - The price of a degree

Still, most of the debt was already built up. Among students who finished a bachelor's degree in twenty twenty-five at public and private nonprofit four-year colleges, forty-seven percent graduated with debt, and the average borrower owed thirty thousand two hundred and twenty dollars.

> `grads` [source_located] College Board 2026: 'Among 2024-25 bachelor's degree recipients from public and private nonprofit four-year institutions, 47% graduated with debt and the average debt among borrowers was $30,220.' (sources: cb)

## m2_gov - The biggest lender

It is the federal government. The Federal Reserve's figures show Washington holding about one point six three trillion dollars of student loans, the vast majority of the total. The Education Department counts about forty-two million people who have federal student loans.

## m2_growth - The biggest lender

The pile grew fast. At the start of two thousand and three, Americans owed about two hundred and forty billion dollars in student loans, according to the New York Fed. Today it is one point six five trillion dollars. By our calculation, that is almost seven times as much in twenty-three years.

## m2_profit - The biggest lender

Was the government making money on all those loans? For years, its own estimates said yes. The Government Accountability Office looked at loans made from nineteen ninety-seven to twenty twenty-one. At first, the Education Department expected them to earn the government one hundred and fourteen billion dollars. Later estimates flipped that to a cost of one hundred and ninety-seven billion dollars. A swing of three hundred and eleven billion dollars.

> `govcost` [source_located] GAO-22-105365 (Jul 2022): estimate for loans made 1997–2021 went from 'generating $114 billion in income for the government to costing $197 billion'; 'This swing of $311 billion'; 'The largest estimated cost increases—$102 billion in total—stemmed from emergency relief'. (sources: gao22)  

## m2_why - The biggest lender

The biggest single reason, about one hundred and two billion dollars of that swing, was the emergency relief of the pandemic, when payments and interest were paused. The rest came from changes in repayment plans and from estimates that turned out to be too optimistic about how much borrowers would repay.

> `govcost` [source_located] GAO-22-105365 (Jul 2022): estimate for loans made 1997–2021 went from 'generating $114 billion in income for the government to costing $197 billion'; 'This swing of $311 billion'; 'The largest estimated cost increases—$102 billion in total—stemmed from emergency relief'. ## m3_servicers - The middlemen

They are called loan servicers. They send the statements, take the payments and answer the phone. According to the Government Accountability Office, in September twenty twenty-five the biggest of them, Nelnet, handled about five hundred and six billion dollars of loans for twelve point four million borrowers. Four other companies split most of the rest.

## m3_fees - The middlemen

How do they get paid? Under the older contracts, the Education Department paid servicers a monthly fee for each borrower. About two dollars and eighty-five cents for a borrower in repayment. About a dollar for a student still in school. And only forty-five cents for a borrower more than two hundred and seventy days behind.

## m3_incentive - The middlemen

Think about what that pays for. A borrower in good standing was worth more to the servicer than one who fell far behind. Newer contracts from twenty twenty-four were meant to tie pay to performance, with penalties of up to twenty percent. But the government's own watchdog found that the agency overseeing them lost almost half its staff in twenty twenty-five, from one thousand four hundred and thirty-three people to seven hundred and seventy-seven.

## m3_private - The middlemen

And then there are the private lenders. Banks and other companies still lend to students, about about fifteen point four billion dollars in the last school year. That is far less than before the financial crisis, but it has been growing again for more than a decade.

## m4_scores - When the pause ended

When missed payments returned to credit reports in early twenty twenty-five, the effect was immediate. The New York Fed found that more than two point two million borrowers who became newly delinquent saw their credit scores drop by more than one hundred points.

> `delinq` [source_located] NY Fed workbook: share of student loan balances 90+ days delinquent 0.53% (2024:Q4), 7.74% (2025:Q1), 10.6% (2026:Q2). Liberty Street (May 2025): 'more than 2.2 million student loan borrowers who became newly delinquent saw their credit scores drop more than 100 points'. (sources: nyfed, lse25)  

## m4_rate - When the pause ended

And it kept climbing. By the middle of twenty twenty-six, ten point six percent of all student loan balances were at least ninety days past due. Before the pause ended, it was close to zero.

> `delinq` [source_located] NY Fed workbook: share of student loan balances 90+ days delinquent 0.53% (2024:Q4), 7.74% (2025:Q1), 10.6% (2026:Q2). Liberty Street (May 2025): 'more than 2.2 million student loan borrowers who became newly delinquent saw their credit scores drop more than 100 points'. (sources: nyfed, lse25)  

## m4_default - When the pause ended

Then came defaults. According to the New York Fed, roughly one million federal borrowers defaulted at the end of twenty twenty-five, and another two point six million in the first three months of twenty twenty-six. The Education Department's own data show defaulted Direct Loans more than doubling in a year, from eighty-two point six billion dollars to one hundred and eighty-seven point eight billion dollars.

> `defaults` [source_located] Liberty Street (May 2026): 'roughly 1 million federal student loan borrowers defaulted during 2025:Q4, with an additional 2.6 million borrowers defaulting during 2026:Q1'; 'credit scores for defaulted borrowers dropped 91 points'; 'collection efforts for defaulted federal student loan borrowers are currently suspended with no clear timeline for resumption.' FSA: Direct Loans cumulative in default $82.6bn (FY2025 Q3) → $187.8bn (FY2026 Q3). (sources: lse26, fsa)  

## m4_collect - When the pause ended

Default is serious. The government can take part of a paycheck, a tax refund, even Social Security. Collections restarted in May twenty twenty-five, and were then put on hold. In May twenty twenty-six, the New York Fed noted they were suspended with no clear timeline for resumption.

## m5_save - The new rules

The plan that was supposed to make payments cheaper, called SAVE, was blocked in court. About eight million borrowers who had enrolled were placed in forbearance. On March tenth, twenty twenty-six, a federal court vacated the plan.

> `newrules` [source_located] FSA GEN-25-04: 'On July 4, 2025, President Trump signed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21)'; 'The RAP ... will be in effect no later than July 1, 2026.' Federal Update (Mar 2026): 'Grad PLUS is eliminated'; graduate '$20,500 annually; $100,000 aggregate'; professional '$50,000 annually; $200,000 aggregate'; Parent PLUS '$20,000 annually or $65,000 lifetime'. GAO-26-107780: 'about 8 million borrowers who were enrolled in SAVE were placed in an administrative forbearance'; 'On March 10, 2026, a federal court order ... vacated the SAVE plan.' (sources: obbb, gao26)  

## m5_obbb - The new rules

Meanwhile, Congress passed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed on July fourth, twenty twenty-five. It eliminated Grad PLUS loans for graduate students. New graduate students now face a limit of twenty thousand five hundred dollars a year and one hundred thousand dollars in total. Parents face new limits too. And a new Repayment Assistance Plan takes effect by July twenty twenty-six.

## m5_draw - The new rules

## payoff - The lesson

So what is the business behind America's student debt? It was built on a simple promise. Borrow today, earn more tomorrow. For many graduates, that promise holds. But the bill has quietly been split between students, their parents, and taxpayers, while the money for the degree was paid on day one.
