## m1_machine - The machine

In fiscal twenty twenty-six, Microsoft brought in about three hundred and thirty-two billion dollars, up eighteen percent. And it now reports just two engines. Agents and Infra, the cloud, the A I platforms and the business software, made about two hundred and sixty-eight billion dollars, at an operating margin of about fifty-one percent. ## m1_azure - The machine

The engine inside the engine is Azure. It made about one hundred and two billion dollars last fiscal year. And in the September quarter alone, about thirty-three billion dollars, up forty-eight percent. That's about thirty-six percent of all of Microsoft's revenue. The Office cloud for businesses grew seventeen percent. Meanwhile, Windows sold with new PCs fell twenty-two percent.

> `azure` [source_located] Recast 8-K: Azure revenue FY2026 $101.9bn (FY2025 $72.6bn). 10-Q Q1 FY27: Azure $33.2bn (+48%), ≈ 36% of quarterly revenue (our calc.); Microsoft 365 commercial cloud $27.8bn (+17%); Windows OEM and devices $3.6bn (−22%). Microsoft Cloud gross margin fell to 66% 'driven by sales mix shift to Azure'. (sources: rel, tenq)  

## m1_openai - The machine

And one customer stands out. In that quarter, Microsoft booked ten billion dollars of revenue from its arrangements with OpenAI, about eleven percent of the total. Microsoft also owns about twenty-four percent of OpenAI on an as converted basis. So OpenAI is a partner, a shareholder story, and one of Azure's biggest buyers, all at once.

> `openai` [source_located] 10-Q: 'For the three months ended September 30, 2026, we recorded revenue from commercial arrangements with OpenAI, inclusive of revenue-sharing payments, of $10.0 billion' = 10.8% of Q1 revenue (our calc.). Microsoft holds 'an approximate 24% interest on an as-converted basis'; receivables from OpenAI $7.2bn. FY2026 10-K: no customer above 10% of annual revenue. (sources: tenq)  

## m1_lines - The machine

Under the hood, the product lines are moving at very different speeds. the cloud Office subscriptions made about twenty-eight billion dollars in the quarter, up seventeen percent. Search and advertising grew ten percent. But the older businesses are shrinking. Windows sales to PC makers fell twenty-two percent, Xbox slipped two percent, and classic software licenses dropped three percent. So the cloud engine runs at an operating margin of almost fifty-three percent, while the consumer side is going backwards.

> `lines` [source_located] 10-Q, revenue by product line, Q1 FY27 vs Q1 FY26 ($M; growth our calc.): Microsoft 365 cloud 27,778 (+17%); Search and advertising 6,554 (+10%); Productivity and server licensing 8,407 (-3%); XBOX 5,379 (-2%); Windows OEM and devices 3,551 (-22%). ## m3_lenses - What's priced in

## m3_proxy - What's priced in

So for a company in a building spree, we use a proxy. We take operating cash and subtract depreciation, about about thirty-eight billion dollars a year, as a rough stand-in for the spending needed just to keep things running. That leaves about one hundred and fifty-eight billion dollars, a yield of about four percent. It's a proxy, and it assumes capex eventually falls back toward that level.

> `proxy` [source_located] OUR proxy base for a capex boom (labelled proxy): operating cash flow $195.9bn minus depreciation of property and equipment ≈ $38.3bn TTM (SEC XBRL: Q2 FY26 $7.9bn, Q3 $9.0bn, Q4 $10.3bn, Q1 FY27 $11.1bn) = ≈ $157.6bn (yield ≈ 4.0%). It assumes capex eventually falls back to roughly the depreciation level. (sources: tenq, px)  

## m3_gauge - What's priced in

Now let's run it backwards. What growth in that cash, for ten years, makes today's price work, before slowing to three percent? With an eight percent discount rate, about five and a half percent a year. With ten percent, about ten percent. But run the same math on today's actual free cash flow, and the answer jumps to between about eighteen percent and about twenty-three percent a year.

> `rdcf` [source_located] OUR illustration (not a valuation): market cap $3,959.3bn = 10 years of cash growing at g plus a terminal value at 3%, discounted at r. On the PROXY base $157.6bn: r = 8% gives g ≈ 5.5% a year; r = 10% gives g ≈ 10.2%. On TTM FREE CASH FLOW $60.0bn: g ≈ 17.9% (8%) / ≈ 23.3% (10%); implied year-10 FCF ≈ $310bn / ≈ $489bn. (sources: tenq, px)  

## m3_mustearn - What's priced in

So the real question is the capex. Here's one more lens, our rough illustration. If Microsoft keeps spending at today's pace, every dollar of capex has to add about about eighteen cents to about thirty-two cents of extra operating cash every year, within a decade, for the price to make sense.

> `mustearn` [source_located] OUR rough illustration: if cash capex stays at the TTM pace ($135.9bn a year), the year-10 free cash flow the price implies (≈ $310bn at 8%, ≈ $489bn at 10%) needs operating cash of FCF plus capex; the extra over today's $195.9bn, spread over ten years of capex ($1,359bn), is ≈ 18 cents (8%) to ≈ 32 cents (10%) of extra yearly operating cash per dollar of capex. (sources: tenq, px)

## m5_capex - Risks and upside

Risk one is the build-out itself. Microsoft's own filing says these investments come in advance of fully developed revenue streams, and that the revenue may not arrive on time or at the expected levels. Overestimating demand could leave capacity sitting idle, and lead to write-downs.

> `risks` [source_located] 10-Q risk factors: AI investments 'are being made at significant scale and on an accelerated timeline ... and are in advance of fully developed revenue streams. The associated revenue may not be realized in the expected timeframes or at expected levels'; 'Overestimation of demand or misalignment of capacity investments may result in underutilization of infrastructure and may lead to impairment of assets'; partners that are 'significant customers of Azure' may not deliver the expected benefits; 'if pricing for AI products and services declines ... our margins ... could be adversely affected'. IRS 'is seeking an additional tax payment of $28.9 billion plus penalties and interest' (2004-2013; Microsoft disputes it). Competition scrutiny in the EU, UK, U.S. and China; tariffs 'could impact cloud and devices supply chain cost competitiveness'. (sources: tenq)
