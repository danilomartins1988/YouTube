## a1_police - Why it's called Black Friday

First, the name. It has nothing to do with profit. In nineteen sixty-one, a public relations newsletter reported that police officers in Philadelphia called the days after Thanksgiving Black Friday and Black Saturday. For them, it meant jammed streets, packed sidewalks and long shifts.

> `police` [source_located] Inquirer (2011), citing Bonnie Taylor-Blake's report to the American Dialect Society: a 1961 Public Relations News article said 'in Philadelphia, it became customary for officers to refer to the post-Thanksgiving days as Black Friday and Black Saturday'; the term referred to traffic problems. History.com: Philadelphia police used the term in the 1950s/60s for the chaos the day after Thanksgiving; merchants 'tried unsuccessfully to change it to Big Friday'. (sources: inq, hist)  

## a1_big - Why it's called Black Friday

Downtown merchants hated the name. According to History dot com, they tried to rebrand it as Big Friday. It didn't stick.

## a1_myth - Why it's called Black Friday

Then, in the late nineteen-eighties, retailers found a happier story. This is the day stores finally move from red ink to black ink, from loss to profit. It's a great story. But it's a reinvention. The police came first.

> `myth` [source_located] History.com: the 'in the black' explanation is 'officially sanctioned—but inaccurate'; 'Sometime in the late 1980s, however, retailers found a way to reinvent Black Friday' as the day stores went from red to black. (sources: hist)

## a2_door - The doorbuster trick

Start with the doorbuster. It's a small batch of products priced so low that the store may earn little or nothing on them. Sometimes less than nothing. Economists call it a loss leader. Its job isn't profit. Its job is to get you through the door.

## a2_plan - The doorbuster trick

Then comes the most profitable question in retail. Would you like the protection plan? Economists Jose Miguel Abito and Yuval Salant studied extended warranties, which averaged about twenty-four percent of a product's price. Using failure rates for televisions, they estimated profit margins as high as sixty-two to seventy-three percent.

> `warranty` [source_located] Kellogg Insight (2019) on Abito & Salant (Review of Economic Studies, 2018): extended warranties averaged 24 percent of a product's price across electronics; using TV failure rates (5-8%), 'profit margins can be as high as 62 to 73 percent'. (sources: kellogg)

## a2_deriv - The doorbuster trick

Some deals are built for the day itself. Consumer Reports has described low-priced TVs made specifically for Black Friday, often sold by a single retailer. The industry calls them derivatives. They're similar to regular models, but with different model numbers and often fewer features, like fewer HDMI inputs.

> `cr` [source_located] Consumer Reports (2018): 'low-priced TVs made specifically for Black Friday and often available through a single retailer'; 'derivatives' are similar to regular models 'but with different model numbers and often fewer features', e.g. fewer HDMI inputs; a 2017 55-inch derivative CR tested 'was about $170 cheaper than the TV it was based on' and 'performed equally well'; single-retailer sets can't be price-matched. (sources: cr)  

## a2_deriv2 - The doorbuster trick

To be fair, one derivative that Consumer Reports tested cost about one hundred and seventy dollars less than the model it was based on, and performed just as well. The catch is comparison. If only one store sells that model number, you can't easily check the price anywhere else.

> `cr` [source_located] Consumer Reports (2018): 'low-priced TVs made specifically for Black Friday and often available through a single retailer'; 'derivatives' are similar to regular models 'but with different model numbers and often fewer features', e.g. fewer HDMI inputs; a 2017 55-inch derivative CR tested 'was about $170 cheaper than the TV it was based on' and 'performed equally well'; single-retailer sets can't be price-matched. ## a2_vendor - The doorbuster trick

And the store doesn't always pay for the discount. Best Buy's annual report describes agreements with vendors for reimbursement of the cost of markdowns. Target lists markdown allowances among the money it collects from suppliers. Part of your price cut is quietly paid by the brand.

> `vendor` [source_located] Best Buy 10-K: inventory management includes 'agreements with vendors relating to reimbursement for the cost of markdowns or sales incentives'; vendor allowances for purchases, sales volumes, advertising and placement reduce cost of sales. Target 10-K: vendor income 'principally earned as a result of volume rebates, promotions, certain advertising activities, and markdown allowances'. (sources: bby10k, tgt10k)  

## a2_clear - The doorbuster trick

Black Friday also empties the warehouse. Stock that sits loses value. Moving it before the new year turns boxes back into cash.

## a3_quarter - The quarter that pays for the year

Now zoom out to the calendar. Best Buy's fiscal fourth quarter, which covers the holidays, is thirteen weeks out of fifty-two. In fiscal twenty twenty-six, it brought in thirteen point eight billion dollars of revenue, out of forty-one point seven billion dollars for the whole year. That's about a third of the money in a quarter of the time, by our calculation.

## a3_crowd - The quarter that pays for the year

So where does all that shopping happen? On Black Friday itself, about eighty million people shopped in stores, and about eighty-six million shopped online.

## a4_card - Everyone takes a cut




## a4_cards - Everyone takes a cut

Every swipe pays a toll. When you pay with a debit card, the store pays an interchange fee to the bank that issued it. The Federal Reserve counted thirty-four billion dollars of debit and prepaid interchange fees in twenty twenty-three. For big banks, the fee is capped, and averaged about twenty-two to twenty-four cents per purchase. For smaller, exempt banks, about fifty-two cents.

> `fed` [source_located] Federal Reserve (Dec 2025), 2023 data: interchange fees on all debit and general-use prepaid transactions totaled $34.12 billion; 100.7 billion transactions worth $4.7 trillion; average interchange fee for covered (Reg II-capped) transactions $0.24 single-message / $0.22 dual-message; exempt transactions $0.52; network fees $12.95 billion. (sources: fed)  

## a4_net - Everyone takes a cut

On top of that, the card networks collected almost thirteen billion dollars in network fees on those debit and prepaid cards. And credit cards aren't covered by that cap at all. On a record weekend, every lane on the payment highway has a toll booth.

> `fed` [source_located] Federal Reserve (Dec 2025), 2023 data: interchange fees on all debit and general-use prepaid transactions totaled $34.12 billion; 100.7 billion transactions worth $4.7 trillion; average interchange fee for covered (Reg II-capped) transactions $0.24 single-message / $0.22 dual-message; exempt transactions $0.52; network fees $12.95 billion. (sources: fed)  

## a4_bnpl - Everyone takes a cut

Then there's buy now, pay later. Split the price into installments, often with no interest for the shopper. Adobe says it drove twenty billion dollars of online spending last holiday season, and on Cyber Monday alone it crossed one billion dollars.

> `bnpl` [source_located] Adobe (Jan 7, 2026): Buy Now Pay Later contributed $20 billion in online spend in the 2025 season, up 9.8% YoY; Cyber Monday was the biggest BNPL day on record, $1.03bn (up 4.2%). (sources: adobe_jan, adobe_cm)  

## a4_fee - Everyone takes a cut

So who pays for the free ride? Often, the merchant. In a Consumer Financial Protection Bureau study of five lenders, merchant fees averaged about two and a half percent of sales volume in twenty twenty-one. The lenders sell stores on bigger baskets.

> `cfpb` [source_located] CFPB (2022), Table 10, aggregated unit margins of five BNPL lenders: merchant discount fees 3.39% (2019), 2.91% (2020), 2.49% (2021) of GMV; lenders market 'incremental sales' and higher average order value to merchants. (sources: cfpb)

## a5_returns - The hangover

Then comes January. Retailers surveyed by the National Retail Federation and Happy Returns expected seventeen percent of holiday sales to come back. For all of twenty twenty-five, they estimated returns of eight hundred and fifty billion dollars, about sixteen percent of sales. And they believed about nine percent of returns were fraudulent.

> `nrf_ret` [source_located] NRF and Happy Returns (Oct 15, 2025): retailers expect 17% of holiday sales to be returned; 15.8% of annual sales returned in 2025, totaling $849.9 billion; 19.3% of online sales; 9% of all returns fraudulent. Surveys: 358 e-commerce professionals at merchants with >$500M revenue; 2,006 consumers. (sources: nrf_ret)  

## a5_mountain - The hangover

Every return has to be shipped back, checked, repacked or sold off cheap. Online orders come back more often, about nineteen percent of online sales by the same estimate. Part of the holiday profit leaks out the back door.

> `nrf_ret` [source_located] NRF and Happy Returns (Oct 15, 2025): retailers expect 17% of holiday sales to be returned; 15.8% of annual sales returned in 2025, totaling $849.9 billion; 19.3% of online sales; 9% of all returns fraudulent. Surveys: 358 e-commerce professionals at merchants with >$500M revenue; 2,006 consumers. (sources: nrf_ret)  

## a5_real - The hangover

And are the deals even real? In Britain, the consumer group Which tracked two hundred and twenty-seven Black Friday deals from twenty twenty-three. Ninety-two percent of them were the same price or cheaper at other times of the year. That's one study, in one country. But the lesson travels.

> `which` [source_located] Which? (UK, Nov 26, 2024): looked at deals on 227 products in the 2023 Black Friday fortnight (Nov 20 - Dec 1) at eight UK retailers; 92% were the same price or cheaper at other times of the year; around four in 10 were cheaper at other times. (sources: which)
