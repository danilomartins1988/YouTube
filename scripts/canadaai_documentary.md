# Why Canada Could Be the Biggest Winner of the AI Boom

_Maya, a fictional young AI researcher in Montreal, weighs a job offer from California while Whirr, a fictional overheating data center, flees the desert for the north. Following them through hydro dams, cold air, mines and labs, the film tests the belief that Canada's economy is crashing against the assets the AI boom wants (clean power, a natural fridge, critical minerals and research talent) and the obstacles (tariffs and US dependence, startups and talent moving south, slow mines, and power that now has to be built). The answer: Canada has the whole shopping list, but it only wins if it builds._

## co_hook - Two moves, opposite directions

Meet Maya. She trains artificial intelligence in a lab in Montreal, and this morning her phone won't stop buzzing. Headline after headline says the same thing. Canada's economy is crashing. Then a message from California. A big AI lab wants her. Bigger paycheck, bigger computers, more sunshine. Pack your bags, it says. Meanwhile, far to the south, in the desert, somebody else is packing too. This is Whirr. Whirr is a data center, and Whirr is overheating. Its fans are screaming, its power bill keeps climbing, and it needs a new home. Maya is thinking of moving south. Whirr is thinking of moving north. One of them has read the map wrong. So which is it? Is Canada losing the AI boom, or could it be the biggest winner of all?

## c1_crash - Is Canada really crashing?

To be fair, the bad news is real. Back in the spring of twenty twenty-five, American tariffs hit Canadian cars and machinery, exports fell, and the economy actually shrank. Maya's uncle sells car parts. He stopped smiling at the news. The headlines did the math. Crash.

> `gdp_shrank` [source_located] StatCan: real GDP declined 0.4% in Q2 2025 (after +0.5% in Q1), driven by a drop in goods exports as US tariffs slowed trade. (sources: statcan_gdp25)  

## c1_rebound - Is Canada really crashing?

But crashes don't usually bounce. This year the economy grew again, and the Bank of Canada expects more growth ahead, even if new tariffs slow things down this winter. That's a forecast, not a promise. Still, it's not the picture on Maya's phone.

> `boc_rebound` [source_located] Bank of Canada (Oct 28, 2026): Q2 2026 GDP growth 3.3% (annualized), about 2% indicated for Q3; growth expected to slow in Q4 under new US tariffs, then pick up in 2027 (FORECAST); unemployment around 6.5%. (sources: boc_os26)

## c2_card - The power




## c2_dam - The power

Whirr heads north first, following the power lines. They lead out of the mountains and down the rivers, to dams the size of small towns. Of all its electricity, Canada makes about six of every ten kilowatt hours from falling water. Whirr has never seen anything like it.

> `hydro_share` [source_located] CER: about 60% of Canada's electricity is generated from hydro. (sources: cer_can)  

## c2_rank - The power

That makes Canada the third-biggest hydropower maker on the planet. Whirr does a little dance. Clean, steady, and already built.

> `hydro_rank` [source_located] CER: in 2023 Canada was the third-largest generator of hydroelectricity in the world. (sources: cer_can)  

## c2_quebec - The power

In Quebec, the power is famous for being cheap. A big factory there pays less than a quarter of what the same factory would pay in Boston. We told that story in Could Cheap Electricity Become the New Oil? Whirr starts unpacking.

> `hq_boston` [source_located] Same as Ep 67: Hydro-Québec 2025 comparison (rates of Apr 1, 2025, excl. taxes): a 50 MW industrial customer's bill indexed Montréal 100 vs Boston 445 (22%); Montréal ≈ 5.52 CAD cents/kWh ≈ 3.95 US cents (OUR CALC at 1.3973). (sources: hq_cmp25, fed_g5a)  

## c2_twist - The power

Then comes the twist. Hydro-Québec says its days of surplus power are over. Big new users now need the government's permission, and the price it has proposed for data centers is more than double what big industry pays today. It's still a proposal, still under review. Whirr stops unpacking.

> `hq_cd` [data_derived] Same as Ep 67: PROPOSED Rate CD (Hydro-Québec, R-4333-2026): $32.704/kW + 8.710 cents/kWh; at an 85% load factor OUR CALCULATION ≈ 14.0 CAD cents/kWh (≈ 10.0 US cents) vs 5.52 cents today (≈ 2.5x). BLG: 'roughly double'. Not approved; ministerial authorization needed for loads of 5 MW or more. (sources: hq_rcd26, blg_qc26, hq_cmp25, fed_g5a)  

## c2_bc - The power

Next door, in British Columbia, big AI and data center projects no longer simply join the line for power. They now have to compete for a limited slice, like a talent show, judged on what they give back to the province.

> `bc_compete` [source_located] BC: AI and data-centre projects of 10 MW or more must compete for power; first two-year call targets up to 400 MW for AI and data centres (opened Jan 30, 2026). (sources: bc_news26, bc_iaf)  

## c2_lesson - The power

So Whirr learns the first lesson of the north. Canada has the power. But the cheap, easy power is already spoken for. The next power has to be built, and dams and power lines take years.

## c3_card - The cold




## c3_cool - The cold

Still, the north has one gift nobody has to build. Whirr steps outside, and for the first time in ages, its fans slow down. In the desert, a data center spends a fortune on air conditioning. Up here, the cold air does a lot of that work for free. Whirr sighs. Ahhh.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).

## c3_claim - The cold

One Quebec operator says it can cool its servers with outside air most of the year. That's its own claim, and it depends on how a building is designed. But the idea is simple. The cold is a giant, natural fridge.

> `free_cool` [source_located] QScale (company claim, 2022): cooling at its Lévis site is free up to 80% of the year thanks to Quebec's colder climate. (sources: qscale22)  

## c3_heat - The cold

And the heat that comes out of the servers doesn't have to be wasted. In Kamloops, British Columbia, the phone company Bell plans an AI data center at a university that will pipe its leftover heat into the campus buildings. The servers keep the students warm.

## c3_money - The cold

The giants have noticed. Microsoft has announced plans worth almost fourteen billion US dollars in Canada over a few years, mostly for cloud and AI data centers. That's a company plan, not money already spent. But it's a big bet on the north.

> `msft_19b` [data_derived] Microsoft: C$19bn in Canada 2023-2027 (C$7.5bn+ in the next two years) ≈ US$13.6bn (OUR CALC at 1.3973). Company PLAN. (sources: msft_ca25, fed_g5a)  

## c3_draw - The cold

Let me draw why the cold is worth money. Here's a server. Power goes in, and almost all of it comes out as heat. In a hot place, you pay twice: once to run the chip, and again to pump the heat away. In a cold place, the outside air carries much of the heat off almost for free. And if you're lucky, a neighbor wants that heat. Same chip, smaller bill.

## c4_card - The metals




## c4_dad - The metals

Meanwhile, Maya goes home for the weekend, to Sudbury, Ontario, where her dad, Luc, works at a nickel mine. He has heard about California. You know what's inside those AI computers? he asks. Rocks. Our rocks.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Story beat (fictional family; Sudbury is a real nickel-mining region).  

## c4_list - The metals

He's not wrong. Copper for the wires. Nickel, lithium and graphite for the batteries. Uranium for the nuclear plants that could power the data centers. Canada's government keeps an official list of minerals the modern economy can't do without. There are thirty-four on it, and Canada mines many of them.

> `crit34` [source_located] NRCan: Canada's critical minerals list has 34 minerals (June 2024 update), including nickel, copper, uranium, lithium and graphite. (sources: nrcan_cml24)  

## c4_uranium - The metals

In uranium, Canada is the world's second-biggest producer. Luc grins. Rocks, he says again.

> `uranium2` [source_located] CER: Canada is the world's second-largest uranium producer (about 15% of world output in 2022). (sources: cer_can)  

## c4_slow - The metals

But here's the catch with rocks. They're slow. Around the world, a big new mine has taken more than sixteen years, on average, to go from discovery to the first truckload of ore. By then, Maya's AI models would be ancient history.

> `mine16` [source_located] IEA (2021): major mines that came online 2010-2019 took on average over 16 years from discovery to first production (global sample). (sources: iea_crit21)  

## c4_fix - The metals

Ottawa has set up a Major Projects Office to speed up approvals. One copper and zinc mine in Saskatchewan was referred to it and started producing less than a year later, although it was already mostly built. Fast for a mine. Whether that becomes normal is the real test.

## c5_lab - The brains

Back in Montreal, Maya walks into her lab, and you can see why California wants her. Some of the big ideas behind today's AI grew up right here, in Canadian universities, when most of the world had given up on them.

In Toronto, Geoffrey Hinton spent decades on artificial neural networks, when much of the field thought they were a dead end. In twenty twenty-four, that work helped earn him the Nobel Prize in Physics.

## c5_bengio - The brains

In Montreal, Yoshua Bengio founded the lab that grew into Mila, which describes itself as the world's largest academic research center for deep learning. Maya's lab is just down the street.

Canada was also the first country in the world to launch a national AI strategy. And now it's paying to build a giant AI supercomputer of its own, owned in Canada.

> `first_strategy` [source_located] CIFAR: the Pan-Canadian AI Strategy, launched 2017, was the first national AI strategy in the world. (sources: cifar)

Promising Canadian startups often find their biggest buyers, and their biggest checks, south of the border. Maya's phone buzzes. The recruiter again. Decided yet? Not yet.

## c6_card - The catch




## c6_tariffs - The catch

Which brings us to the biggest catch of all. Canada's best customer is also its biggest risk. Of every dollar of goods Canada sells abroad, nearly three of every four dollars go to the United States. When Washington adds tariffs, Canada feels it first.

> `us_exports` [source_located] StatCan: share of Canada's merchandise exports going to the US was 75.9% in 2024 and 71.7% in 2025. (sources: statcan_trade26)  

## c6_cables - The catch

AI could change that list. Electricity turned into computing doesn't ride on trucks. It travels through cables. That could be a new kind of export, but only if Canada builds the data centers, and only if someone at home owns the companies that use them.

## c7_answer - Maya's decision

So, could Canada be the biggest winner of the AI boom? It has something rare: the whole shopping list, in one country. Power, cold, metals and brains. Most countries would trade a lot for that. But having the list isn't the same as winning. Canada wins only if it builds: power lines, mines, data centers, and companies that grow up at home instead of moving away.
