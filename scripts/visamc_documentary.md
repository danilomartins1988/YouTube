## m1_launch - The bank club

The story starts in California, in nineteen fifty-eight. Bank of America launches a new kind of card, called BankAmericard. The bank does everything itself. It signs up the shoppers, it signs up the stores, and it carries the risk. It is one bank, running one closed system.

## m1_license - The bank club

But one bank can only reach so far. So in nineteen sixty-six, according to the Federal Reserve's own history, Bank of America starts licensing the card to other banks, in the United States and abroad. That same year, a group of rival banks forms its own answer, a cooperative called Interbank. In nineteen sixty-nine, it takes the name Master Charge. That is the ancestor of Mastercard.

## m2_why - The club goes public

Why sell? Part of the reason was the courts. Stores had been suing the card networks for years, saying the banks inside the club were setting fees together. As public companies, Visa and Mastercard would be businesses in their own right, not clubs of competing banks. Merchant lawyers even asked courts to undo the listings.

## m3_platform - The moat

In twenty eighteen, the Supreme Court described what a card network is. It called it a two-sided platform, serving two groups at once, card holders and merchants. And it said these platforms have what economists call indirect network effects. The network is worth more to stores when more shoppers carry its cards, and worth more to shoppers when more stores accept them.

## m4_durbin - The rules

Then Congress went after debit cards. A law passed in twenty ten, known as the Durbin Amendment, told the Federal Reserve to limit debit fees for large banks. Today the cap is twenty-one cents, plus five hundredths of a percent of the purchase, plus one cent for fraud prevention. The law also requires every debit card to work on at least two unrelated networks.

> `durbin` [source_located] Fed: an issuer 'may not receive, for any electronic debit transaction, an interchange fee that exceeds $0.21 plus 0.05 percent multiplied by the value of the transaction, plus a $0.01 fraud-prevention adjustment' (section 920, Dodd-Frank, 2010). Fed proposal Oct 25, 2023: for an average $50 transaction the cap 'would decline from 24.5 cents ... to 17.7 cents.' Visa 10-K (Nov 2026) still describes the Fed as 'proposing further rate reductions'; Mastercard 10-K: two district courts issued 'conflicting decisions' in Aug/Sep 2025; 'The interchange cap remains in effect while the litigation continues.' DOJ: Durbin 'requires that each debit card support at least two unaffiliated debit networks.' (sources: regii, visak, mak, doj)

## m4_eu - The rules

Europe went further. Since twenty fifteen, European Union law has capped the fee banks earn on consumer cards at zero point two percent for debit and zero point three percent for credit.

> `ccca` [source_located] Durbin press release (Jan 13, 2026): the Credit Card Competition Act would require banks with 'more than $100 billion in assets' to support 'at least two unaffiliated card networks (including one outside Visa/Mastercard)'; 'President Trump endorsed the legislation early this morning.' ## payoff - The lesson

So how did Visa and Mastercard become gatekeepers? Not by lending money, and not by owning stores. They became the meeting point. First as clubs that let thousands of banks act like one. Then as companies sitting on a loop that gets stronger every time someone opens a card or a store accepts one. That is the real business. Not the plastic, but the place where everyone has to meet.

> `origins` [source_located] Fed History: 'The origins of the Visa network, for example, trace to 1958 when Bank of America launched BankAmericard in California.' 'The MasterCard network began in 1966 under the name Interbank, a cooperative formed by several banks in New York.' 'BankAmericard was spun off into its own company in 1970 and rebranded as Visa in 1976.' MasterCard S-4 (2001): 'In 1969, ICA acquired exclusive rights to the "MasterCharge" name.'
