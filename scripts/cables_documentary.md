# The Cables at the Bottom of the Ocean That Carry the Internet

_Almost all the internet traffic that crosses borders travels through cables on the ocean floor: how they work, who owns them, who builds and repairs them, what happens when one breaks, and why governments fight over them._

## h_question - The cables

What if the internet you use every day wasn't in the sky, but at the bottom of the ocean?

## h_stakes - The cables

It is. According to the United Nations' telecom agency, more than 99 percent of international data traffic travels through about 500 cables lying on the seabed, stretching more than 1.7 million kilometers. Every video call, every bank transfer, every message to another country.

> `itu_99` [source_located] ITU (Feb 2025): more than 99 per cent of international data traffic is carried by a network of about 500 submarine telecommunications cables spanning over 1.7 million kilometres worldwide. (sources: itu_2025)

## m1_question - How the internet crosses the ocean

So how does the internet actually cross an ocean? Most people think it goes through satellites. It mostly doesn't.

## m1_satellite - How the internet crosses the ocean

Satellites carry only a very small share of international traffic. In the United States, government statistics put satellites at just 0.37 percent of the country's international capacity. The cable research firm TeleGeography has checked the famous ninety nine percent claim for traffic between continents, and says it holds, even if nobody can measure it exactly.

> `satellite` [source_located] TeleGeography: satellites carry a very small percentage of international traffic; FCC statistics indicate satellites account for just 0.37% of all U.S. international capacity. (sources: tg_faq)  
> `tg_check` [source_located] TeleGeography (May 2023) confirms that submarine cables carry over 99% of intercontinental data traffic, but says precise calculations are impossible without satellite data. (sources: tg_myth_2023)  

## m1_draw - How the internet crosses the ocean

Let me draw how it works. Your data leaves a data center and reaches the coast, at a landing station. From there it enters a cable on the seabed. Inside, the data travels as pulses of light through glass fibers, and comes out at another landing station, on another continent. Then it goes on to its destination.

## m1_light - How the internet crosses the ocean

Why light? Because light in glass can carry an enormous amount of data over very long distances. Each fiber is roughly as thin as a human hair. Switch the light on and off extremely fast, and every flash becomes part of a message. Put many fibers in one cable, and one cable can carry the traffic of whole countries.

> `cable_size` [source_located] TeleGeography: for most of its journey a cable is typically as wide as a garden hose; the fibres are roughly the diameter of a human hair, wrapped in layers of plastic (and sometimes steel wire); cables near shore get extra armouring. (sources: tg_faq)  
> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Fibre thickness from TeleGeography (tg_faq); the rest is general explanation, no figures.  

## m1_discovery - How the internet crosses the ocean

For most of its journey across the ocean, a cable is about as wide as a garden hose. The thin glass fibers are wrapped in layers of plastic, and sometimes steel wire. Near the shore, where fishing boats and anchors are, the cable gets extra armor. And it is built to last at least 25 years.

> `cable_size` [source_located] TeleGeography: for most of its journey a cable is typically as wide as a garden hose; the fibres are roughly the diameter of a human hair, wrapped in layers of plastic (and sometimes steel wire); cables near shore get extra armouring. (sources: tg_faq)  
> `design_life` [source_located] TeleGeography: cables are engineered with a minimum design life of 25 years, but are often retired earlier because they become economically obsolete. (sources: tg_faq)

## m2_draw - Who owns the cables

Let me draw the old way. For decades, cables were owned by telephone companies. Several of them would form a club, called a consortium. Each member paid for a share of the cable, and got a share of its capacity. Then, in the late nineteen nineties, new companies started building private cables, and selling the capacity to anyone who wanted it.

> `owners_model` [source_located] TeleGeography: cables were traditionally owned by telecom carriers forming a consortium; in the late 1990s entrepreneurial companies built private cables; both models exist today, and content providers such as Google, Meta, Microsoft and Amazon are major investors in new cable. (sources: tg_faq, tg_bandwidth_2025)  

## m2_shift - Who owns the cables

Then the users changed. In 2016, internet backbone companies, the carriers that move traffic between networks, still used most of the international bandwidth. By twenty twenty-four, content and cloud companies used almost three quarters of it. That means a handful of names: Google, Meta, Microsoft and Amazon.

> `content_share` [source_located] TeleGeography (May 2025): in 2024 content and cloud networks accounted for almost three-quarters of all international bandwidth demand; as recently as 2016 internet backbone providers accounted for the majority. (sources: tg_bandwidth_2025)  
> `owners_model` [source_located] TeleGeography: cables were traditionally owned by telecom carriers forming a consortium; in the late 1990s entrepreneurial companies built private cables; both models exist today, and content providers such as Google, Meta, Microsoft and Amazon are major investors in new cable. (sources: tg_faq, tg_bandwidth_2025)  

## m2_demand - Who owns the cables

And demand keeps exploding. Between twenty twenty and twenty twenty-four, international bandwidth demand tripled, to more than 6.4 petabits per second. Streaming, cloud computing and now artificial intelligence all need more capacity than ever.

> `demand_2024` [source_located] TeleGeography (May 2025): international bandwidth demand tripled between 2020 and 2024, surpassing 6.4 Pbps. (sources: tg_bandwidth_2025)  

## m2_africa - Who owns the cables

So the tech giants stopped renting and started building. One example: a cable called Two Africa. Announced by Meta in twenty twenty with African and global telecom partners, it was planned at 37,000 kilometers, almost the circumference of the Earth, to connect 23 countries in Africa, the Middle East and Europe.

> `africa2` [source_located] Meta (May 2020): 2Africa, planned at 37,000 kilometres, nearly equal to the circumference of the Earth, to interconnect 23 countries in Africa, the Middle East and Europe, built with African and global operators. (sources: meta_2africa)  

## m2_discovery - Who owns the cables

The companies that fill the cables with data are now the ones paying to lay them. Owning the cable means they don't have to wait, or pay someone else, for capacity. The internet's underwater roads are becoming private highways.

## m3_layers - Who builds and fixes them

Let's open one up. At the center, the glass fibers that carry the light. Around them, layers of plastic that protect them. Sometimes, steel wire for strength. And near the coast, extra armor against anchors and fishing gear. Simple in theory. Very hard to make in one piece across an entire ocean, so that it survives for decades under the sea.

> `cable_size` [source_located] TeleGeography: for most of its journey a cable is typically as wide as a garden hose; the fibres are roughly the diameter of a human hair, wrapped in layers of plastic (and sometimes steel wire); cables near shore get extra armouring. (sources: tg_faq)  
> `design_life` [source_located] TeleGeography: cables are engineered with a minimum design life of 25 years, but are often retired earlier because they become economically obsolete. (sources: tg_faq)  

## m3_builders - Who builds and fixes them

For decades, three companies have dominated building and laying these cables. SubCom from the United States, NEC from Japan, and Alcatel Submarine Networks from France. And one newcomer is growing faster than all of them: HMN Tech, from China.

> `big_three` [source_located] Reuters (Mar 2023): three companies have dominated the construction and laying of subsea cables for decades: America's SubCom, Japan's NEC and France's Alcatel Submarine Networks; China's HMN Tech is the fastest-growing, according to TeleGeography data. (sources: reuters_smw6, lightreading_smw6)  

## m3_repair - Who builds and fixes them

Then there are the repairs. When a cable breaks, a specialized cable repair ship has to sail to the spot, find the damaged section on the seabed, bring it up and repair it at sea. These ships are stationed around the world, ready to go. When two cables were damaged in the Baltic Sea in November twenty twenty-four, both were repaired within 2 weeks.

> `repair_ships` [source_located] ICPC: faults are repaired by cable repair ships that are strategically located around the world; in the November 2024 Baltic incidents two cables were damaged and both were repaired within 2 weeks. (sources: icpc_media)  
> General description of a repair; the Baltic example and strategically located ships are from the ICPC.

## m4_question - When a cable breaks

So what happens when a cable breaks? One cut can slow down, or switch off, the internet of a whole region.

## m4_faults - When a cable breaks

According to the International Cable Protection Committee, there are 150 to 200 cable faults around the world every year. And 70 to 80 percent of them are accidents caused by people, mostly fishing gear and ships' anchors. Not sabotage. Just everyday activity at sea.

> `faults` [source_located] ICPC (May 2026): approximately 150-200 submarine cable faults occur globally each year; around 70-80% are caused by accidental human activity such as commercial fishing and ships' anchors, rather than sabotage. (sources: icpc_media)  

## m4_redsea - When a cable breaks

In February twenty twenty-four, it happened in the Red Sea. A cargo ship called the Rubymar was hit by a missile attack and later sank. American officials said its dragging anchor most likely cut three cables: Seacom, A A E one, and E I G. One internet provider estimated these cables carried about 25 percent of the traffic in the region.

> `redsea` [source_located] Bloomberg (Mar 2024): three of more than a dozen cables running through the Red Sea (Seacom, AAE-1 and EIG) were knocked offline in late February 2024; US NSC spokesman John Kirby said most likely by an anchor dragging from the Rubymar as it sank; HGC estimated these cables carry about 25% of traffic in the region. (sources: redsea_2024)  

## m4_backup - When a cable breaks

Let me draw why that wasn't a disaster. The Red Sea has more than a dozen cables. When three go down, traffic is sent around the break, through the others. It gets slower and more expensive, but it keeps flowing. But what if a country has only one cable?

> `redsea` [source_located] Bloomberg (Mar 2024): three of more than a dozen cables running through the Red Sea (Seacom, AAE-1 and EIG) were knocked offline in late February 2024; US NSC spokesman John Kirby said most likely by an anchor dragging from the Rubymar as it sank; HGC estimated these cables carry about 25% of traffic in the region. (sources: redsea_2024)  
> More than a dozen cables in the Red Sea is from Bloomberg (redsea_2024); rerouting is a general explanation.  

## m4_tonga - When a cable breaks

That is what happened to Tonga. On January fifteenth, twenty twenty-two, a huge volcano erupted near its islands. The eruption tore 80 kilometers of its cable into pieces. Tonga's main link to the rest of the world was gone. It took more than five weeks to bring it back, and about 90 kilometers of new cable.

> `tonga` [source_located] The January 15, 2022 eruption near Tonga mangled an 80-kilometre stretch of its undersea cable; the main internet connection was restored on February 22, more than five weeks later; about 90 km of cable was replaced. (sources: tonga_techxplore, tonga_aljazeera)  

## m4_discovery - When a cable breaks

The internet is only as strong as its number of routes. Where there are many cables, a cut is an inconvenience. Where there is one, it is a blackout.

## m5_smw6 - The fight over the seabed

Take a cable called SEA-ME-WE six, from Southeast Asia, through the Middle East, to Western Europe. In early twenty twenty, the Chinese company HMN Tech was chosen to build it. Then the United States government pressed the members to drop it. In the end, the American company SubCom won the contract, with a bid that was about a third more expensive, according to Light Reading. Two Chinese carriers, China Telecom and China Mobile, left the project.

> `smw6` [source_located] Reuters/Light Reading (Mar 2023): HMN Tech (predecessor majority-owned by Huawei) was selected in early 2020 to build SEA-ME-WE 6; after US pressure SubCom won the contract (announced February 2022), with a bid about a third higher than HMN's; China Telecom and China Mobile pulled out, China Unicom stayed. (sources: reuters_smw6, lightreading_smw6)  

## m5_plcn - The fight over the seabed

It also happened across the Pacific. The Pacific Light Cable Network, owned by Google and Meta, was meant to link the United States with Hong Kong. After an American security review recommended rejecting that leg, it was dropped. The cable now carries data from the United States only to Taiwan and the Philippines.

> `plcn` [source_located] Reuters (Mar 2023): after Team Telecom recommended that the FCC reject the Hong Kong leg, the Pacific Light Cable Network, owned by Google and Meta, will only transmit data from the United States to Taiwan and the Philippines. (sources: reuters_smw6)
