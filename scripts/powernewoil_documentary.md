# Could Cheap Electricity Become the New Oil?

_Nia, a fictional site scout, is hired to find a home for a giant AI campus, with one rule from her boss, look for the plug, not the workers. Following her from pin to pin, from an old aluminum smelter town to Norway, Iceland, Quebec, Texas, the Gulf and Brazil, the film asks whether cheap electricity is becoming the new oil, and finds that it is, with one big difference, power can't be shipped, so the winners are the places that can plug you in fast and keep the plug on._

## co_hook - Follow the plug

Meet Nia. She's a site scout, and her new job sounds simple. An AI company wants to build a giant data center campus, and Nia has to find it a home. Her boss gives her one rule. Don't look for workers. Look for the plug. This place will drink as much power as a whole city, every hour of every day. A century ago, companies went hunting for oil. Now Nia is hunting for cheap electricity. So could cheap electricity become the new oil?

## c2_smelt - The metal made of electricity

And this has happened before. Meet aluminum, the metal in your soda can. You can't just dig it up. You have to tear it out of its ore with a massive electric current. Making a single ton takes about as much electricity as an American home uses in more than a year. That's why people call aluminum solid electricity.

> `al_home` [data_derived] OUR CALCULATION: about 14,000 kWh to smelt one tonne of aluminium (IAI world average ~14,114 kWh/t, 2021) vs 10,791 kWh bought by an average US home in 2022 (EIA) = about 1.3 years of a home's electricity per tonne. (sources: iai_int, eia_res)  
> IAI world average ~14,114 kWh/t (2021) vs EIA 10,791 kWh per home (2022); comparison is OUR CALCULATION.  

## c2_iceland - The metal made of electricity

So the smelters went hunting for cheap power, exactly like Nia. In the nineteen sixties, Iceland built its first giant dam mainly to power its first aluminum smelter. Electricity in, metal out, ships to the world. Iceland's national power company still sells most of its power to heavy industry.

> `iceland60s` [source_located] Landsvirkjun, founded 1965, built the Búrfell hydro station (on line 1969) mainly to supply the ISAL aluminium smelter at Straumsvík. (sources: lv_burfell)  
> `lv_share` [source_located] Landsvirkjun sells approximately 85% of its generated energy to power-intensive industries. (sources: lv_cust)  
> Landsvirkjun (founded 1965; Búrfell on line 1969, built mainly for the ISAL smelter); ~85% of its energy to power-intensive industry.  

## c2_quebec - The metal made of electricity

## c3_norway - Pin by pin

First pin, northern Norway. Nearly all of Norway's electricity comes from falling water, and the far north has more of it than it can use. In the port town of Narvik, a new AI campus was announced as Stargate Norway, picked for hydropower and cool air. Since then, the tenant changed, and Microsoft became the big customer. The plug stayed the same. Norway's oil fortune is the story we told in Why Norway Is So Rich. This is its other energy.

## c3_iceland - Pin by pin

Second pin, Iceland. Power from rivers and from the heat under the ground, and cold air that cools servers almost for free. Data centers came here years ago. But Nia hears a warning. When the reservoirs ran low, Iceland's big power company had to sell less power. Cheap doesn't mean endless.

## c3_quebec2 - Pin by pin

Then the catch. Hydro-Québec now says its days of surplus power are over. Big new users need the government's permission to plug in. And it has proposed a new rate just for data centers, more than double what big industry pays today. The cheapest plug in North America is starting to act like an oil producer. It wants a bigger share of the prize.

BLG: about 13 cents, 'roughly double'; Le Soleil: would double. Not approved. (sources: hq_rcd26, blg_qc26, lesoleil26, hq_cmp25, fed_g5a)

## c3_brazil - Pin by pin

Sixth pin, Brazil. Its electricity is mostly renewable, from rivers, wind and sun, and TikTok has confirmed a data center in Ceará, fed by new wind farms. But Nia finds a catch on the bill. ## c4_queue - The catch

And the wait can be the biggest cost of all. The IEA found that in parts of Europe, a data center can wait as long as a decade for a grid connection. Building the data center itself is much faster than that. Cubby can be ready long before the plug is.

> `eu_wait` [source_located] IEA: in Europe grid-connection waits for data centres range from two to ten years by country; seven to ten years on average in the main hubs. (sources: iea_eu_dc)  
> IEA commentary on Europe (2-10 years by country; 7-10 in main hubs).  

## c4_ireland - The catch

Ireland shows what happens when the plugs win too fast. Data centers there now use more than a fifth of all the metered electricity in the country, more than all the homes in its cities. Around Dublin, the grid got so tight that new data centers now have to bring their own power.

> `ie_fifth` [source_located] CSO: data centres used 22% of Ireland's metered electricity in 2024, more than urban dwellings (18%). (sources: cso_ie24)  
> CSO (22% vs urban dwellings 18%, 2024); CRU decision Dec 2025 (on-site generation or storage).  

## c4_heat - The catch

Then there's heat and distance. Chips run hot, and cooling them takes water or even more power, which is why the cold north looks so good. But distance matters too. Training a giant AI model can happen far away. Answering your question happens best close to you. So the far north wins one kind of job, and the big cities keep another.

## c4_politics - The catch

And then, politics. When power is cheap, voters notice who gets it. Norway moved to block new crypto mines. Quebec decides who plugs in. Ireland makes newcomers bring their own power. Texas passed a law letting the grid tell giant users to cut back in an emergency. Cheap electricity, it turns out, is a resource governments guard, just like oil.

## c5_draw - Is power the new oil?

Let me draw the big difference. Oil made one world market. A barrel costs roughly the same wherever you buy it, plus shipping. So oil money flowed to whoever owned the wells. Electricity makes lots of little local markets, each one stuck behind its own wires. So the prize doesn't go to whoever has the most energy. It goes to whoever can deliver it fast, in huge blocks, and keep it on.

## c5_verdict - Is power the new oil?

So, could cheap electricity become the new oil? In one way, yes. Just like oil a century ago, it's deciding where the next giant industries land, and countries are already competing for them with dams, solar fields and tax deals. But in another way, no. Oil could be shipped to anyone. Electricity has to be used close to where it's made. So the new oil isn't just cheap power. It's cheap power you can plug into now, and that a country is willing to share.
