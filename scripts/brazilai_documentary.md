# Could Brazil Become the World's Factory for Robots?

_Gus, a fictional factory scout for a fictional American robot company, must find a home for a new plant where robots build robots. Because it barely needs workers, the map changes, and Bia, an engineer from Minas Gerais, shows him why Brazil suddenly fits: clean power, iron, niobium, rare earths, lithium, food and a big market. Then, at every stop, his cartoon cost backpack gets heavier: taxes piled on taxes, paperwork, trucks, expensive loans, the "Custo Brasil". The answer: Brazil has the ingredients few countries have and robots make them matter more, but it will only become a robot factory if the backpack gets lighter; the reform is a start, and it runs to 2033._

## co_hook - The country that could build your robot

Somewhere in America, a robot company is planning a giant new factory, a place where robots build robots. Meet Gus. He's the factory scout, and his boss has given him a single job. Find the best spot on Earth to build it. For decades, that question had an easy answer. Go where workers are cheap. But Gus's new factory barely needs workers. So the answer changes. And a country that never looked like the obvious pick suddenly does. Could the robot in your future be made in Brazil?

## c2_card - The treasure chest




## c2_power - The treasure chest

First stop, a giant dam on a wide river, with wind turbines on the hills behind it. In Brazil, almost nine out of every ten units of electricity come from renewable sources, mostly falling water, plus fast-growing wind and sun. Gus is impressed. A robot factory could run on that day and night. We told Brazil's power story in Could Cheap Electricity Become the New Oil?

> `br_renew` [source_located] EPE Brazilian Energy Balance 2026: renewables were 86.8% of Brazil's electricity matrix in 2025 (88.2% in 2024). (sources: epe_ben26)  

## c2_iron - The treasure chest

Second stop, the red hills of Minas Gerais. Huge trains roll out of the mines, loaded with iron ore, the rock that becomes steel. Brazil is the second-biggest producer of iron ore on Earth. Gus pulls up a robot arm on his phone. And robots are mostly steel.

> `br_iron` [source_located] USGS MCS 2026: Brazil is the world's second-largest iron ore producer (420 Mt usable ore in 2025e, after Australia's 980 Mt). (sources: usgs_fe26)  

## c2_niobium - The treasure chest

Then Bia hands Gus a tiny jar of gray powder. This is niobium. A pinch of it makes steel much stronger and lighter. And Brazil mines more than nine out of every ten tons of it in the world. Gus nearly drops the jar. You have almost all of it? Bia grins. Most of it comes from a single mine in my state.

> `br_nb` [source_located] USGS MCS 2026: Brazil accounts for approximately 93% of global niobium production (104,000 t of 112,000 t, 2025e). (sources: usgs_nb26, cbmm)  

## c2_magnets - The treasure chest

Robots also need motors, and the best motors need magnets made from rare earths. Brazil has the second-largest known reserves of rare earths on the planet, though today it barely mines them, and China still does most of that work. And in the Jequitinhonha Valley, lithium for batteries is already being dug up and shipped abroad.

> `br_ree` [source_located] USGS MCS 2026: Brazil holds the world's second-largest rare-earth reserves (11 Mt REO vs China 44 Mt), but mined only about 2,000 t in 2025 vs China's 270,000 t. (sources: usgs_ree26)

## c3_card - The backpack




## c3_twist - The backpack

Here's the twist. For years, Brazil's factories have been losing ground. Today they make only about one-eighth of everything the country produces. Economists have a long word for it, deindustrialization. Gus looks around. With all this treasure? Bia nods. Making things here costs extra.

> `br_mfg` [data_derived] OUR CALCULATION from IBGE quarterly national accounts: manufacturing was 11.8% of Brazil's GDP in 2025 (R$1.50tn of R$12.74tn), down from 13.1% in 1996 (start of the series); CNI reports 11.3% in 2018 as the lowest since 1947. (sources: ibge_cnt)  

## c3_tax - The backpack

The backpack tour begins at the tax counter. In Brazil, the bill to produce carries extra weight. As a part moves from factory to factory, it picks up taxes, and some taxes land on top of other taxes, like a snowball. Even the power bill carries a heavy load, as we saw in the new oil episode. Thunk. The first brick lands in Gus's backpack.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Mechanism in story terms (owner preference - no tax mechanics). Cascading consumption taxes are what EC 132/2023 targets; the power-bill figure is in Ep 67 and is not repeated. Sources: br_reform.  

## c3_paper - The backpack

Next, the paperwork. Brazil's tax rules are famous for being thick. In the World Bank's last worldwide comparison, companies here spent more hours on tax forms than anywhere else on Earth. Thunk. Another brick. Gus leans a little to the left.

> World Bank Doing Business 2020 (1,501 hours in calendar 2018, the highest globally; series discontinued). No figure spoken. Sources: wb_db20.  

## c3_truck - The backpack

Then, the road. Brazil is enormous, and about two of every three tons of its cargo ride on trucks, over long highways, to busy ports. Over distances this big, trains and ships are usually cheaper, but there are far fewer of them. Thunk. Another brick. Gus starts to sweat.

> `br_road` [source_located] CNT Transport and Logistics Plan 2026 (industry study): road transport carries 65.8% of Brazil's freight. (sources: cnt_plan26)  

## c3_credit - The backpack

And finally, the bank. To build a factory, you borrow. And Brazil's central bank keeps its key interest rate at almost fourteen percent, high on purpose, to keep prices in check. So every loan in the country costs more, for families and for factories. Thunk. Gus's knees wobble.

> `br_selic` [source_located] Banco Central do Brasil: Selic target rate 13.75% a year (held on Nov 4, 2026). (sources: bcb_selic)  

## c3_total - The backpack

Add it all up, and the backpack has a name, the Custo Brasil, the cost of Brazil. A study by a business group, working with Brazil's industry ministry, put the extra cost of producing here, compared with the average rich country, at about three hundred billion dollars a year. That's an estimate, but it's heavy. Gus sits down on his backpack. Or, it sits down on him.

> `br_custo` [data_derived] MBC / MDIC study (2023, FGV update), cited by CNI: producing in Brazil costs companies R$1.7 trillion a year more than the OECD average (about 19.5% of GDP); OUR CONVERSION at 5.5891 BRL/USD = about US$304 billion. An industry estimate. (sources: mbc_custo, fed_g5a)  

## c3_draw - The backpack

Now here's the catch for robots. Let me draw it. Robots take the wage brick out of the backpack. But they don't touch the tax brick, the truck brick or the loan brick. And a robot factory is mostly expensive machines, bought with borrowed money. So in a robot factory, the loan brick gets heavier, not lighter. Robots make Brazil's treasure more valuable, and its backpack more painful.

## c4_card - Lightening the load




## c4_reform - Lightening the load

The good news is, Brazil knows about the backpack. It recently passed the biggest change to its sales taxes in decades. A pile of old taxes is being replaced by a simpler system, built so that taxes stop landing on top of taxes. Bia pulls the first brick out. See? It's already a little lighter.

> EC 132/2023 and LC 214/2025 (dual VAT CBS + IBS, non-cumulative, replacing five consumption taxes). No figure spoken. Sources: br_reform.  

## c4_slow - Lightening the load

The catch is, it's slow. The new system started its test run this year, and the old taxes don't fully disappear until twenty thirty-three. Gus checks his calendar. That's a long time to carry a backpack.

> `br_vat` [source_located] EC 132/2023 and LC 214/2025: the dual VAT (CBS + IBS) is tested from 2026 and the old consumption taxes (ICMS, ISS) are fully extinguished in 2033. (sources: br_reform)  

## c4_ford - Lightening the load

And factories can come back. In Bahia, Ford closed its giant car plant in Camaçari and left. The halls went quiet. Then a Chinese carmaker, BYD, took over the site, and began building cars there in twenty twenty-five. Brazil has also passed a special tax deal for data centers that run on clean power. Investors are watching.

> `br_byd` [source_located] Ford idled its Camacari (Bahia) plant in January 2021; BYD took over the site and began producing cars there on July 1, 2025. (sources: byd_ba)

So Bia and Gus put Brazil on the scale. The good reason. Robots shrink the cost Brazil was never cheapest at, and they raise the value of what it already has, clean power and metals. The catch. The backpack. Taxes, trucks and loans still sit on every robot, and the rivals carry lighter loads.

## c5_card - So, could Brazil?




## c5_verdict - So, could Brazil?

So, could Brazil become the world's factory for robots? The honest answer is, it could, but not with this backpack. Brazil has ingredients few countries have, clean power, rare metals, food and customers, and robots make those ingredients matter more. But ingredients don't build factories. Costs do. If the reform really lightens the load, and roads, rails and loans get cheaper too, Brazil could be among the big winners of the robot age. If not, its metals may keep sailing away, to be built into robots somewhere else.
