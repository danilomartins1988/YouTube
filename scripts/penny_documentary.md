## m1_seign - The coin that costs too much

## m3_states - The rounding

States are writing their own rules. Washington passed a law in twenty twenty-six that lets stores round cash payments the same way, but does not force them to. It took effect in June. ## m5_cash - The nickel problem

There is also a bigger trend behind all of this. According to the St. Louis Fed, cash was used in only about fourteen percent of payments in twenty twenty-four. Every year, fewer people need small coins at all. That makes it easier for governments to stop making them. For the Mint, ending the penny means less zinc and copper to buy, fewer coins to strike, and one less loss on its books every single year. ## m5_draw - The nickel problem

Let me draw the trade-off. On one side, the cost of making coins that lose money. On the other side, the cost of rounding without them. For the penny, the minting loss was far bigger than the rounding cost. For the nickel, by these estimates, rounding would cost more than the minting loss. That is one reason the nickel is a harder call.

## payoff - The lesson

So why did America stop making the penny? Because a coin is not just money. It is a product, made of metal, with a cost. When the cost of the product rises above the value printed on it, every new coin is a loss. The penny crossed that line years ago. The only surprise is how long it took to stop.
