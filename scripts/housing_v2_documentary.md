## a1_ratio - The price tag

Start with the simplest test there is. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies divides the price of a typical existing single-family home by the typical household's income. In the nineteen-nineties, a home cost about three point two years of income. By twenty nineteen, four point one. In twenty twenty-two, an all-time high of five. And in twenty twenty-five, still four point seven.

> `ratio` [source_located] JCHS (Jun 2026): the median existing single-family price 'hovered near five times the median household income, falling slightly from the all-time high of 5.0 in 2022 to 4.7 in 2025. By comparison, this ratio was 4.1 in 2019 and averaged 3.2 throughout the 1990s.' Press release: 'Home prices have increased 54 percent nationwide since 2020'. (sources: jchs, jchs_pr)

## a1_shrink - The price tag

## a2_coaster - The rate rollercoaster

Now the part that really hurts. Almost nobody pays cash, so the real price of a home is the loan. Freddie Mac tracks the average thirty-year fixed mortgage rate every week. In January twenty twenty-one it hit a record low of two point six five percent. Before the end of twenty twenty-two it was above seven. This October it climbed back to seven point four percent, and last week it was still seven point one one percent.

Here's what that does to one house, by our own calculation. Take October's median price, put twenty percent down, and borrow the rest for thirty years. At seven point one one percent, about two thousand two hundred and forty dollars. Same house, same kitchen, same tiny lawn. about nine hundred dollars more, every month, for thirty years.

## a2_hai - The rate rollercoaster

The National Association of Realtors runs the same test every month. Its Housing Affordability Index equals one hundred when a family on the median income has exactly enough to qualify for the median home, with twenty percent down. In October it read one hundred point seven, down from one hundred and seven point three a year earlier. Barely over the line, and only if you have the down payment.

> `hai` [source_located] NAR (Nov 12, 2026): 'The Housing Affordability Index registered at 100.7, down from 107.3 one year ago.' NAR method: an index of 100 means a family earning the median family income has exactly enough income to qualify for a mortgage on a median-priced existing home, assuming a 20% down payment and principal and interest no more than 25% of income. (sources: nar_ehs, nar_hai)  

## a2_harvard - The rate rollercoaster

Harvard, adding taxes and insurance, puts the bar even higher. The income needed for the median-priced home was one hundred and twenty thousand eight hundred dollars at the end of twenty twenty-five, up from sixty-eight thousand seven hundred dollars five years earlier. In twenty twenty, only thirty-one metro areas required more than a hundred thousand dollars a year. By twenty twenty-five, one hundred and sixty-nine did.

## a3_lock - The great lock-in

High rates did something stranger, too. They froze the people who already own. Meet Rex, Maya's dad. Rex refinanced when rates were near the bottom. And he is not alone. According to Harvard, half of all outstanding mortgages carried rates below four percent at the end of twenty twenty-five.

> `lockin` [source_located] JCHS (Jun 2026): 'With half of all outstanding mortgages carrying rates below 4 percent as of late 2025, many homeowners still face a strong financial disincentive to relocate.' 'Residential mobility fell to a record low of 11.2 percent in 2024.' 'In 2025, existing home sales were unchanged from 2024 at 4.1 million, stuck at a 30-year low that is two-thirds of the 6.1 million sales in 2021.' (sources: jchs)  

## a3_cage - The great lock-in

So if Rex sells, he gives up a cheap loan and takes a new one at more than seven percent. His kids have moved out. The house is too big. And he stays anyway. Economists call it the lock-in effect. The golden handcuffs.

> `pmms` [source_located] Freddie Mac PMMS: 30-year fixed average 7.11% for the week of Nov 12, 2026 (7.18% the week before; 6.24% a year earlier). Historical weekly file: series low 2.65% (week of Jan 7, 2021); first week of 2022 3.22%, above 7% by Oct 27, 2022 (7.08%); 2023 peak 7.79% (Oct 26, 2023); 2026 low 5.98% (Feb 26, 2026) and 2026 high 7.40% (Oct 8 and Oct 15, 2026). (sources: pmms)  

## a3_fhfa - The great lock-in

Economists at the Federal Housing Finance Agency measured it. For every percentage point the market rate sits above a homeowner's own rate, the chance they sell falls by about eighteen percent. Between mid twenty twenty-two and the end of twenty twenty-three, they estimated lock-in prevented about one point three million home sales. And by keeping homes off the market, it pushed prices up by five point seven percent, more than higher rates pulled them down.

> `fhfa` [source_located] FHFA Working Paper 24-03 (Mar 2024): 'for every percentage point that market mortgage rates exceed the origination interest rate, the probability of sale is decreased by 18.1%. This mortgage rate lock-in led to a 57% reduction in home sales with fixed-rate mortgages in 2023Q4 and prevented 1.33 million sales between 2022Q2 and 2023Q4. The supply reduction increased home prices by 5.7%, outweighing the direct impact of elevated rates, which decreased prices by 3.3%.' (sources: fhfa)  

## a3_sales - The great lock-in

When nobody moves, nothing opens up for the next buyer. The share of Americans who moved in a year fell to a record low of eleven point two percent in twenty twenty-four. Harvard says existing-home sales were stuck at four point one million in twenty twenty-five, a thirty-year low, two thirds of the pace of twenty twenty-one. And in October, the National Association of Realtors measured an annual pace of just three point seven five million.

## a4_bust - The homes we never built

Then there's the oldest problem of all. Supply. After the housing crash, builders went quiet. By our count of Census data, America completed about fifteen and a half million new homes in the two thousands, but only about nine million in the twenty-tens. In twenty eleven, just five hundred and eighty-five thousand, the lowest year since the records began in nineteen sixty-eight.

> `building` [source_located] Census via FRED COMPUTNSA (monthly, thousands), OUR sums: completions ≈ 15.64 million in 2000–2009 and ≈ 9.15 million in 2010–2019; 585,000 in 2011, the lowest calendar year in the series (which starts in 1968); ≈ 1.49 million in 2025. (sources: completions)  

## a4_shortage - The homes we never built

So how many homes are missing? Honestly, nobody knows exactly, and the estimates disagree. Harvard says various organizations put the shortage between one point two and five point five million homes. The research group Up for Growth estimates about three point eight million. These are estimates built on different assumptions. But nearly all of them point the same way. Not enough.

> `shortage` [source_located] JCHS (Jun 2026): 'various organizations estimate the shortage to be between 1.2 and 5.5 million units.' Up for Growth (Nov 2025): underproduction 'from 3.85 million homes in 2022 to 3.78 million homes in 2023.' NAHB (Jun 2026): 'NAHB estimates the U.S. has a structural housing deficit of 1.2 million units.' (sources: jchs, upforgrowth, nahb)

The arrow between them is the gap. Change the assumptions, and the gap stretches or shrinks. It never disappears.

## a4_cost - The homes we never built

And building got more expensive. Harvard says the price of residential construction inputs jumped forty percent since January twenty twenty. The home builders' own trade group, the NAHB, estimates that regulation makes up twenty-six point four percent of the price of a new single-family home, about about one hundred and thirty-two thousand dollars. That's the builders' estimate. It's also a clue about where to look next.

> `listings` [source_located] JCHS (Jun 2026): 'just 23 percent of listings in March 2026 were at a price affordable to a household earning $75,000 or less, down from 49 percent of listings in March 2019.' (sources: nahb)

## a5_room - The zoning board

Welcome to the most powerful room in American housing. Not a bank. Not Congress. The local zoning board, on a Tuesday night. Zoning decides what may be built on every piece of land. And in much of the country, the answer for homes is simple. One house, on one lot.

## a5_berkeley - The zoning board

How much? Researchers at UC Berkeley mapped the Los Angeles region and found that about seventy-eight percent of its residential land is reserved for single-family homes only. That's one region, not the whole country. But it shows how much land a single rule can cover.

> `zoning` [source_located] UC Berkeley Othering & Belonging Institute (Mar 2022): 'we find that 77.70 percent of residential land in Greater LA is exclusively reserved for single-family housing.' (sources: berkeley)  

## a5_hearing - The zoning board

Want to put four apartments where one house stands? You may need a variance, a hearing, and a room full of worried neighbors. Parking rules, height limits, minimum lot sizes. Each rule may make sense on its own. Together, they make the cheapest kinds of homes the hardest to build.

## a6_basement - The basement generation

So who pays for all of this? The first-time buyer. In the National Association of Realtors' twenty twenty-six survey of home buyers, first-timers made up just twenty-two percent of purchases, about half the norm of forty percent before two thousand eight. And the typical first-time buyer is now forty years old. Forty.

> `firsttime` [source_located] NAR 2026 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers (Nov 5, 2026; transactions July 2025-June 2026): 'The share of first-time home buyers rose slightly to 22% from last year's record low of 21%'; 'still about half the historical norm of 40% seen before 2008'; median first-time buyer age 40, matching the all-time high; repeat buyers 62; first-time median down payment 9%. (sources: nar_profile)  

## payoff - The basement generation
