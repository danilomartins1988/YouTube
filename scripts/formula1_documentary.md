# The Hidden Business Behind Formula 1

_Formula One looks like a sport. Underneath it is a media business that sells attention to broadcasters, cities and brands, and shares the profit with the teams that make the show._

## open_hook - The business

A pack of cars screams past the grandstand. Every surface you can see has been sold: the boards, the cars, the overalls, even the time on the screen. The race on track is only half the story.

## p1_card - A sport becomes a property




## p1_sketch - A sport becomes a property

At its core, Formula One is simple. A handful of teams design their own cars, and race them around the world. For most of its history, that was the whole idea. The business began when someone realised the show itself could be packaged and sold.

## p1_history - A sport becomes a property

For decades, that someone was Bernie Ecclestone, who sold the sport to television as one package. In 2006, the investment firm CVC took majority control. According to CVC, the calendar grew from sixteen to twenty-two races while it was in charge.

> `ecclestone_role` [source_located] Bernie Ecclestone ran Formula 1's commercial business for decades until Liberty Media's takeover in January 2017, when he became Chairman Emeritus. (sources: liberty_close_2017)  
> `cvc_2006` [source_located] In early 2006 funds managed by CVC acquired a majority share of Formula One; CVC says that during its ownership the calendar grew from 16 to 22 races. (sources: cvc_case)  
> The CVC growth figures are the former owner's own account.  

## p1_liberty - A sport becomes a property

In 2016, the American media company Liberty Media agreed to buy Formula One, valuing the business at 8 billion dollars, including its debt. The deal closed in January 2017, and a new era began.

> `liberty_deal` [source_located] On 7 September 2016 Liberty Media agreed to acquire Formula 1 at an enterprise value of $8.0 billion (equity value $4.4 billion); the deal completed on 23 January 2017. (sources: liberty_deal_2016, liberty_close_2017)  

## p2_card - Who owns what




## p2_layers - Who owns what

Today the sport has three layers. The FIA writes and polices the rules. Formula One, owned by Liberty Media, holds the commercial rights and sells the show. And the teams compete, sharing in the money. Liberty's own filings say the FIA must put safety and sporting concerns above Formula One's commercial interests.

> `fia_role` [source_located] Liberty's 10-K states that the FIA, the sport's regulator, 'must place safety and other sporting concerns over Formula 1's commercial interests'. (sources: liberty_10k_2025)  

## p2_motogp - Who owns what

Liberty has kept building. In 2025, it also bought most of MotoGP, the motorbike championship. Two world series, one owner.

> `liberty_motogp` [source_located] In July 2025 Liberty Media completed the acquisition of about 84% of MotoGP. (sources: liberty_fy2025)

## p3_mix - The money machine

Last year, media rights brought in 31 percent of the revenue. Race promotion fees, about 27 percent. Sponsorship, about 22 percent. The rest came from hospitality, freight and licensing.

> `revenue_mix_2025` [source_located] In 2025 media rights were 31.3% of Formula 1's total revenue, race promotion 26.7%, sponsorship 21.7% and other revenue (hospitality, freight, licensing) 20.3%. (sources: liberty_10k_2025, liberty_fy2025)

## p4_card - Media rights




## p4_media - Media rights

Media rights are the biggest single stream. By our calculation, they were worth about 1.2 billion dollars last year. Broadcasters pay to show every session, and Formula One also sells its own streaming service directly to fans.

> `revenue_usd_2025` [source_located] Our calculation: applying Liberty's 2025 shares to total revenue of $3,873m gives about $1.21bn from media rights, $1.03bn from race promotion and $0.84bn from sponsorship. (sources: liberty_10k_2025, liberty_fy2025)  
> `media_share` [source_located] Media rights were the largest single revenue stream, at 31.3% of total Formula 1 revenue in 2025 (32.8% in 2024). (sources: liberty_10k_2025)  

## p4_apple - Media rights

The biggest new deal is in America. Apple is now the sport's exclusive broadcaster in the United States, for five years. The price was never disclosed, but it is reportedly about 140 million dollars a year.

> `apple_deal` [source_located] Apple became the exclusive US broadcast partner of Formula 1 for five years from 2026 (announced 17 Oct 2025); the fee was not disclosed and is reported at about $140 million a year. (sources: apple_f1_2025, cnbc_apple_value)  

## p4_audience - Media rights

Why pay that much? Look at the American audience. On ESPN, the average race drew 554,000 viewers in twenty eighteen. By twenty twenty-five, it was 1.3 million, up 135 percent.

> `espn_audience` [source_located] ESPN's average US audience per Formula 1 race rose from 554,000 in 2018 to 1.3 million in 2025, an increase of 135 percent. (sources: espn_2025)  

## p5_card - Hosting fees




## p5_fees - Hosting fees

The second stream is the hosting fee. Circuits and cities pay Formula One for the right to stage a Grand Prix. Contracts typically run three to seven years, with fees rising by up to 5 percent a year. By our calculation, those fees came to about 1 billion dollars last year.

> `promoter_terms` [source_located] Liberty's 10-K says race promotion contracts typically have an initial term of three to seven years, with annual fee increases tied to inflation or fixed rates of up to 5% per year; some counterparties are governments or their agencies. (sources: liberty_10k_2025)  
> `revenue_usd_2025` [source_located] Our calculation: applying Liberty's 2025 shares to total revenue of $3,873m gives about $1.21bn from media rights, $1.03bn from race promotion and $0.84bn from sponsorship. (sources: liberty_10k_2025, liberty_fy2025)  

## p5_governments - Hosting fees

The fee for each race is almost never published. But we know who often pays: Liberty says some of its partners are governments. In Singapore, the twenty twenty-two race cost about 100 million dollars to stage, and the government covered 60 percent of it.

> `promoter_terms` [source_located] Liberty's 10-K says race promotion contracts typically have an initial term of three to seven years, with annual fee increases tied to inflation or fixed rates of up to 5% per year; some counterparties are governments or their agencies. (sources: liberty_10k_2025)  
> `singapore_2022` [source_located] Singapore's 2022 Grand Prix cost S$135-140 million to organise, of which the government paid 60% (MTI, 2 Aug 2023). At the 2022 average rate of S$1.3787 per US dollar that is about $98-102 million (our conversion). (sources: mti_singapore_2023, fed_h10)  
> Singapore's cost is converted from Singapore dollars at the 2022 Federal Reserve average rate.  

## p5_vegas - Hosting fees

## p6_card - Sponsorship




## p6_sponsors - Sponsorship

Sponsorship is the third stream, and it is growing fast: about 22 percent of revenue last year, up from less than a fifth the year before. The luxury group LVMH signed a ten-year global partnership. The oil giant Aramco joined earlier, with Formula One promising it an audience of 500 million fans.

> `revenue_mix_2025` [source_located] In 2025 media rights were 31.3% of Formula 1's total revenue, race promotion 26.7%, sponsorship 21.7% and other revenue (hospitality, freight, licensing) 20.3%. (sources: liberty_10k_2025, liberty_fy2025)  
> `lvmh_deal` [source_located] LVMH became a 10-year Global Partner of Formula 1 from 2025 (announced 2 Oct 2024); the value was not disclosed. (sources: f1_lvmh_2024)  
> `aramco_deal` [source_located] Aramco became a Formula 1 Global Partner in March 2020; F1 said the deal would connect Aramco to 'an engaged audience of 500 million fans'. (sources: f1_aramco_2020)  

## p6_hospitality - Sponsorship

Then there is hospitality. In the Paddock Club, corporate guests watch from above the pit lane. Hospitality, freight and licensing together brought in another 787 million dollars.

> `revenue_mix_2025` [source_located] In 2025 media rights were 31.3% of Formula 1's total revenue, race promotion 26.7%, sponsorship 21.7% and other revenue (hospitality, freight, licensing) 20.3%. (sources: liberty_10k_2025, liberty_fy2025)  

## p7_card - How teams make money




## p7_payments - How teams make money

So how do the teams get paid? First, a share of Formula One's profits. Last year, Formula One paid the teams 1.4 billion dollars, about 60 percent of its underlying profit before those payments. And that sum has grown every year.

> `team_payments` [source_located] Formula 1 paid the teams $1,400 million in 2025 (plus a one-off $50m Concorde incentive), up from $1,266m in 2024; team payments were 59.7% of pre-team-payment Adjusted OIBDA. (sources: liberty_fy2025)  
> `team_payments_series` [source_located] Team payments by Formula 1 ($ million, Liberty Media): 2022 1,157; 2023 1,215; 2024 1,266; 2025 1,400. (sources: liberty_fy2025, liberty_fy2023)  

## p7_own - How teams make money

Second, their own sponsors: the logos on the car, the overalls and the garage. Mercedes, for example, reported revenue of about 810 million dollars in twenty twenty-four, with more than 60 percent of it from sponsorship and licensing. Its profit was about 150 million dollars.

> `mercedes_2024` [source_located] Mercedes-Benz Grand Prix Ltd reported 2024 revenue of £636m and profit of £120.3m, with sponsorship and licensing over 60% of income. At the 2024 average of US$1.2781 per pound that is about $813m of revenue and $154m of profit (our conversion). (sources: blackbook_mercedes_2024, fed_h10)  
> Converted from pounds sterling at the 2024 Federal Reserve average rate.

## p8_card - The cost cap




## p8_cap - The cost cap

For years, the richest teams could simply outspend the rest. So in twenty twenty-one, the sport brought in a cost cap: 145 million dollars that first year, falling to 135 million dollars. From twenty twenty-six, it rises to 215 million dollars, but it now covers far more costs. Driver salaries and the three best-paid executives are still outside it.

> `cost_cap` [source_located] The F1 cost cap was $145m in 2021, $140m in 2022 and $135m in 2023-2025; from 2026 it is $215m with a much wider scope (now including items previously outside it). Driver salaries, the three highest-paid staff and marketing are excluded. (sources: f1_cost_cap_2026)  

## p8_breach - The cost cap

And the cap has teeth. Red Bull Racing went over its twenty twenty-one limit by 1.6 percent. The penalty: a 7-million-dollar fine, and 10 percent less aerodynamic testing.

> `redbull_breach` [source_located] The FIA found Red Bull Racing over the 2021 cap by 1.6% ('Minor Overspend Breach'); under an Accepted Breach Agreement it paid a $7 million fine and lost 10% of its aerodynamic testing allowance. (sources: fia_rbr_2022, autosport_rbr_2022)  

## p8_values - The cost cap

With costs capped and income shared, teams have started to look like profitable franchises. Forbes estimates that the average team was worth 3.6 billion dollars last year, with Ferrari on top at 6.5 billion dollars.

> `forbes_values` [source_located] Forbes estimated the average F1 team was worth $3.6 billion in 2025 (up 89% since 2023), with Ferrari the most valuable at $6.5 billion and the lowest at $1.5 billion. (sources: forbes_teams_2025)

> "Look like profitable franchises" is our reading of the valuations.  

## p8_entry - The cost cap

That is why a seat at the table is so expensive. When Cadillac was approved as an eleventh team, it reportedly agreed to pay 450 million dollars to the others, more than double the 200 million dollars in the old rules, to make up for sharing the prize money eleven ways.

> `cadillac_entry` [source_located] F1 and the FIA approved Cadillac as an 11th team for 2026 (7 Mar 2025); new teams pay an anti-dilution fee to existing teams, $200m under the 2021 Concorde terms and reportedly $450m for Cadillac. (sources: f1_cadillac_2025, theRace_antidilution)  

## p9_card - Why brands invest




## p9_fans - Why brands invest

Why do brands pay so much? Reach, and a changing audience. Formula One says it had 827 million fans last year, up 63 percent since twenty eighteen. And 42 percent of them are women.

> `fanbase_2025` [source_located] Formula 1 says its fanbase reached 827 million in 2025, up 12% year on year and 63% since 2018; 42% of fans are female and 43% are under 35; 6.7 million people attended races. (sources: f1_2025_numbers)  

## p9_why - Why brands invest

For a sponsor, that means a global stage almost every other weekend, a wealthy audience, and a technology story to tell. Aramco's deal, for example, also covered sustainable fuels.

## p11_costs - The host-city deal

The bills can be large. The twenty twenty-four Australian Grand Prix cost Victorian taxpayers about 67 million dollars. In Las Vegas, a study paid for by Formula One put the race's economic impact at 934 million dollars, with 45 million dollars in direct taxes.

> `australia_2024` [source_located] The 2024 Australian Grand Prix cost Victorian taxpayers A$102.3 million (AGPC annual report 2023-24, via AAP). At the 2024 average rate of US$0.6597 per Australian dollar that is about $67 million (our conversion). (sources: aap_agp_2024, fed_h10)  
> `vegas_impact_2024` [source_located] An F1-commissioned study by Applied Analysis put the 2024 Las Vegas Grand Prix's economic impact at $934 million and direct tax revenue at $45 million; economists note such totals are gross spending, not net gains. (sources: lvgp_impact_2024)  
> Australian figure converted from Australian dollars at the 2024 Federal Reserve average rate.  

## p11_doubt - The host-city deal

Economists are sceptical. Independent studies of big sporting events nearly unanimously find that the promised benefits are exaggerated. Victoria's own Auditor-General found that for the twenty oh-five race, the costs exceeded the benefits. And in Las Vegas, local businesses complained of lost trade, until three of the disputes were settled.

> `matheson_view` [source_located] Economists Robert Baade and Victor Matheson report that independent researchers 'nearly unanimously' find that boosters' projections exaggerate the economic impact of mega-events. (sources: matheson_2006)  
> `vago_2005` [source_located] The Victorian Auditor-General estimated that for the 2005 Australian Grand Prix the costs exceeded the benefits; the Grand Prix Corporation disputed parts of the method. (sources: vago_2007)  
> `vegas_settle` [source_located] In August 2025 the Las Vegas Grand Prix said it had resolved disputes with three local businesses (two of which had sued) that said race construction had cost them trade. (sources: vegas_settle_2025)  

## p12_card - Risks and opportunities




## p12_risks - Risks and opportunities

The risks are real. This spring, the Bahrain and Saudi Arabian races were called off because of the conflict in the Middle East. Fewer races means less money: in the first half of this year, Formula One's revenue fell 15 percent. Rights groups accuse some hosts of sportswashing. And sponsors cut back in a recession.

> `gulf_cancel` [source_located] In March 2026 the Bahrain and Saudi Arabian Grands Prix scheduled for April were called off because of the conflict in the Middle East (later Liberty assumed 23 races for 2026). (sources: gulf_cancel_2026, liberty_q2_2026)  
> `h1_2026` [source_located] In the first half of 2026 Formula 1 revenue was $1,381m against $1,629m a year earlier (-15%), with eight races held against eleven; Liberty attributes the fall to fewer races and the timing of the calendar. (sources: liberty_q2_2026)  
> `sportswashing` [source_located] In November 2020 Human Rights Watch criticised F1's Saudi Arabia deal, describing such events as a way to 'sportswash' serious human rights abuses; rights groups have made similar complaints about Bahrain. (sources: hrw_2020)  
> `recession_risk` [source_located] Liberty's 10-K warns that discretionary spending (including sponsorship, hospitality and tickets) 'typically falls during times of economic recession or instability'. (sources: liberty_10k_2025)
