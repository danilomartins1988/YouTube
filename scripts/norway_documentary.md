# Why Norway Is So Rich — The Secret Behind Its Oil Fortune

_Norway struck oil in 1969. What made it rich was not the oil itself, but a set of decisions about who owns it, how it is taxed, where the money is kept and how little of it is spent._

## open_hook - The fortune

Somewhere in the North Sea, a platform pumps oil from beneath the seabed. The money it earns ends up in one of the largest investment funds on Earth.

## p1_card - Before the oil




## p1_economy - Before the oil

Before the oil, Norway lived from the sea and the mountains. Fishing boats worked the coast. By the nineteen-fifties its merchant fleet was described as the third largest in the world. And In 1905, Norsk Hydro began turning waterfalls into fertiliser.

> `merchant_fleet_1956` [source_located] In 1956 the US Naval Institute described Norway's merchant fleet as the third largest in the world, behind the United States and the British Commonwealth. (sources: usni_merchant_1956)  
> `hydro_1905` [source_located] Norsk Hydro was founded on 2 December 1905, using hydropower to produce nitrogen fertiliser. (sources: hydro_history)  
> "Lived from the sea and the mountains" is a summary of fishing, shipping and hydropower-based industry.  

## p1_neighbours - Before the oil

It was a modest, hard-working economy, but not an especially rich one. In 1970, Norway's income per person was only about four-fifths of Sweden's, and behind Denmark's too.

> `maddison_1970` [source_located] Maddison Project 2023, GDP per capita in 2011 international dollars, 1970: Norway 15,983, Sweden 20,269, Denmark 20,221 (Norway about 79% of Sweden). (sources: maddison_owid)  

## p2_card - The discovery




## p2_doubt - The discovery

Few believed there was oil under Norway's seabed at all. In 1958, the national geological survey told the government the chances could be discounted.

> `geology_1958` [source_located] In 1958 the Geological Survey of Norway told the Foreign Ministry that the chance of finding coal, oil or sulphur on the continental shelf could be discounted. (sources: np_history)  

## p2_shelf - The discovery

Still, Norway claimed its continental shelf. In 1965, it agreed a median line through the North Sea with Britain and Denmark, and handed out its first 22 production licences.

> `shelf_1965` [source_located] In March 1965 Norway agreed median-line shelf boundaries with the UK and Denmark, and on 13 April 1965 announced its first licensing round with 22 production licences. (sources: np_history)  

## p2_ekofisk - The discovery

The first wells came up dry. Then, just before Christmas 1969, the American company Phillips Petroleum reported a find called Ekofisk. At the time, it was the largest offshore oil field ever discovered.

> `ekofisk_1969` [source_located] Phillips Petroleum reported the Ekofisk discovery to the Norwegian government just before Christmas 1969 (23 December); it was then the world's largest offshore field. (sources: np_history, ekofisk_museum)  

## p2_production - The discovery

Oil started flowing from Ekofisk in June 1971. Norway had become an oil nation almost overnight.

> `ekofisk_production` [source_located] Production from Ekofisk started on 15 June 1971. (sources: np_history)  

## p3_card - Who owns the oil




## p3_commandments - Who owns the oil

Norway's first big decision was about control. In 1971, parliament adopted ten principles, later called the oil commandments, to keep the resource under national control and the industry serving the country.

> `ten_commandments` [source_located] On 14 June 1971 the Storting adopted the 'ten oil commandments', principles for national control of petroleum activities. (sources: storting_oljebud)  

## p3_statoil - Who owns the oil

In 1972, it created a state oil company, Statoil, and a regulator, the Petroleum Directorate. In 1985, the state also began holding direct stakes in oil fields itself. Today the government still owns 67 percent of Statoil's successor, Equinor.

> `statoil_1972` [source_located] On 14 June 1972 the Storting decided to establish the state oil company Statoil and the Norwegian Petroleum Directorate, both in Stavanger. (sources: sodir_50)  
> `sdfi_1985` [source_located] From 1 January 1985 the state's interests were split between Statoil and the State's Direct Financial Interest (SDFI), managed by Petoro since 2001. (sources: np_history)  
> `equinor_67` [source_located] The Norwegian state owns 67% of the shares in Equinor (formerly Statoil). (sources: np_state_org)  

## p3_tax - Who owns the oil

The second decision was tax. Oil companies pay the normal 22 percent company tax, plus a special petroleum tax of 71.8 percent. Together, the state takes 78 percent of the profit at the margin.

> `petroleum_tax` [source_located] Oil and gas companies pay the ordinary 22% company tax plus a 71.8% special tax, for a combined marginal rate of 78%. (sources: np_revenues, np_tax)  

## p3_streams - Who owns the oil

So the oil pays the state three ways: taxes on company profits, its own share of the fields, and dividends from Equinor.

> `cashflow_series` [source_located] Net government cash flow from petroleum activities, NOK billion (SSB): 2022 1,285; 2023 978; 2024 702; 2025 664. (sources: ssb_cashflow)  
> `equinor_67` [source_located] The Norwegian state owns 67% of the shares in Equinor (formerly Statoil). (sources: np_state_org)  
> Three components of the net cash flow as reported by Statistics Norway (taxes, SDFI, Equinor dividend).  

## p4_card - The money machine




## p4_cashflow - The money machine

Those streams are enormous. In 2022, when energy prices spiked, the state's net cash flow from petroleum hit a record about 134 billion dollars. Even in a calmer year like last year, it was about 64 billion dollars.

> `cashflow_2022` [source_located] In 2022 the state's net cash flow from petroleum activities reached a record NOK 1,285 billion. (sources: ssb_cashflow)  
> `cashflow_series` [source_located] Net government cash flow from petroleum activities, NOK billion (SSB): 2022 1,285; 2023 978; 2024 702; 2025 664. (sources: ssb_cashflow)  
> "Last year" = 2025 (film produced in November 2026).  

## p4_exports - The money machine

Oil and gas made up 57 percent of the value of everything Norway exported in 2025. And through about 8,800 kilometres of pipelines, Norway's gas now covers more than 30 percent of all the gas used in the European Union and Britain.

> `petroleum_exports_share` [source_located] In 2025 crude oil, natural gas and condensate exports were worth about NOK 1,000 billion, 57% of the value of Norway's goods exports. (sources: np_front)  
> `gas_share_europe` [source_located] Norway's gas exports in 2025 equalled more than 30% of the total gas consumption of the EU and the UK, through a pipeline network of about 8,800 km. (sources: np_exports)  

## p4_problem - The money machine

That much money arriving that fast is dangerous. Spend it all at home, and wages and prices surge, the currency soars, and every other industry struggles to compete. Economists call it the resource curse.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Standard economics (Dutch disease / resource curse); no statistic stated.  

## p5_card - The fund




## p5_law - The fund

Norway's answer was to park the money abroad. In 1990, parliament created a petroleum fund. In May 1996, the first transfer, worth about 300 million dollars, went in.

> `fund_law_1990` [source_located] In 1990 the Storting passed a law establishing the Government Petroleum Fund to receive petroleum revenue. (sources: nbim_history)  
> `first_transfer_1996` [source_located] On 30 May 1996 the Ministry of Finance made the first transfer, of almost 2 billion kroner, to the Government Petroleum Fund. (sources: nbim_25yrs)  

## p5_governance - The fund

Parliament sets the rules. The finance ministry owns the fund on the people's behalf. And the central bank's investment arm, created In 1998, manages it. One rule matters most: the fund is not allowed to invest in Norway at all.

> `governance` [source_located] The Storting sets the fund's framework in the Government Pension Fund Act, the Ministry of Finance has overall responsibility and issues guidelines, and Norges Bank manages it. (sources: nbim_about)  
> `nbim_1998` [source_located] Norges Bank Investment Management was established on 1 January 1998, when the fund began investing in equities; it was renamed the Government Pension Fund Global in 2006. (sources: nbim_history)  
> `no_norway_investment` [source_located] The fund cannot invest in securities issued by Norwegian entities, in Norwegian kroner, or in real estate and infrastructure located in Norway. (sources: nbim_ar2025)  

## p5_growth - The fund

Then the money compounded. Here is the value of the fund at the end of each year, in billions of dollars. The line barely moves in the beginning, then it climbs, and climbs, past two thousand billion.

> `fund_value_series` [source_located] Fund market value at year end, NOK billion (NBIM): 1996 47.4; 1999 222.4; 2001 613.7; 2005 1,399.1; 2008 2,275.4; 2010 3,077.4; 2012 3,815.8; 2014 6,430.7; 2016 7,510.5; 2018 8,256.0; 2020 10,913.8; 2022 12,429.1; 2023 15,764.8; 2024 19,755.0; 2025 21,268. (sources: nbim_history, nbim_2025_results)  

## p5_returns - The fund

Here is the surprising part. According to the fund's manager, the oil money paid in accounts for less than half of its value today. The rest has been earned by the investments themselves.

> `deposits_less_than_half` [source_located] According to NBIM, petroleum-revenue deposits account for less than half of the fund's value; the rest is investment return. (sources: nbim_about)  

## p6_card - How the fund invests




## p6_allocation - How the fund invests

Most of the money, about 72 percent, is in shares. Around a quarter is in bonds. A small slice is in property and in renewable energy infrastructure.

> `allocation_q3_2026` [source_located] Fund allocation at 30 September 2026: equities 72.1%, fixed income 25.8%, unlisted real estate 1.6%, unlisted renewable energy infrastructure 0.5%. (sources: nbim_q3_2026)  
> "Around a quarter" = 25.8% fixed income (allocation_q3_2026).  

## p6_world - How the fund invests

And it is spread across the planet. The biggest share, 52.9 percent, is invested in the United States. Its largest single holdings are Nvidia, Apple and Alphabet.

> `us_share` [source_located] The United States was the fund's largest country exposure, 52.9% of the fund at the end of 2025. (sources: nbim_ar2025)  
> `top_holdings` [source_located] The fund's largest holdings at 30 June 2026: NVIDIA (NOK 612bn), Apple (NOK 522bn), Alphabet (NOK 499bn). (sources: nbim_home)  
> `fund_companies` [source_located] At the end of 2025 the fund held shares in 7,201 companies, with investments in 68 countries. (sources: nbim_ar2025)  

## p6_returns - How the fund invests

Since it began buying shares, the fund has returned an average of 6.6 percent a year, or 4.3 percent after inflation and costs. But it is not a smooth ride: it lost minus 23 percent in the financial crisis, and minus 14 percent in 2022.

> `returns_since_1998` [source_located] Between 1998 and 2025 the fund's average annual return was 6.6%; the annual net real return, after inflation and management costs, was 4.3%. (sources: nbim_ar2025)  
> `bad_years` [source_located] The fund returned -23.3% in 2008 (financial crisis) and -14.1% in 2022; it returned 15.1% in 2025. (sources: nbim_history, nbim_ar2025)  
> 2008 = -23.3% (NBIM history); 2022 = -14.1% (annual report 2025).  

## p7_card - The fiscal rule




## p7_rule - The fiscal rule

The real secret is a rule about spending. Since 2001, the government may only take out, over time, what the fund is expected to earn after inflation. That was set at 4 percent, and cut to 3 percent in 2017.

> `fiscal_rule` [source_located] Under the fiscal rule (since 2001), transfers from the fund to the budget shall over time follow the expected real return on the fund, set at 4% in 2001 and reduced to 3% in 2017. (sources: mof_framework)  

## p7_budget - The fiscal rule

In the budget for this year, the government planned to use about 56 billion dollars of oil money, about 2.8 percent of the fund. The IMF estimates that money from the fund now pays for about a quarter of all public spending.

> `budget_2026_deficit` [source_located] The 2026 budget's structural non-oil deficit is NOK 579.4 billion, 2.8% of the fund's value and 13.1% of mainland trend GDP. (sources: mof_budget_2026)  
> `budget_share` [source_located] According to the IMF (2026 Article IV), withdrawals from the fund finance about a fourth of public expenditure; NBIM's own page says almost 20 percent of the government budget. (sources: imf_2026, nbim_about)  
> "This year" = budget year 2026 (film produced in November 2026). IMF 2026 vs NBIM (almost 20%) differ; IMF used.  

## p7_why - The fiscal rule

Spending only the return means the capital is never used up. The oil is converted into shares, bonds and buildings around the world, and Norwegians who are not yet born will share in it too.

> `fiscal_rule` [source_located] Under the fiscal rule (since 2001), transfers from the fund to the budget shall over time follow the expected real return on the fund, set at 4% in 2001 and reduced to 3% in 2017. (sources: mof_framework)  
> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Interpretation of the rule's purpose; consistent with the Ministry's framework description.  

## p7_insulate - The fiscal rule

It also protects the economy at home. Because spending is tied to the fund, not to this year's oil price, the IMF concluded that the rule and the fund would largely insulate Norway from a fall in oil revenue.

> `imf_insulate` [source_located] IMF (2014): the fiscal rule and the Government Pension Fund Global would largely insulate the economy from the direct effects of lower oil revenue. (sources: imf_2014)  

## p8_card - Beyond oil




## p8_mainland - Beyond oil

Oil is still big, but it is not the whole story. In 2025, about four-fifths of Norway's output came from the mainland economy: the industries, services and public sector outside oil, gas and shipping.

> `mainland_share` [source_located] In 2025, Mainland Norway (excluding petroleum activities and ocean transport) produced 79.8% of GDP: NOK 4,397,716m of NOK 5,511,334m (our calculation from SSB table 09189). (sources: ssb_09189)  

## p8_model - Beyond oil

That economy runs on an unusual model. About half of workers belong to a union. In wage talks, the industries that compete internationally negotiate first, and everyone else follows their lead, so pay keeps in step with what exporters can afford.

> `union_density` [source_located] Union density in Norway was 52% in 2024 (Fafo), and collective agreements covered 71% of employees in 2019. (sources: fafo_2025)  
> `frontfag` [source_located] In Norwegian wage bargaining, industries exposed to international competition negotiate first, setting a norm for the rest of the economy (SSB, 2023). (sources: ssb_frontfag)  

## p8_trust - Beyond oil

Income is spread evenly, and trust is high. In the latest OECD survey, 57 percent of Norwegians said they trusted their national government, against 40 percent across the OECD. Trust makes long-term rules, like the fund's, easier to keep.

> `trust_2025` [source_located] In the OECD Trust Survey, 57% of Norwegians had high or moderately high trust in the national government in 2025, against an OECD average of 40%. (sources: oecd_trust_2026)  
> `gini_2024` [source_located] Norway's Gini coefficient for equivalised household income was 0.256 in 2024 (SSB). (sources: ssb_gini)  
> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> "Trust makes long-term rules easier to keep" is the channel's interpretation.  

## p8_productivity - Beyond oil

But there is a warning sign. The OECD says Norway's productivity growth is now among the lowest in the OECD, and its lead in living standards over other rich countries has narrowed, from about 42% to 27% in fifteen years.

> `oecd_productivity` [source_located] The OECD (2024) says Norway's productivity growth is among the lowest in the OECD, and its living-standards lead over the richer half of OECD countries has shrunk from around 42% to 27% over 15 years. (sources: oecd_blog_2024)  

## p9_card - The risks




## p9_volatility - The risks

The first risk is the oil price. After the summer of twenty fourteen, oil fell to around a third of its level. Norway's currency weakened by about 30 percent, and unemployment rose from 3.6 to 4.7 percent.

> `oil_crash_2014` [source_located] After mid-2014 the oil price fell below USD 40 a barrel, around a third of its summer-2014 level; the krone was 30% weaker at the end of 2015 than two years earlier (Norges Bank). (sources: nb_papers_2016)  
> `unemployment_2014_16` [source_located] Norway's LFS unemployment rate rose from 3.6% in 2014 to 4.7% in 2016 (SSB). (sources: ssb_08517)  

## p9_decline - The risks

The second is time. More than half of Norway's known oil and gas, 57 percent, has already been produced. Output is forecast to fall by the end of the decade, as the world tries to move away from fossil fuels.

> `resources_produced` [source_located] Of Norway's total expected recoverable petroleum resources (about 15.72 billion Sm3 o.e.), 57% had been produced by the end of 2025. (sources: np_resources)  
> `production_forecast` [source_located] The Norwegian Offshore Directorate forecasts petroleum production of 239.3 million Sm3 o.e. in 2025 and 205.2 million in 2030. (sources: np_forecast, np_production)  

## p9_ageing - The risks

The third is age. Today there are about three people of working age for every Norwegian over sixty-five. By 2050, official projections imply about two. Pensions and care will cost more, just as oil money starts to shrink.

> `ageing` [source_located] SSB's 2024-based projections (main alternative) imply 3.2 people aged 20-64 for every person aged 65+ in 2024, falling to 2.1 by 2050 (31.7 and 47.6 people aged 65+ per 100 aged 20-64). (sources: ssb_14288)  

## p9_markets - The risks

And the fund itself is exposed to the world's markets. In the manager's own stress test, a fragmented world economy could knock 37 percent off its value.

> `stress_test` [source_located] NBIM's stress test (data at 31 Dec 2025): the 'Fragmented world' scenario gives a 37% drawdown of the fund; a replay of the global financial crisis gives -29.8%. (sources: nbim_stress_2025)

## p10_secret - The real secret
