# When Tokyo's Land Was Worth More Than Entire Countries

_At the end of the nineteen eighties, the land under Tokyo's Imperial Palace was estimated to be worth as much as all of California. Japan's land was worth about four times all the land in the United States. How the biggest bubble of modern times was built, how it burst, and why it took Japan's stock market thirty-four years to recover._

## h_stakes - The most expensive land on Earth

In Tokyo, at the end of the nineteen eighties, it was. At neighbouring land prices, economists estimated that the land under the Emperor's palace was worth as much as all of California, or all of Canada. Japan's land as a whole was worth about four times all the land in the United States. Then it all came down, and Japan's stock market would not see its old record again for more than thirty-four years.

> `palace` [source_located] LSE paper: 'At neighboring land prices, the value of land under the Emperor's palace and garden in Tokyo equaled that of all California or of Canada.' (sources: lse)

## m1_roots - Money with nowhere to go

Land in Japan had been a one-way bet for decades. Between nineteen fifty-five and nineteen ninety, land in the six largest cities rose twenty times faster than consumer prices. Nationwide, land gained about fifteen times its value between nineteen fifty-five and nineteen seventy alone. And from around nineteen eighty-three, prices in central Tokyo climbed faster still, as foreign financial firms rushed to open offices in the city.

> `roots` [source_located] LSE paper: 'Land values in the six largest cities outpaced the Consumer Prices Index (CPI) by twenty times from 1955 to 1990'; 'For land in the whole country, the 1955 to 1970 period produced gains of about 15 times the 1955 values.' MOF: 'The sharp rise in land prices had already started in around 1983 in the commercial area in central Tokyo. This was because foreign financial institutions began to launch their businesses in Tokyo'. (sources: lse, mof)  

## m1_plaza - Money with nowhere to go

It started with a deal. In September nineteen eighty-five, the Group of Five, the leading industrial economies, agreed to push the dollar down against the currencies of Japan and Germany. It became known as the Plaza Accord. Japan's currency soared, and the country feared a recession caused by it. So the central bank began cutting interest rates, again and again.

> `plaza` [source_located] MOF: in September 1985 the G5 agreed on concerted intervention to correct the dollar against the yen and the mark ('The Plaza Accord'); after it, an easy money policy; the official discount rate stayed at 2.50% until May 1989 (BOJ data in the MOF chapter); fund management boomed, 'even creating the word Zaitech'; a speculative boom ('Bubble Economy') became strong from around 1987; the BOJ worried, 'dry firewood piled up goes up in flames'. (sources: mof)  

## m1_cheap - Money with nowhere to go

By February nineteen eighty-seven, the official interest rate was two and a half percent, the lowest in Japan's history, and it stayed there until May nineteen eighty-nine. Companies had more cash than they could use. Japan's Ministry of Finance says fund management became so fashionable that a new word appeared, zaitech, financial engineering. Companies started making money from trading shares and land, not just from making things.

## m1_draw - Money with nowhere to go

Let me draw the machine that turned cheap money into a bubble. A company owns land. The bank lends against that land. The company uses the loan to buy more land, or shares. That pushes prices up. Higher prices make the land worth more, so the bank lends even more. Round and round. Each turn makes everyone feel richer, and makes the next loan look safe.

## m1_firewood - Money with nowhere to go

The central bank saw the danger. The Ministry of Finance history quotes a saying used at the time. Dry firewood piled up goes up in flames. The money was the firewood. But ordinary prices in the shops were stable, so there was no obvious reason to raise interest rates. And the pile kept growing.

## m2_ginza - The price of a square meter

In Tokyo's Ginza district, one square meter of land, about the size of a small table, was worth more than two hundred thousand dollars. Choice land downtown sold for the equivalent of nearly a billion dollars an acre. Office space in the financial district cost nearly seventy-five thousand dollars per square foot. Even simple houses rented for more than ten thousand dollars a month.

> `ginza` [source_located] LSE paper: 'In the Ginza district of Tokyo each square meter of land was worth well over US$200,000 with some plots approaching $300,000. Choice downtown land in Tokyo sold for the equivalent of nearly a billion dollars an acre'; 'Simple houses in Tokyo rented for more than $10,000 per month'; 'Office space for sale in Tokyo's financial district cost nearly $75,000 per square foot.' (sources: lse)  

## m2_total - The price of a square meter

Add it all up, and the numbers become absurd. By the end of nineteen eighty-nine, all the land in Japan was worth about fifteen trillion dollars. In just four years, its value had more than doubled.

## m2_banks - The price of a square meter

The banks were deep inside it. When the Ministry of Finance inspected them, it found that loans linked to land and to zaitech had significantly increased, and that the checks on those land loans were incomplete. Land was the collateral behind almost everything, and its price was the one number nobody wanted to question.

> `banks` [source_located] MOF: 'As a result of the inspections, land-related loans and loans for zaitech significantly increased. The defects of the management system, such as the incomplete examination of land-related loans… were also pointed out.' (sources: mof)

## m3_rates - The pin

Interest rates. In May nineteen eighty-nine, the central bank finally began to raise them. Then again in October, and again on Christmas Day. In March nineteen ninety came another rise, and in August, the rate reached six percent. In just over a year, the price of money had more than doubled.

> `hikes` [source_located] MOF (BOJ data): official discount rate 2.50% → 3.25% (31 May 1989) → 3.75% (11 Oct 1989) → 4.25% (25 Dec 1989) → 5.25% (20 Mar 1990) → 6.00% (30 Aug 1990); 'the official discount rate recorded 2.5%, which was the lowest level in history (as of) February 1987. This low interest rate was maintained until May 1989'. In March 1990 the MOF issued the 'Sōryō Kisei' (quantitative restrictions on real-estate-related loans). (sources: mof)  

## m3_warning - The pin

Some saw it coming. One of the paper's authors, the economist William Ziemba, was then working with the research institute of Yamaichi Securities in Tokyo. His model, comparing bond yields with company earnings, flashed a danger signal in April nineteen eighty-nine, months before the peak. It could say a crash was coming, but not when. And Yamaichi's top management, he writes, did not listen.

> `warning` [source_located] LSE paper: 'The crash in the Nikkei which started on the first trading day of 1990 was predictable in April 1989 using the bond-stock earnings yield model which signaled a crash but not when'; Ziemba was then consulting at the Yamaichi Research Institute of Yamaichi Securities; 'It is too bad Yamaichi's top management did not listen'. (sources: lse)  

## m3_crash - The pin

The stock market turned first. On the last trading day of nineteen eighty-nine, the Nikkei index closed near thirty-eight thousand nine hundred. From the very first trading day of nineteen ninety, it fell. By October, it had lost forty-eight percent of its value.

> `crash` [source_located] LSE paper: 'When it bottomed, the market had fallen 48% from 38,916 at the end of December 1989 to 20,222 on October 1, 1990'; 'In time sequence, the stock market fell first then the golf course membership market and finally the land markets'; the land crash began in 1991 and 'ushered in two decades of deflation, weak economic markets and a lost generation of young people'; 'By 1999 Yamaichi Securities [once the fourth largest securities firm in Japan] was bankrupt and ceased to exist'; George Soros 'shorted the Japanese market too soon in 1988 and lost about $1 billion'. (sources: lse)  

## m3_soros - The pin

## m3_land - The pin

Land held on a little longer. Even as shares fell, the public stayed bullish about property. So in March nineteen ninety, the Ministry of Finance stepped in directly. It told banks to keep their real estate loans from growing faster than their total lending. The flow of new money into land was cut off.

## m4_golf - The long fall

The strangest signal came from golf. In the bubble, memberships of golf clubs were traded like shares, and according to the researchers, that market was an even bigger bubble than stocks. It fell after the stock market, and before the land. Then, from nineteen ninety-one, land prices began a long decline.

Let me draw why this hurt for so long. Here is the loop from before, running backwards. Land prices fall. So the land that backs the loans is worth less. The banks are left with bad loans, and they stop lending. Companies cannot borrow, so they sell land and shares to pay their debts. That pushes prices down again. The same machine that built the bubble now takes it apart.

## m5_compare - Thirty-four years

Today, nobody thinks the Imperial Palace is worth as much as California. But the comparison survives because it captures something real. When cheap money meets something people believe can never lose value, prices stop measuring what a thing is worth. They start measuring how much people can borrow.

## payoff - The lesson

So what is the lesson of Japan's bubble? That a price can be real on paper and impossible in practice. Japan's land was never going to be sold all at once to anyone. Its value only existed while everyone kept believing, and kept borrowing. When the borrowing stopped, the belief went with it. Every bubble since has had its own version of the palace and California. The trick is to notice it while you are still inside.
