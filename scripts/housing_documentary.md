## m1_card - The price tag




## m1_ratio - The price tag

Start with a simple yardstick. Take the price of a typical existing home and divide it by the income of a typical household. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies tracks exactly that. In the nineteen nineties, a home cost about three point two years of income. In twenty nineteen, about four point one. In twenty twenty-five, four point seven times, just below the all-time high of five reached in twenty twenty-two.

> `ratio` [source_located] JCHS (Jun 2026): the median existing single-family price 'hovered near five times the median household income, falling slightly from the all-time high of 5.0 in 2022 to 4.7 in 2025. By comparison, this ratio was 4.1 in 2019 and averaged 3.2 throughout the 1990s.' Press release: 'Home prices have increased 54 percent nationwide since 2020.' (sources: jchs, jchs_pr)  

## m1_jump - The price tag

The biggest jump came fast. According to the same report, home prices rose fifty-four percent nationwide after twenty twenty. Wages rose too, but nowhere near that fast.

## m1_new - The price tag

New homes tell the same story over a longer stretch. The Census Bureau's median price for a newly built house sold in the summer of two thousand was one hundred and sixty-eight thousand eight hundred dollars. In the summer of twenty twenty-six, it was three hundred and ninety-three thousand three hundred dollars. These are dollars of the day, not adjusted for inflation, but the price more than doubled.

> `newprice` [source_located] Census/HUD via FRED MSPUS (median sales price of new houses sold, NSA): 2000Q3 $168,800; 2019Q3 $318,400; 2026Q3 $393,300. (sources: mspus)

## m2_card - The rate shock




## m2_rates - The rate shock

In the first week of twenty twenty-one, the average thirty-year fixed mortgage rate, tracked by Freddie Mac, fell to two point six five percent. That was the lowest point of that period. Then inflation arrived, and interest rates climbed. In early November twenty twenty-six, the same average stood at seven point one eight percent.

> `rates` [source_located] Freddie Mac PMMS via FRED: 30-year fixed average 2.65% (week of 2021-01-07, the series low of 2020-21); 7.18% (week of 2026-11-05). JCHS: 'The monthly mortgage payment on the median-priced home is $2,420 ... payments on the median-priced home were $1,240 at the end of 2020 ... total monthly homeownership costs balloon to $3,120.' (sources: pmms, jchs)  

## m2_payment - The rate shock

Higher prices times higher rates. According to Harvard's housing center, the monthly mortgage payment on the median-priced home reached two thousand four hundred and twenty dollars. At the end of twenty twenty, it was one thousand two hundred and forty dollars. The payment roughly doubled. Add insurance and property taxes, and the total monthly cost of owning that home comes to about three thousand one hundred and twenty dollars.

> `rates` [source_located] Freddie Mac PMMS via FRED: 30-year fixed average 2.65% (week of 2021-01-07, the series low of 2020-21); 7.18% (week of 2026-11-05). JCHS: 'The monthly mortgage payment on the median-priced home is $2,420 ... payments on the median-priced home were $1,240 at the end of 2020 ... total monthly homeownership costs balloon to $3,120.' (sources: pmms, jchs)  

## m2_lockin - The rate shock

And high rates did something strange. They froze the market. Half of all outstanding mortgages carried rates below four percent at the end of twenty twenty-five. If you sell, you give up that cheap loan and take a new one at around seven. So many owners simply stay put.

> `lockin` [source_located] JCHS (Jun 2026): 'With half of all outstanding mortgages carrying rates below 4 percent as of late 2025, many homeowners still face a strong financial disincentive to relocate.' 'Residential mobility fell to a record low of 11.2 percent in 2024.' 'In 2025, existing home sales were unchanged from 2024 at 4.1 million, stuck at a 30-year low that is two-thirds of the 6.1 million sales in 2021.' (sources: jchs)  

## m2_frozen - The rate shock

The numbers show it. The share of Americans who moved in a year fell to a record low of eleven point two percent in twenty twenty-four. And existing home sales were stuck at four point one million in twenty twenty-five, a thirty-year low, about two thirds of the sales in twenty twenty-one.

> `lockin` [source_located] JCHS (Jun 2026): 'With half of all outstanding mortgages carrying rates below 4 percent as of late 2025, many homeowners still face a strong financial disincentive to relocate.' ## m3_card - The missing homes




## m3_crash - The missing homes

Go back to two thousand and eight. The housing bubble burst, builders went bust, and construction collapsed. By our calculation from Census data, about about fifteen and a half million new homes were completed in the two thousands. In the twenty tens, it was about nine million. In twenty eleven alone, completions fell to about five hundred and eighty-five thousand, the lowest year in the Census records, which go back to nineteen sixty-eight.

> `building` [source_located] Census via FRED COMPUTNSA (monthly, thousands), our sums: completions ≈ 15.64 million in 2000–2009 and ≈ 9.15 million in 2010–2019; 585,000 in 2011, the lowest calendar year in the series (which starts in 1968); ≈ 1.49 million in 2025. (sources: completions)

## m3_shortage - The missing homes

How many homes are missing? Nobody agrees on one number. Harvard's housing center notes that estimates of the shortage range between one point two and five point five million homes. The research group Up for Growth puts the gap at about three point eight million homes. Either way, it is millions of homes that people need and that do not exist.

> `shortage` [source_located] JCHS (Jun 2026): 'Construction activity since 2022 has added roughly 900,000 vacant units at a time when various organizations estimate the shortage to be between 1.2 and 5.5 million units.' Up for Growth (Nov 2025): shortfall 'has narrowed, from 3.85 million homes in 2022 to 3.78 million homes in 2023.' (sources: jchs, upforgrowth)  

## m3_recovery - The missing homes

Builders have picked up the pace again. Completions came back to about one and a half million in twenty twenty-five. But filling a hole that took a decade to dig takes much longer than one good year.

## m3_transition - The missing homes

Which raises an obvious question. If homes are so valuable, why not just build more of them? Often, the answer is written in the rules.


## m4_card - The rules on the land




## m4_zoning - The rules on the land

In most American cities, local zoning rules decide what can be built on each piece of land. And very often, the answer is one house per lot, and nothing else. Researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, mapped Greater Los Angeles. They found that about seventy-eight percent of its residential land is reserved for single-family homes only.

> `zoning` [source_located] UC Berkeley Othering & Belonging Institute (Mar 2022): 'we find that 77.70 percent of residential land in Greater LA is exclusively reserved for single-family housing.' (sources: berkeley)  

## m4_meaning - The rules on the land

That means no apartments, no townhouses, no small duplex, on most of the land where people live. Even where demand is highest, the rules can keep the number of homes fixed.

> `zoning` [source_located] UC Berkeley Othering & Belonging Institute (Mar 2022): 'we find that 77.70 percent of residential land in Greater LA is exclusively reserved for single-family housing.' (sources: berkeley)

## m4_regulation - The rules on the land

Then there is the cost of the rules themselves. Permits, fees, codes and delays. The National Association of Home Builders, the builders' own trade group, estimates that regulation makes up about twenty-six point four percent of the price of a new single-family home. On an average new home, that is about one hundred and thirty-two thousand dollars.

> `regulation` [source_located] NAHB (Jun 2026): 'regulations imposed by government at all levels account for 26.4% of the final price of a new single-family home built for sale'; 'applied to the current average price ($499,500) of a new home, regulation accounts for $131,734 of the final house price.' (sources: nahb)  

## m4_debate - The rules on the land

That estimate comes from the industry, and many rules protect safety and the environment. But the direction is clear. Every extra step makes a new home slower and more expensive to build.

## m5_card - Locked out




## m5_age - Locked out

The National Association of Realtors tracks who buys homes. In its latest survey, the typical first-time buyer was forty years old, an all-time high. First-time buyers made up just twenty-two percent of all buyers, about half the historical norm before two thousand and eight.

> `firsttime` [source_located] NAR (Nov 2026): 'The share of first-time home buyers rose slightly to 22% from last year's record low of 21%'; 'still about half the historical norm of 40% seen before 2008'; 'the typical age of first-time buyers remained at an all-time high of 40'. JCHS: 'just 23 percent of listings in March 2026 were at a price affordable to a household earning $75,000 or less, down from 49 percent of listings in March 2019.' (sources: nar, jchs)  

## m5_listings - Locked out

And there is little on the shelf for them. According to Harvard's housing center, in March twenty nineteen, forty-nine percent of homes listed for sale were affordable to a household earning seventy-five thousand dollars or less. By March twenty twenty-six, it was just twenty-three percent.

## payoff - The lesson

So why did American homes become so expensive? Not because of one villain. A decade of too little building, rules that limit what can be built, and then a jump in prices and rates on top. For owners who bought early, it has been a windfall. For everyone else, the American dream now comes with a much higher price tag.
