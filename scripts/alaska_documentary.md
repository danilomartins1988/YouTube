## m1_rules - The check

The rules are simple. You must live in Alaska for the whole calendar year, and plan to stay there indefinitely. You must also have spent at least seventy-two hours in a row in the state during the past two years. In twenty twenty-five, six hundred and sixty-five thousand people applied, and six hundred and eighteen thousand were paid. That is most of a state with about seven hundred and thirty-nine thousand people.

## m2_oil - The fund

In nineteen seventy-six, as oil money began to flow, Alaskans voted to change their constitution. At least twenty-five percent of the state's oil and mineral royalties would go into a permanent fund. Later, a state law raised that to fifty percent for newer leases.

## m2_rooms - The fund

The fund has two rooms. The principal is locked. The constitution says it cannot be spent. The earnings, the profits from investing it, go into a second account, called the earnings reserve. That second room is where the dividend comes from. At the end of June twenty twenty-six, the fund's managers reported about ten point six billion dollars of realized earnings in that room, plus four billion already set aside for the next year's draw. Unlike the principal, the legislature can spend this room through the yearly budget, as the HB two sixty-three budget did this year.

## m5_jobs - What the money does

The big fear about free money is that people stop working. Economists Damon Jones and Ioana Marinescu tested that in Alaska. They found no effect on employment. Part-time work actually rose a little, by about one point eight percentage points. Their study was published in a peer-reviewed economics journal in twenty twenty-two.

## payoff - The lesson

So why does Alaska pay you to live there? Because in nineteen seventy-six, it decided that oil money belonged to everyone, and locked it away. That fund grew to about ninety-two billion dollars. But once the state started paying its bills from the same account, every dividend became a choice. The check is not shrinking by accident. It is shrinking by vote. Every spring, the legislature decides again how much of the oil fortune goes to people, and how much goes to the government. The fund keeps growing. The argument over the check does too.
