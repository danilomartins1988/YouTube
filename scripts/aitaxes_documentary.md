# If AI Replaces Workers, Who Pays the Taxes?

_Rosa, the mayor of a fictional small town, watches its biggest employer hand the work to robots that never get a paycheck. Following the last pay stub from the warehouse, she discovers that almost all of Washington's money comes from people's income and paychecks, then tours an idea fair of fixes (robot taxes, taxing owners, taxing shopping, AI dividends and basic-income pilots), weighing one good reason and one catch for each. The answer: nobody has decided yet; every fix just moves the bill onto different shoulders._

## co_hook - The robot with no paycheck

Meet Rosa. She's the mayor of Pinewick, a small town with one big employer, the warehouse on the hill. This morning, the warehouse is throwing a party. Not for the workers. For the robots. They lift faster. They never get tired. They never take a sick day. And there's one more thing robots never do. They never get a paycheck. Which means they never pay income tax. Rosa looks at the shiny new machines and asks the question nobody at the party wants to hear. If robots do the work, who pays the taxes?

## c2_split - Follow the paycheck

So Rosa follows Stub. Every payday, before Ray ever saw his money, some of it was already gone. In most states, a slice went to the state capital, to help pay for schools and roads. A slice went to Washington as income tax. And another slice went to Social Security, the check that Ray's parents get every month.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Mechanism: withholding of state income tax (most states), federal income tax and FICA payroll tax. No figures.  

## c2_big - Follow the paycheck

Then Stub shows Rosa the big picture. In America, almost nine out of every ten dollars the federal government collects come from people, through their income taxes and the taxes taken out of their paychecks.

> `fed_people` [data_derived] FY2026 federal receipts: individual income taxes $2,845.5bn + social insurance and retirement receipts $1,811.8bn = $4,657.3bn of $5,403.3bn = 86.2% (OUR CALCULATION, Treasury MTS). Caveat: individual income tax also falls on investment income, not only wages. (sources: mts_fy26, cbo_mbr26)  

## c2_corp - Follow the paycheck

And companies? Corporate income tax brought in only about seven cents of every federal tax dollar in the year that just ended. Rivet's company pays that. Rivet itself pays nothing. Rivet looks a little embarrassed.

> `fed_corp` [data_derived] FY2026 corporation income taxes $381.4bn of $5,403.3bn total receipts = 7.1% (OUR CALCULATION, Treasury MTS); down from $452.1bn in FY2025 (8.6%). (sources: mts_fy26, cbo_mbr26)  

## c2_draw - Follow the paycheck

Let me draw why this matters. Here's the government's money tank. Most of the water pours in from one big tap, people's paychecks. A thin tap drips in from company profits. Now watch what happens if robots take over the work. The big tap shrinks. The thin tap might grow, if profits grow. But the whole tank was built around the big one.

## c2_ss - Follow the paycheck

Social Security would feel it first. It's paid for mainly by one tax, the payroll tax, a tax on wages. No wages, no payroll tax. And it's already under strain. Its trustees say its reserve fund is projected to run dry in 2034.

> `ss_2034` [source_located] 2026 Trustees Report: combined OASI and DI trust fund reserves projected depleted in Q3 2034 (a PROJECTION under intermediate assumptions). (sources: ssa_tr26, aaa_tr26)  

## c2_ss2 - Follow the paycheck

After that, the money still coming in from paychecks would cover only part of every check, unless Congress changes the law. Stub does the math on his fingers, and he doesn't have many fingers.

> `ss_payable` [source_located] After depletion, continuing income would cover about 83% of scheduled benefits (combined funds), unless Congress acts. (sources: ssa_tr26, aaa_tr26)  

## c2_world - Follow the paycheck

Is America unusual? Not really. Across the world's rich democracies, about half of all tax money comes from taxes on work and personal income. Everywhere you look, the system leans on the paycheck.

> `oecd_half` [data_derived] OECD average 2023: personal income tax 23.7% + social security contributions 25.5% = 49.2% of total tax revenue (OUR CALCULATION; payroll taxes add a little more). (sources: oecd_rs25, acctimes25)  

## c2_debt - Follow the paycheck

And Washington already spends more than it collects, a story we told in Who Actually Owns America's Debt. Rosa doesn't need the details. She gets the picture. If the biggest tap slows down, the hole only gets deeper.

> `fed_deficit` [source_located] FY2026 federal deficit $2.0 trillion (CBO). (sources: cbo_mbr26)

## c3_fair - The idea fair

So Rosa goes looking for answers, and she finds a whole fair of them. Economists, think tanks and governments have proposed all kinds of fixes, and every booth promises to keep the money flowing. Stub gives her one rule. For every idea, find one good reason, and one catch.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Story beat; the booths summarize proposals in the public debate.  

## c3_robot - The idea fair

Booth one. Tax the robots. The idea got famous in 2017, when Bill Gates said that if a robot does a worker's job, maybe the robot should be taxed at a similar level. Rivet takes one step backward.

> `gates17` [source_located] Feb 2017 Quartz interview: Bill Gates said that if a robot does the work of a taxed human worker, 'you'd think that we'd tax the robot at a similar level.' (sources: gates_qz17)  

## c3_korea - The idea fair

That same year, South Korea tried something close. It didn't put a tax on robots. Instead, it trimmed a tax break for companies that buy automation machines. Headlines called it the first robot tax anyway.

> `korea17` [source_located] South Korea's 2017 tax revision plan reduced the tax credit for investment in automation equipment (by up to 2 points from 3-7%); widely called the first 'robot tax', though no tax was levied on robots. (sources: korea17)  

## c3_robot_scale - The idea fair

The good reason? It keeps money coming in as jobs go, and it slows things down so workers have time to adjust. The catch? What exactly counts as a robot? A dishwasher? A piece of software? And tax machines too hard, and companies may just buy fewer of them, or build somewhere else. Europe's lawmakers looked at a robot tax and turned it down.

> `eu_reject` [source_located] On Feb 16, 2017 the European Parliament rejected the proposal for a robot tax in its civil-law rules on robotics resolution. (sources: gates_qz17)  

## c3_profit - The idea fair

Booth two. Tax the owners. If robots make companies more profitable, tax those profits, or the money their shareholders make, a little more. In Who Gets Rich When Robots Do the Work, we saw that the gains from machines tend to flow to whoever owns them.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Proposal summary; Ep 64 linked by title (its figures not repeated).  

## c3_profit_scale - The idea fair

The good reason? It follows the money to wherever the robots send it. The catch? Profits can travel. A big company can book its profits in a country with lower taxes far more easily than Ray could move his job.

> `profit_shift` [source_located] Multinational companies can shift profits to low-tax jurisdictions (OECD BEPS); the 15% global minimum tax aims to limit this. (sources: oecd_beps)  

## c3_shop - The idea fair

Booth three. Tax the shopping. Robots don't earn anything, but somebody buys what they make. Most rich countries already collect a big national sales tax, called a value added tax. America is the only one in the club without one.

> `us_novat` [source_located] The US is the only OECD member without a national VAT; VAT averages about a fifth of OECD tax revenue (20.5%, 2023). (sources: oecd_vat, acctimes25)  

## c3_shop_scale - The idea fair

The good reason? It's hard to dodge. Everyone who buys things pays, wherever the robot happens to live. The catch? Families with less money spend almost everything they earn, so a shopping tax takes a bigger bite out of their budget than out of a rich family's.

> `regressive` [source_located] Consumption taxes take a larger share of lower-income households' income because they spend a larger share of what they earn (CBO). (sources: cbo_vat)  

## c3_draw - The idea fair

Let me draw what all three booths have in common. Today, the tax bill sits mostly on workers. A robot tax slides it over to whoever buys the machines. A profit tax slides it to the owners. A shopping tax slides it to the shoppers. Nobody makes the bill disappear. Each idea just decides whose shoulders it lands on.

## c4_dividend - Send everyone a check?

Then Rosa reaches the strangest booth of all. It isn't a new tax. It's a dividend. The idea is simple. If robots and AI make the country richer, share a slice of that wealth with every citizen, the way a company pays its shareholders.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Proposal summary (citizen or AI dividend).  

## c4_alaska - Send everyone a check?

One state already does something like this, with oil. Every year, Alaska sends a check to nearly every resident, paid out of a giant investment fund built on oil money. We told that story in Alaska, The State That Pays You to Live There. Rosa wonders. Could a country build a fund like that out of robots?

> `ak_pfd` [source_located] Alaska pays an annual Permanent Fund Dividend to nearly every resident (618,863 paid in 2025 of a 738,737 population) from an oil-revenue investment fund. (sources: pfd_div)  

## c4_div_scale - Send everyone a check?

The good reason? Everyone shares in the gains, even people who don't own a single share of stock. The catch? Alaska's fund was filled with oil the state already owned. To fill a robot fund, a government would first have to own part of the robots, or tax them. And when money gets tight, the size of the check becomes a political fight.

> `ak_fight` [source_located] The dividend's size is set each year by the legislature and governor and has been cut (2016 veto) amid budget fights (Ep 48 FACT_CHECK). (sources: pfd_div)  

## c4_ubi - Send everyone a check?

Some people go one step further. Just give everyone a basic income, no strings attached. So Rosa asks, has anyone actually tried? Yes. And the results surprised both sides.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Transition; no figures.  

## c4_stockton - Send everyone a check?

In Stockton, California, a pilot sent a small group of residents $500 a month, no strings attached. Plenty of critics expected people to stop working. Instead, after a year, more of them had full-time jobs than in a comparison group that got nothing.

> `seed500` [source_located] SEED: 125 Stockton residents received $500 a month for 24 months, no strings attached (from Feb 2019). (sources: seed21)  
> `seed_ft` [source_located] SEED first year: recipients' full-time employment 28% -> 40% vs control 32% -> 37% (preliminary). (sources: seed21)  

## c4_or - Send everyone a check?

Then came a much bigger, carefully controlled study, run by a research group called OpenResearch. It sent low-income people a thousand dollars a month, for years, and compared them with people who got almost nothing.

> `or_design` [source_located] OpenResearch study: 1,000 low-income people got $1,000/month for three years; 2,000 controls got $50/month (randomized). (sources: or_nber)  

## c4_or2 - Send everyone a check?

This time, people worked a little less, on average about an hour or two a week. They spent more on everyday needs, and they had more choices. But the money didn't lead to better jobs, and the happiness boost faded. So, a cushion, not a cure.

> `or_result` [source_located] Recipients worked 1-2 fewer hours a week; labor force participation -3.9 points; earned income about $1,800/yr lower; no impact on job quality; spending up; well-being gain in year 1 faded (NBER w32719 rev. Sept 2025). (sources: or_nber)  

## c4_ubi_scale - Send everyone a check?

The good reason? A floor that nobody falls through, even if work disappears. The catch? Paying everyone is enormously expensive, and that money has to come from somewhere. Which brings Rosa right back to her first question.

> Pro/con summary; no figures.

## c5_verdict - So who pays?

So, if AI replaces workers, who pays the taxes? The honest answer is, nobody has decided yet. Today, it's mostly workers. If work shrinks, the bill has to move, to profits, to shopping, to the owners of the robots, or to some mix of them. Every option has a good reason and a catch. And in the end, the choice isn't really about robots. It's about which people carry the bill.
