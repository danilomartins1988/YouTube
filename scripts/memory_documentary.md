# The Three Companies That Decide How Much Your Memory Costs

_The memory inside every computer and phone is made almost entirely by three companies. In 2026 they chose a better-paying customer, artificial intelligence, and the price of ordinary memory exploded. Who decides, why, and why the boom may end the way every memory boom has ended._

What if the price of one tiny part inside your computer jumped more than five times in a single year, and almost nobody outside the industry noticed why?

## h_stakes - The price on the shelf

That part is a memory chip. In October twenty twenty-five, big buyers paid eight dollars and seventy cents for one standard sixteen gigabit memory chip. By September twenty twenty-six, the same chip cost forty-eight dollars. That is a rise of about four hundred and fifty percent. And behind that price are just three companies, making one quiet choice.

## m1_what - The shelf

The chip is called DRAM. It is the working memory of every computer, phone, game console and server. It holds whatever the machine is thinking about right now. Without it, nothing runs. And it is a commodity, sold by the billions, one tiny chip after another. Most of it is sold under contracts. Big buyers, like computer and phone makers, agree a price with the chipmaker every few months. Anyone without a contract, or who needs more than the contract covers, buys on the spot market, at whatever price the day brings.

## m1_stick - The shelf

Take one ordinary sixteen gigabyte memory stick. Inside it sit eight of those chips. In October twenty twenty-five, the chips on that stick cost big buyers about seventy dollars. By September twenty twenty-six, about three hundred and eighty dollars. And that is only the chips, before anyone builds the stick, ships it or puts it on a shelf.

## m1_record - The shelf

The worst jump came at the start of twenty twenty-six. The research firm TrendForce expected contract prices for PC memory to rise by more than double in just three months, the largest quarterly price surge it had ever recorded. Computer makers that had signed supply deals watched their stockpiles shrink. Those without deals had to pay whatever the market asked. Server memory, the kind data centers buy, was expected to jump about ninety percent in the same quarter. And the biggest cloud companies in North America and China were negotiating long-term deals to lock in supply.

> `q1_record` [source_located] TrendForce (Feb 2026): conventional DRAM contract prices expected to rise 90-95% QoQ in 1Q26; PC DRAM contract prices more than 100%, 'the largest quarterly price surge on record'; server DRAM about +90%; CSPs negotiate long-term agreements. (sources: tf_1q26)

## m2_three - Three companies

Almost all of it comes from three companies. In the second quarter of twenty twenty-six, Samsung took 39.4% of all DRAM sales, SK hynix 24.9% and Micron 23.3%. Together, almost nine in every ten dollars spent on memory went to just these three. The whole market reached about one hundred and fifty-five billion dollars in a single quarter, up almost sixty percent from the quarter before. And look at how the order moved. SK hynix sells the most AI memory, so its prices rose the least, and its share fell from 28.8% to 24.9%. Samsung, which started mass production of the newest AI memory early, pulled further ahead.

> `big3` [source_located] TrendForce (7 Sep 2026): in 2Q26 Samsung had 39.4% of DRAM industry revenue, SK hynix 24.9% and Micron 23.3% (together 87.6%, our sum); in 1Q26 the shares were 38.5%, 28.8% and 22.4%; SK hynix's HBM-heavy mix limited its ASP growth; Samsung moved early into HBM4 mass production; CXMT ranked fourth with 9.5%; total DRAM revenue reached nearly US$154.73bn, up 59.5% QoQ, driven by sharp rises in conventional DRAM contract prices. (sources: tf_2q26, evertiq_2q26)  

## m2_draw - Three companies

Let me draw why three is such a powerful number. A memory factory costs billions and takes years to build. So nobody new can jump in when prices rise. And when only three sellers exist, none of them has to agree with the others on anything. Each one simply looks at its own order book and asks one question: which customer pays the most for the same silicon?

## m3_hbm - The customer who pays more

Artificial intelligence. The chips that train and run AI models need a special, extremely fast kind of memory called HBM, high bandwidth memory, stacked right next to the processor. It sells for far more. But it is greedy. According to Micron, making HBM takes about three times the wafers that ordinary DDR five memory needs for the same amount of memory. And Micron expects the market for HBM alone to reach one hundred billion dollars by twenty twenty-eight. That is a forecast, but it tells you where the factories want to go.

> `hbm3x` [source_located] Micron (via The Next Platform, June 2024): HBM3E consumes 3x the wafers of normal DDR5 to produce a given amount of capacity at a given process node. Tom's Hardware (Dec 2025): HBM requires three times as much wafer space as DDR5; Micron projects the HBM market at $100bn by 2028. (sources: nextplatform_2024, thw_micron_dec25)  

## m3_draw - The customer who pays more

Let me draw what that means for the factory. Imagine one silicon wafer. Cut it into ordinary memory, and you get a certain amount of capacity to sell to computer and phone makers. Turn the same wafer into HBM, and you get only about a third as much memory, but each bit sells for a lot more to AI companies. Every wafer moved to HBM is a wafer taken away from the memory in your laptop.

> `hbm3x` [source_located] Micron (via The Next Platform, June 2024): HBM3E consumes 3x the wafers of normal DDR5 to produce a given amount of capacity at a given process node. Tom's Hardware (Dec 2025): HBM requires three times as much wafer space as DDR5; Micron projects the HBM market at $100bn by 2028. (sources: nextplatform_2024, thw_micron_dec25)

## m3_crucial - The customer who pays more

Some companies went further. In December twenty twenty-five, Micron announced it was leaving its Crucial consumer brand, the memory sticks and drives sold in shops, to improve supply for its larger, strategic customers. Crucial products shipped until the end of February twenty twenty-six. And Micron said that even then, it expected to meet only half to two-thirds of what its key customers wanted. Its customers, worried about long-term access to memory, were lining up for contracts lasting several years.

> `crucial` [source_located] The Register (3 Dec 2025): Micron is abandoning its Crucial consumer memory and storage business 'to improve supply and support for our larger, strategic customers in faster-growing segments' (Sumit Sadana); Crucial products ship through the end of February 2026, warranties continue. (sources: register_crucial)  
> `half_demand` [source_located] Tom's Hardware (17 Dec 2025): Micron expects to meet only 'half to two-thirds' of demand from its key customers; supply constraints to 'persist beyond calendar 2026'; customers are 'concerned about long-term access to memory' and line up for multi-year contracts; first new Idaho fab expected to produce chips in mid-2027. (sources: thw_micron_dec25)  

## m3_squeeze - The customer who pays more

By September twenty twenty-six, TrendForce said suppliers had significantly curtailed memory for consumer products. That is why consumer memory was expected to see the strongest price increases of all. The phone in your pocket and the laptop on your desk were now competing for silicon with the biggest data centers on Earth. And it was not only Micron. According to Nikkei, the leading manufacturers could meet only about sixty percent of global demand, even with their expansion plans.

> `consumer_curtailed` [source_located] TrendForce (Sep 2026): suppliers have significantly curtailed consumer DRAM supply, so consumer DRAM is expected to post the strongest price growth; conventional DRAM contract price growth to moderate to 13-18% QoQ in 3Q26 because PC and smartphone customers have limited ability to absorb further increases. (sources: tf_2q26)  
> `nikkei` [source_located] Computerworld citing Nikkei Asia (Apr 2026): leading manufacturers can meet only about 60% of global demand despite expansion plans; memory prices rose about 90% in 1Q26; balance is not expected to normalise until 2028. (sources: computerworld_nikkei)

## m4_cxmt - The challenger

CXMT, China's largest memory chipmaker, is trying. In the second quarter of twenty twenty-six it was already the world's fourth largest DRAM maker, with about nine and a half percent of sales. In July twenty twenty-six it listed its shares in Shanghai and raised at least eight point six billion dollars. On the first day of trading, the shares jumped four hundred and sixty-six percent. That made it the most valuable company listed in mainland China, worth more than four hundred and eighty-seven billion dollars on paper. Investors were betting that a fourth seller would get rich from the shortage too.

(sources: tf_2q26, evertiq_2q26)

## m4_limits - The challenger

But CXMT has a problem. Its access to advanced chipmaking tools is restricted, and some American lawmakers have called for blocking US companies from buying its memory. The research firm Counterpoint forecasts its share at about eleven percent by twenty twenty-eight, and estimates it would need at least fifteen percent to be competitive in the long run. A challenger, yes. A rescue for buyers, not yet.

## m5_2023 - Boom and bust

Just look at what happened only three years earlier. In early twenty twenty-three, demand collapsed after the pandemic buying spree. In one quarter, Micron's revenue fell from seven point eight billion dollars a year earlier to three point seven billion dollars, and it lost two point three billion dollars. For every dollar of memory it sold, it was losing about thirty-three cents before other costs, after writing down chips it could no longer sell at a profit. Micron cut the wafers going into its factories by about a fifth. Even Samsung, which had refused for months, finally announced a meaningful cut in memory output.

> `crash2023` [source_located] Tom's Hardware (Mar 2023): Micron's revenue in Q2 FY2023 was $3.693bn, down from $7.786bn a year earlier, with a $2.312bn GAAP net loss, $1.43bn of inventory write-downs and gross margin of -32.7%; in late 2022 Micron cut DRAM and NAND wafer starts by about 20%. Motley Fool (Apr 2023): after its profit crashed, Samsung announced a 'meaningful' cut in memory production. (sources: thw_micron_2023, thw_micron_cut, fool_2023)  

## m5_draw - Boom and bust

Let me draw the memory cycle. Prices rise, so profits soar. Profits pay for new factories. But factories take years, so they all arrive at once. Supply floods in, prices crash, and everyone cuts back. Then shortages return, and the wheel turns again.

## m5_next - Boom and bust

And the new factories are coming. Micron's first new plant in Idaho is expected to start producing chips in mid twenty twenty-seven. CXMT is expanding. Forecasts reported by Nikkei say supply and demand should return to balance around twenty twenty-eight, and the memory maker TeamGroup does not expect balance before the second half of twenty twenty-eight. These are forecasts, not facts. TeamGroup even expects the tightest months still ahead, from the end of twenty twenty-six to the middle of twenty twenty-seven. But they point to the same place the cycle always goes.

## payoff - The lesson

So who decides how much your memory costs? Not you, and not the shop. Three companies, each choosing the customer who pays the most for every wafer. Today that customer is artificial intelligence. Tomorrow, when the new factories open, it may be you again. So the next time a memory stick costs more than you expected, remember that its chips were promised to the highest bidder long before it reached the shelf. In memory, the price is never about the chip. It is about who is first in line.
