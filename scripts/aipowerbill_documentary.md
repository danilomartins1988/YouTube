# Why AI Could Make Your Electricity Bill Explode

_Maya, a homeowner in Northern Virginia, opens her $150 electric bill while AI data centers rise down the road. Following her bill through the grid's power auction, the utilities' building plans and the new rules for giant customers, the film asks who really pays for the AI boom's electricity, and finds the answer is decided less by physics than by rate cases._

## co_hook - The power-hungry boom

Meet Maya. She lives in Northern Virginia, and this is her electric bill. About about a hundred and fifty dollars a month. Now look at her new neighbors. Giant windowless buildings, packed with AI chips, humming day and night. The grid's own watchdog says those buildings are a big reason why. So who really pays for the AI boom? The tech giants... or Maya?

## c1_machines - The power-hungry boom

Here's what those buildings eat. A data center turns electricity into answers, and heat, around the clock. Government researchers at Berkeley Lab measured it. In their latest count, America's data centers were already using as much electricity as about seventeen million homes. And their forecast says that could nearly double, or even triple, before the decade is out.

## c1_forecast - The power-hungry boom

Grid operators see it coming. PJM runs the grid for Maya's state and several others. A few years ago, it expected demand on the hottest afternoons to barely grow. Now it expects it to grow about twelve times as fast, mainly because of data centers. These are forecasts, and some of these projects will never be built. Remember that. It turns out to matter a lot.

> `pjm_peak` [source_located] PJM 2026 Long-Term Load Forecast (Jan 14, 2026): summer peak forecast ~222,000 MW in 2036 (2025 actual: over 161,000 MW); 3.6%/yr over ten years vs 0.3%/yr in the 2021 forecast (OUR CALCULATION: 12 times the growth rate); data centers the main driver; near-term 2027 peak trimmed 3,735 MW. (sources: pjm_lf26)

## c3_card - The auction at the cap




## c3_auction - The auction at the cap

Every year, PJM holds an auction, paying power plants to promise they'll be there when the grid needs them most. A few years ago, that promise was a bargain. This July, it cleared at the maximum the rules allow, more than eleven times higher. And that maximum is a cap, set with the governors, to stop it going even higher.

> `bra2829` [source_located] PJM 2028/29 BRA (Jul 14, 2026): cleared at the FERC-approved cap $325/MW-day (2.5% below $333.44 in 2027/28); cleared supply x price = $16.4 billion; 6,831 MW short of the reliability requirement; grid serves more than 67 million people in 13 states and DC; first auctions in PJM history (this and 2027/28) in which the whole RTO fell short; 525 MW UCAP new generation and uprates cleared. 2024/25 RTO price: $28.92/MW-day (OUR CALCULATION: $325 / $28.92 = 11.2 times). (sources: pjm_bra2829, pjm_bra2425)  

## c3_tickets - The auction at the cap

Think of it like concert tickets. The seats are power plants. The crowd is everyone who needs electricity on the hottest day of the year. For years the crowd barely grew, and tickets were cheap. Then a new group showed up at the box office, with very deep pockets and a very big appetite. When the venue can't add seats fast enough, the price goes up for everyone in line.

## c3_monitor - The auction at the cap

So how much of this is AI? The market monitor estimates that across its last several auctions, data centers were behind almost half of the capacity bill. And that bill is shared by everyone who uses power at the peak. Including Maya.

> `imm_dc` [source_located] PJM Market Monitor (Jul 28, 2026): of the $16.4B 2028/29 capacity charges, $6.3B (38.2%) resulted from data center load; over the last four auctions $29.4B (46.2%). ## c3_fix - The auction at the cap

The monitor's proposed fix is blunt. Take the data centers out of the shared auction, and make them buy their own power. PJM is trying other routes, like letting big new users connect if they agree to cut back when the grid is stressed. None of it has settled the real question. Who pays?

## c4_rate - Who pays for the wires

Here's the part most people never see. Let me draw it. A regulated utility earns a set return on what it builds. Build more, and its earnings grow. Then the cost is split among groups of customers. Homes, shops, factories, data centers. And how it's split is a choice, made by regulators, with formulas.

## c4_study - Who pays for the wires

And Virginia's own numbers show why that choice matters. In Dominion's latest rate case, a cost study looked at the customers who would form the new data center class. On the big transmission lines, they earned the utility about a seventh of the return that other large customers did. In plain English, the biggest users were paying relatively little for the biggest lines. The regulators say they'll look at that split next.

> `scc_ror` [source_located] Virginia SCC final order PUR-2025-00058: the class cost-of-service study shows customers that will form GS-5 (large, mostly data centers) earned a per-books return of 3.01% on the transmission function vs 22.41% for remaining GS-4 customers (6.28% vs 20.75% overall); the commission will examine transmission cost allocation in the next proceeding. OUR CALCULATION: 3.01 / 22.41 = 0.13, about one-seventh. (sources: scc_dom)  
> SCC order (3.01% vs 22.41% per-books transmission return); "a seventh" = OUR CALCULATION.

## c5_card - The fightback




## c5_ohio - The fightback

But regulators are pushing back. In Ohio, after the local utility paused new data center deals around Columbus, a new rule arrived. Big new data centers must pay for at least eighty-five percent of the capacity they reserve, whether they use it or not, for years. Walk away early, and there's an exit fee.

> `aep_ohio` [source_located] AEP Ohio data center tariff adopted by PUCO (Case 24-0508; AEP release July 9, 2025): new data centers of 25 MW or more pay for at least 85% of subscribed capacity; contract up to 12 years incl. a ramp of up to 4 years; exit fee of 36 months of minimum charges. (sources: aep_dct)  

## c5_gs5 - The fightback

Virginia followed, with a new rate class just for the giants. Long contracts. Minimum bills. Collateral. Dominion says the goal is simple. Stop one group of customers from subsidizing another.

> `gs5` [source_located] Virginia SCC final order: new rate class GS-5 for customers of 25 MW or more with load factor >= 75%; for service agreements from Jan 1, 2027 the contract term is 14 years (ramp up to 4 years); minimum demand charges 85% of contracted demand for distribution and transmission, 60% for generation; exit fee covering outstanding minimum charges. (sources: scc_dom)  

## c5_hearing - The fightback

And these fights happen in public. When Virginia decided its rules, the parties in the room included lawyers for Amazon, Google and Microsoft, the data center industry, consumer advocates, and the state's own attorney general's office. Anyone can read the filings. Most people never do, which is exactly why the outcome can surprise them on their bill.

## c5_why - The fightback

Why does a minimum bill matter so much? Picture a utility building a line for a data center that never shows up, or that shuts down when the AI boom cools. Without a contract, that line stays in everyone's rates for decades. With a minimum bill, the data center pays either way. That one clause may decide more about Maya's future bill than any chip.

## c5_deals - The fightback

And the tech giants are signing their own power deals. Microsoft is buying the output of a reactor being brought back to life at the old Three Mile Island plant. Meta signed a long-term deal that keeps an Illinois nuclear plant running, its owner says without ratepayer support. Amazon locked in nuclear power in Pennsylvania for well over a decade. Some deals add new power to the grid. Others reserve power that already exists.

## c5_counter - The fightback

And there's a stronger counterpoint. A Berkeley Lab study found that in recent years, more demand has usually pushed prices down, not up. More customers share the fixed cost of the grid. It's not clear that will hold. But new supply is coming. The EIA expects a wave of new solar and wind this year and next. And PJM itself trimmed its near-term forecast, after checking data center requests more carefully.

> `lbnl_load` [source_located] LBNL report summarized by Brattle (Oct 17, 2025): 'Load growth has tended to depress retail electricity prices in recent years, though it is unclear to what extent this will hold in the future.' National average retail prices have tracked inflation. (sources: lbnl_prices)

## c6_dials - Will your bill explode?

But averages hide the hot spots. Where data centers cluster and the grid runs short, the capacity and wire slices are the ones that can jump. Whether they land on Maya comes down to three dials. First, do the giants sign minimum-bill contracts, so they carry their own grid costs? Second, does new supply arrive as fast as new demand? Third, how do regulators split the cost of shared lines? Turn them all the right way, and growth can even spread costs thinner. Turn them the other way, and households help pay for the AI boom.
