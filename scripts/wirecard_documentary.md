# The $2 Billion That Never Existed

_A German payments company joined the country's top stock index, was worth billions, and then admitted that almost two billion euros in its accounts probably never existed. How it rose, who warned, where the money "was", how it collapsed, and who failed to see it._

## h_question - The missing billions

What if one of Europe's most celebrated tech companies said it had billions in the bank, and then, one day, admitted that the money had probably never existed?

## h_stakes - The missing billions

That happened in June twenty twenty. The German payments company Wirecard said that cash worth about 2.2 billion dollars, converted at that year's average exchange rate, was most likely not there at all. Days later, it was bankrupt. Germany's own finance minister would later call it the country's greatest postwar accounting fraud.

(sources: irish_2020, aljazeera_2020, bbk_rates)

## m1_origin - The rocket

Wirecard was founded In 1999, in Aschheim, a suburb of Munich, during the first internet boom. Its job sounded simple: process card payments for websites. Its early customers were businesses many banks saw as high risk, such as online gambling and adult sites. In 2005, it got onto the stock market without a classic listing, by merging into a company that was already listed in Frankfurt.

> `founded` [source_located] Quartr: Wirecard was founded in 1999, based in the Munich suburb of Aschheim; it processed payments for high-risk online merchants such as adult content and online gambling; in 2005 it merged into InfoGenie Europe AG, listed in Frankfurt. (sources: quartr)  

## m1_draw - The rocket

Let me draw what a payment processor does. You buy something online and tap your card. The shop sends the payment to a processor. The processor checks it, talks to the card network and the banks, and makes sure the shop gets paid. For each payment, it keeps a tiny fee. Millions of tiny fees can become a very big business.

## m1_asia - The rocket

And Wirecard grew fast, especially far from home. By early twenty sixteen, about 40 percent of its revenue came from Southeast Asia and the Middle East. In twenty seventeen it reported revenue of more than 1.1 billion dollars for the first time. By then, its shares had risen roughly eight hundred percent since its stock market debut in twenty oh five.

> `asia` [source_located] Quartr: by early 2016 the stock was up roughly 800% since 2005 and about 40% of Wirecard's revenue came from Southeast Asia and the Middle East; in 2017 it posted revenue of over €1 billion for the first time (about US$1.13bn at the 2017 average of 1.1297). (sources: quartr, bbk_rates)  

## m1_dax - The rocket

Then came the big moment. In September 2018, Wirecard entered the DAX, Germany's index of its top companies, and it pushed out Commerzbank, one of the country's biggest banks. Wirecard had fewer than 5,000 employees, a tenth of the bank's, but was worth twice as much. At its peak, it was valued at about 28 billion dollars.

> `dax` [source_located] Reuters (Sept 2018): Commerzbank was expelled from the DAX to make room for Wirecard, effective Sept. 24; Wirecard's market value was double Commerzbank's (€10.6bn); Wirecard had a staff of less than 5,000, one tenth that of Commerzbank. (sources: reuters_dax)  
> `peak` [source_located] Reuters (June 2020): Wirecard was worth US$28 billion at its peak; Quartr: its market capitalisation briefly topped €24 billion around its DAX entry (≈ US$28bn at the 2018 average of 1.1810). (sources: reuters_insolvency, quartr, bbk_rates)

## m2_ft - The warnings

In 2015, a journalist at the Financial Times, Dan McCrum, began asking questions about Wirecard's acquisitions and its accounts. In January twenty nineteen, the paper went further. Based on a whistleblower, a former head of legal for Wirecard in Asia, it reported suspicions about the company's business in Singapore.

> `ft_2015` [source_located] Quartr: Financial Times journalist Dan McCrum wrote in 2015 an article raising questions about Wirecard's acquisitions and accounting; in January 2019 the FT (McCrum and Stefania Palma) reported suspicions raised by a whistleblower, the former head of legal for Wirecard Asia, in Singapore. (sources: quartr)  

## m2_bafin - The warnings

What happened next was extraordinary. Germany's financial regulator, BaFin, did not turn on the company. It banned investors from betting against Wirecard's shares for two months, the first time it had ever done that for a single stock. And prosecutors in Munich opened an investigation into a Financial Times journalist, after a criminal complaint. The newspaper rejected the allegations as baseless and false.

> `short_ban` [source_located] Reuters (18 Feb 2019): BaFin banned the taking or increasing of short positions in Wirecard until April 18, a first for an individual stock; Munich prosecutors said they were investigating a Financial Times journalist in response to a criminal complaint; the FT rejected allegations as 'baseless and false'. Irish Times (2021): the regulator also filed complaints against two FT journalists. (sources: reuters_short, irish_2021)

## m3_tpa - The money that wasn't there

An important part of Wirecard's business ran through so-called third-party partners: other companies in Asia and the Middle East that processed payments for Wirecard's customers. Wirecard booked the revenue. And the cash from that business, it said, sat in special trust accounts, held for it by a trustee.

(sources: wirecard_kpmg)

## m3_kpmg - The money that wasn't there

In April twenty twenty, a special investigation by the auditor KPMG was supposed to settle the question. It did not. According to Wirecard's own statement, not all the data needed to prove the revenues of that business could be obtained, because it was in the hands of the partners. Wirecard, for its part, said the investigation had found no incriminating evidence of balance sheet forgery.

> `kpmg` [source_located] Wirecard release (28 April 2020): KPMG's special investigation found that not all data could be obtained that would have been required to prove the revenues of the third-party acquiring business, because the data were primarily in the control of third parties; the company said incriminating evidence for the allegations of balance sheet forgery had not been identified. (sources: wirecard_kpmg)  

## m3_ey - The money that wasn't there

Then, on June eighteenth, twenty twenty, Wirecard's regular auditor, EY, refused to sign the accounts. Cash worth about 2.2 billion dollars could not be confirmed. There were indications, the company said, that a trustee had tried to deceive the auditor. That day, the shares fell by as much as 71 percent.

> `missing` [source_located] Irish Times (18 June 2020): EY refused to sign Wirecard's annual accounts due to a missing €1.9 billion; shares plunged as much as 71 per cent; a trustee had sought to 'deceive the auditor'. Al Jazeera (22 June 2020): €1.9bn ($2.13bn), about a quarter of its assets; the board said there was a 'prevailing likelihood' that the trust account balances 'do not exist'; BPI and BDO said the documents were false and Wirecard was not a client; the Philippine central bank said the money did not appear to have entered the country; CEO Markus Braun quit on Friday 19 June. (sources: irish_2020, aljazeera_2020, bbk_rates)  

## m3_banks - The money that wasn't there

The search ended in the Philippines, where the money was supposed to be held in two banks. Both said the documents were false, and that Wirecard was not even a client. The Philippine central bank said the money did not seem to have entered the country at all. Four days later, Wirecard admitted it: the money most likely did not exist. That was about a quarter of everything the company said it owned.

## m4_week - The collapse

Very fast. On June nineteenth, the chief executive, Markus Braun, resigned. On June twenty-fifth, Wirecard filed for insolvency. It became the first company in the DAX ever to go out of business, leaving creditors owed nearly 4 billion dollars. A company once valued at 28 billion dollars was gone. Two years earlier, it had pushed one of Germany's biggest banks out of the DAX. Now it had fallen out of existence.

## m4_marsalek - The collapse

And one man vanished. Jan Marsalek, Wirecard's chief operating officer, is wanted by German authorities. His whereabouts are unknown, but he is believed to be in Russia. In twenty twenty-five, a court in London convicted members of a spy ring working for Russia. According to British prosecutors, its leader reported to Marsalek. Between twenty twenty and twenty twenty-three, prosecutors said, the group gathered information about people and places of interest to the Russian state.

> `marsalek` [source_located] Reuters (Mar 2025): former COO Jan Marsalek, an Austrian national, is wanted by German authorities; his whereabouts are unknown but he is believed to be in Russia; three Bulgarians were convicted in London of spying for Russia in a unit run by Marsalek. CPS: between August 2020 and February 2023 the group gathered information about individuals and locations of interest to the Russian state; Orlin Roussev reported to Marsalek and was jailed for 10 years and eight months on 12 May 2025. (sources: reuters_spies, cps_spies)  

## m4_trial - The collapse

Markus Braun went on trial in Munich in December 2022, accused of commercial gang fraud, false accounting and breach of trust. If convicted, he faces up to 15 years in prison. He rejects all the charges, and has blamed Marsalek. The trial has gone on for years: the court set new hearing dates until the end of twenty twenty-six. Until there is a verdict, he is presumed innocent.

> `trial` [source_located] LTO (Aug 2025): the criminal trial of Markus Braun began on 8 December 2022 at the Munich Regional Court, on charges of commercial gang fraud, false accounting and breach of trust, with up to 15 years in prison if convicted. eNCA/AFP (Feb 2023): Braun said 'I reject all the charges' and blamed Marsalek. Bavarian judiciary (May 2026): further hearing dates were set up to 23 December 2026, with no conclusion about the end of the trial. (sources: lto_2025, enca_2023, bavaria_2026)

## m5_auditor - Who failed

First, the auditor. EY had signed off Wirecard's accounts without objections for more than a decade. In twenty twenty-three, the German audit watchdog banned it from taking on new listed audit clients in Germany for two years, and fined the firm and several current and former employees.

> `ey_ban` [source_located] Irish Times / FT (3 April 2023): EY was banned from taking on listed companies as new audit clients in Germany for two years; the firm and five current and former employees were fined up to €500,000 each; Wirecard had received unqualified audits from EY for more than a decade. (sources: irish_2023)  

## m5_regulator - Who failed

Then, the regulator. In January 2021, the head of BaFin, Felix Hufeld, left his post. The finance minister at the time, Olaf Scholz, said it would allow a fresh start at an organisation that had overlooked Germany's greatest postwar accounting fraud. His deputy in charge of securities supervision left too.

> `hufeld` [source_located] Irish Times (29 Jan 2021): BaFin president Felix Hufeld departed; finance minister Olaf Scholz called it a mutual decision to allow a fresh start at an organisation that 'overlooked Germany's greatest postwar accounting fraud'; deputy Elisabeth Roegele (securities supervision) also left. (sources: irish_2021)  

## m5_draw - Who failed

Let me draw the safety net that was supposed to work. The company reports its numbers. The auditor checks them. The regulator watches the market. Journalists and investors ask hard questions. At Wirecard, the last line, the critics, was the one that worked. The official checks, the ones everyone trusted, came too late.

## m5_lesson - Who failed

The lesson is not only about one company. When a story is exciting, a national champion, a tech success, a rising share price, people want to believe it. And the questions that could ruin the story are the ones nobody wants to ask. Every check in the system assumed that someone else had already checked. And for years, the people who did ask were treated as the problem.

## payoff - The lesson

So, the two billion dollars that never existed were not stolen from a vault. They were never in one. They lived in documents, in trust and in confidence, until someone finally asked to see them. And when they looked, there was nothing there. The most expensive question in this story was also the simplest one: can I see the money?
