# America's $1 Trillion of Gold Is Booked at $11 Billion

_The United States holds about eight thousand tonnes of gold, and its own books value it at about eleven billion dollars. At market prices it was worth about one trillion. Why a 1973 price tag survives, and what would happen if it changed._

## m1_deep - The pile

Almost all of it sits in what the Mint calls deep storage, sealed vaults that are not touched for daily work. The Treasury's inspector general notes that the Mint did not use any of that deep storage gold to make coins in twenty twenty-four or twenty twenty-five. The gold is not inventory. It is a reserve that simply sits there, year after year.

## m1_owner - The pile

Here is a detail that surprises a lot of people. The Federal Reserve, America's central bank, does not own any of this gold. In its own words, the Federal Reserve does not own gold. The owner is the Treasury. The Fed only holds a small part of it for safekeeping.

> `certs` [source_located] Fed FAQ: 'The Federal Reserve does not own gold.' The Gold Reserve Act of 1934 required the Fed to transfer its gold to Treasury in exchange for gold certificates, 'denominated in U.S. dollars' and not redeemable in gold. CRS: Treasury 'monetized the gold by issuing gold certificates to the Fed', which 'credited the Treasury's General Account'; certificates must be less than or equal to book value. ## m2_history - The price tag

For a long time, the dollar was defined in gold. From nineteen hundred, an ounce was worth twenty dollars and sixty-seven cents. In nineteen thirty-four, the government raised it to thirty-five dollars an ounce. That one change cut the gold value of the dollar to about fifty-nine percent of what it had been.

## m2_law - The price tag

Then came two laws in quick succession. In March nineteen seventy-two, Congress set the price at thirty-eight dollars. And in September nineteen seventy-three, it raised it one last time, to forty-two and two-ninths dollars. That is forty-two dollars and twenty-two cents an ounce. The same law also started to end the ban on Americans owning gold.

## m5_catch - Rewriting the price

Here is the catch. Revaluing the gold does not create a single new ounce. It changes a number on a page, and lets the government turn that number into fresh money at the central bank. Supporters see a hidden asset. Critics see printing money with extra steps. Either way, the gold in Kentucky stays exactly where it is.

## payoff - The lesson

So why is America's trillion dollars of gold booked at eleven billion? Because a price written into law in nineteen seventy-three still sets how much money that gold can be turned into. The gold is real, it is audited, and it barely moves. The only thing that is out of date is the price tag. And whoever gets to rewrite it gets to create a lot of money.
