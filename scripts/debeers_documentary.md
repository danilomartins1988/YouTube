# How One Company Made Diamonds a Symbol of Love

_For most of the twentieth century, one company controlled most of the world's rough diamonds, and an advertising line from nineteen forty-seven taught the world that a diamond means forever. How De Beers built the most successful cartel and slogan in history, and why it is now being sold for a fraction of what it was worth._

## h_stakes - Four words

The answer starts with one company, De Beers. For most of the last century, it controlled seventy-five to eighty percent of the world's rough diamonds. And in nineteen forty-seven, its advertising agency came up with four words that changed weddings forever. A diamond is forever. Today, the company that made diamonds priceless is being sold off, at a price that would have seemed impossible not long ago.

## m1_kimberley - The cartel

In the early eighteen seventies, huge diamond mines were found around Kimberley, in South Africa. Suddenly there were so many diamonds that prices swung wildly. Two rivals, Cecil Rhodes and Barney Barnato, fought for control of the mines. In eighteen eighty-eight, Rhodes won, and De Beers Consolidated Mines was born. Both men agreed on one thing. Controlling how many diamonds came out of the ground was the only way to keep prices stable.

> `syndicate` [source_located] GIA: in 1888 De Beers Consolidated Mines took control of the Kimberley mines after a battle between Cecil Rhodes and Barney Barnato; 'Rhodes and Barnato both believed that controlling production was essential to stabilizing prices'; De Beers signed a contract with 10 London distributors to buy all its production (the London Syndicate); Ernest Oppenheimer later directed all rough sales through the Diamond Trading Company; prices 'were adjusted upward when market conditions warranted, but never downward'; in slack periods it 'would stockpile rough diamonds or impose production quotas'. (sources: gia)  

## m1_syndicate - The cartel

So De Beers signed a deal with ten diamond dealers in London to buy all of its production. Later, under Ernest Oppenheimer, all rough sales went through a single selling company. It sorted the stones into thousands of categories and sold them at regular sales, called sights, to a small circle of approved buyers, at prices it set.

> `syndicate` [source_located] GIA: in 1888 De Beers Consolidated Mines took control of the Kimberley mines after a battle between Cecil Rhodes and Barney Barnato; 'Rhodes and Barnato both believed that controlling production was essential to stabilizing prices'; De Beers signed a contract with 10 London distributors to buy all its production (the London Syndicate); Ernest Oppenheimer later directed all rough sales through the Diamond Trading Company; prices 'were adjusted upward when market conditions warranted, but never downward'; in slack periods it 'would stockpile rough diamonds or impose production quotas'. (sources: gia)  

## m1_anglo - The cartel

The syndicate nearly collapsed twice, in the financial crash of nineteen oh seven and during the First World War. The man who rebuilt it, Ernest Oppenheimer, did it through another company. With American backers, he created a mining firm called Anglo American. By nineteen twenty-nine, he had used it to take over both the London syndicate and De Beers itself. Remember that name. Almost a century later, Anglo American would be the one trying to sell De Beers.

> `anglo_origin` [source_located] GIA: 'The Syndicate nearly collapsed during the financial crash of 1907 and again during World War I'; Oppenheimer 'gained backers from the United States to take over the newly discovered coastal mines' of South-West Africa; 'That venture was named the Anglo American Corporation. Oppenheimer eventually leveraged Anglo American's highly profitable operations to take over both the Syndicate and De Beers by 1929'. (sources: gia)  

## m1_draw - The cartel

Let me draw how the cartel worked. Here are the mines, De Beers' own and others around the world. All their stones flow into one box, the selling company. From there, they go out to a small circle of approved buyers. And here is the trick. When demand was weak, the box simply kept the diamonds, or told the mines to dig less. So prices went up when times were good, and never came down.

## m2_1938 - A diamond is forever

In September nineteen thirty-eight, with war coming in Europe, diamond prices were falling. Harry Oppenheimer, the son of De Beers' chairman, decided the company's future lay in America. He traveled to Philadelphia, to an advertising agency called N W Ayer and Son. For almost a decade, the agency draped diamonds on Hollywood stars. But it still had no line that stuck.

## m2_gerety - A diamond is forever

Then, in nineteen forty-seven, a young copywriter named Frances Gerety, according to Adweek, wrote four words under a photo of a honeymooning couple. A diamond is forever. De Beers adopted the line the next year, and has used it ever since.

## m2_meaning - A diamond is forever

The genius was in what the line connected. Not beauty, not wealth, but love that lasts. A diamond stopped being a gem and became a promise. And since grooms make up about nine out of ten diamond buyers, the ads spoke straight to them, with lines about how the stone reflects the greatness of your love.

## m2_price - A diamond is forever

The ads were not shy about money either. A De Beers advertisement from nineteen fifty-seven listed a one carat diamond at six hundred to twelve hundred and ten dollars. At the time, the average American man took home about four hundred and seventeen dollars a month. The message was clear. Real love costs months of pay.

## m3_usa - Cracks

There was always one awkward fact. America, the market De Beers worked so hard to win, had antitrust laws. In nineteen ninety-four, the US government indicted the company for conspiring to fix the prices of industrial diamonds. For years, the case went nowhere, because the court had no hold over De Beers. Only in two thousand four did a De Beers company finally plead guilty, and pay a fine of ten million dollars.

> `doj` [source_located] US DOJ (13 Jul 2004): De Beers Centenary AG pleaded guilty and was sentenced to pay a $10 million criminal fine for conspiring to fix prices of industrial diamonds; the plea resolved a 1994 indictment; the company had not been tried because the court lacked jurisdiction until it consented. (sources: doj_2004)  

## m3_soviet - Cracks

Meanwhile, the walls around supply were falling. When the Soviet Union broke up in the early nineteen nineties, Moscow lost its tight grip on the country's diamond mines. In nineteen ninety-eight, Canada opened its first diamond mine, and its owners sold most of the stones themselves. More and more diamonds were reaching the market without passing through De Beers.

> `change` [source_located] GIA: the DTC's control began to wane in the early 1990s after the breakup of the Soviet Union; new Canadian mines (Ekati, 1998) sold most output themselves; De Beers 'sold its diamond stockpile and curtailed its market custodianship by declining to limit sales during slower demand periods'; it agreed to phase out marketing Russia's production over seven years; rough sales moved increasingly to tenders and auctions, and since 2007 rough prices became 'extremely volatile'. (sources: gia)  

## m3_stockpile - Cracks

So De Beers changed strategy. It sold off its great stockpile of rough diamonds, and stopped holding back supply when demand was weak. It agreed to phase out selling Russia's diamonds. By two thousand three, its share of the market had fallen to sixty-five percent. And as more stones were sold at auction, rough diamond prices became, in GIA's words, extremely volatile.

> `change` [source_located] GIA: the DTC's control began to wane in the early 1990s after the breakup of the Soviet Union; new Canadian mines (Ekati, 1998) sold most output themselves; De Beers 'sold its diamond stockpile and curtailed its market custodianship by declining to limit sales during slower demand periods'; it agreed to phase out marketing Russia's production over seven years; rough sales moved increasingly to tenders and auctions, and since 2007 rough prices became 'extremely volatile'. ## m4_lab - Diamonds from a machine

Diamonds can now be grown in factories, with the same carbon and the same sparkle. In twenty eighteen, De Beers itself launched a lab-grown jewellery brand, called Lightbox, at eight hundred dollars per carat. Its idea was to show that lab diamonds were a cheap, different product, not a rival to the real thing.

> `lab` [source_located] De Beers Group press release (9 May 2025, via Media OutReach Newswire / European Business Magazine): it intends to close its lab-grown diamond jewellery brand Lightbox; 'The business was launched with transparent linear pricing of $800 per carat. Since then, LGD prices in the jewellery sector have fallen 90% at wholesale'. (sources: debeers_2025)  

## m4_cook - Diamonds from a machine

At first, lab diamonds were sold almost as luxury goods. As De Beers' chief executive Al Cook later put it, back in twenty eighteen, synthetics were selling for thousands of dollars, almost as much as natural diamonds. Lightbox was meant to show they deserved a much lower price.

> `cook` [source_located] National Jeweler (12 May 2025), De Beers CEO Al Cook: 'Back in 2018, when we started Lightbox, synthetics were selling for thousands of dollars, almost as much as natural diamonds.' (sources: nj_2025)

## m4_collapse - Diamonds from a machine

Then the price of lab diamonds collapsed. According to De Beers, wholesale prices for lab-grown jewellery diamonds fell by ninety percent. In twenty twenty-five, the company announced it would close Lightbox altogether. Factories could simply make more, and the value of the stone started to look like the cost of the machine that made it.

## m5_2011 - For sale

In twenty eleven, the mining group Anglo American paid the Oppenheimer family five point one billion dollars for forty percent of De Beers. That put a value of almost thirteen billion dollars on the whole company. The family that had run De Beers for generations stepped aside.

> `oppenheimer_sale` [source_located] Anglo American (4 Nov 2011): agreed to acquire the Oppenheimer family's 40% interest in De Beers for a total cash consideration of US$5.1 billion (implying about $12.75 billion for 100%). (sources: anglo_2011)  

## m5_writedown - For sale

Fifteen years later, the story was very different. In its results for twenty twenty-five, Anglo American booked an impairment of two point three billion dollars on De Beers, as diamond markets weakened. It is now trying to sell the business.

> `writedown` [source_located] Anglo American (20 Feb 2026, FY2025 results): 'Loss attributable to equity shareholders of $3.7 billion – including a pre-tax impairment of $2.3 billion relating to De Beers'; it was 'progressing the separation of De Beers'. Anglo (30 Jul 2026): 'advancing the sale process for De Beers… to minimise the impact from challenging diamond markets'. (sources: anglo_2025, anglo_2026)  

## m5_sale - For sale

In July twenty twenty-six, Bloomberg reported that Anglo was in advanced talks to sell its stake to a group led by a former De Beers chief executive, for about one billion dollars. Anglo did not confirm the figure, and said it was not exclusive with any buyer. But even as a rough guide, the gap is enormous. Almost thirteen billion dollars, then. Perhaps one billion, now.

> `sale` [source_located] JCK (30 Jul 2026): Bloomberg reported Anglo American was in advanced talks to sell its majority stake in De Beers to a consortium led by former De Beers CEO Gareth Penny for $1 billion; CEO Duncan Wanblad: 'We are not exclusive with anybody at this particular stage'; he hoped to sign by year-end. (sources: jck_2026)

## payoff - The lesson

So what is the lesson of De Beers? That a price is not only about how rare something is. It is about who controls the supply, and what story people believe. De Beers mastered both for a century. It held the stones back, and it gave them a meaning. The slogan still works. But a story can only hold a price up for as long as people stop asking what the thing would cost without it.
