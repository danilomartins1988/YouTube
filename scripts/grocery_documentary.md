## m2_eggs - The price shock

Eggs became the symbol. A dozen large eggs cost about a dollar forty-six in January twenty twenty. In March twenty twenty-five, the average price hit six dollars and twenty-three cents. The main reason, according to the Department of Agriculture, was an outbreak of bird flu that began in twenty twenty-two and hit egg farms again and again.

## m4_shrink - The profit debate

There is also a price rise that does not look like one. When a package gets smaller but the price stays the same, the Labor Department counts the price per ounce. In its own example, a sixty-four ounce carton cut to sixty ounces at the same price counts as a six point seven percent price increase. Shrinkflation is in the numbers. It is just hidden on the label.

> `shrink` [source_located] BLS: CPI uses 'An effective price per standard size, usually a price per ounce'; example: a 64-ounce carton reduced to 60 ounces at the same price is recorded as a 6.7 percent price increase. (sources: blsshrink)

## m5_merger - Fewer, bigger players

Supermarkets tried to get bigger too. Kroger planned to buy its rival Albertsons for twenty-four point six billion dollars. The combined company would have had more than five thousand stores. The FTC sued to block it. In December twenty twenty-four, a federal court stopped the deal, and both companies walked away. Albertsons is now suing Kroger over the breakup.

> `merger` [source_located] FTC (Feb 26, 2024): challenged 'Kroger Company's $24.6 billion acquisition of the Albertsons Companies, Inc.'; combined firm 'more than 5,000 stores'. Kroger 10-K: 'On December 10, 2024 ... the court issued a preliminary injunction' (D. Or., 3:24-cv-00347-AN); a Washington state court issued a permanent injunction the same day. Albertsons terminated the merger agreement on December 10, 2024 and sued Kroger; Kroger sent its own termination notice December 11, 2024; litigation ongoing as of the 10-Ks. (sources: ftcmerge, kroger, aci)  

## m5_slot - Fewer, bigger players

Then there is the shelf itself. Stores often charge food makers for space, called slotting fees. In a study of a few product categories, the FTC found the average fee per item, for one retailer in one city, ranged from two thousand three hundred and thirteen dollars to twenty-one thousand seven hundred and sixty-eight dollars. That study is from two thousand three, but the practice is still around.

> `slot` [source_located] FTC (2003): 'the average amount of slotting allowances (per item, per retailer, per metropolitan area) for all five categories combined ranged from $2,313 to $21,768, depending on the particular retailer and metropolitan area.' Small, non-representative case studies. PLMA (Jan 20, 2026, Circana data): store brands $282.8bn in 2025; dollar share 21.3% (19.1% in 2021). (sources: ftcslot, plma)  

## m5_brands - Fewer, bigger players

And the stores have a weapon of their own. Store brands. According to the private label industry's trade group, they made up twenty-one point three percent of grocery dollar sales in twenty twenty-five. When the store makes its own cereal, it competes with the brands on its own shelves.

## payoff - The lesson

So what is the hidden business behind your grocery bill? It is not one villain. The farmer gets about twelve cents. The store keeps a couple of pennies. Most of your money pays for everything in between, the processing, the trucks, the shelves and the people. Some of the biggest jumps came when something in that long chain broke, like a flock of hens or a herd of cattle. The real question is how much power sits in the narrow parts of the chain.
