# Why Switzerland Is So Rich

_A small, landlocked country with one of the highest outputs per person in the world. Stability, money, specialisation and skills - and the hidden costs that come with Swiss success._

## act2_card - Stability




## stab_timeline - Stability

The first force is stability, and it is old. In 1815, at the end of the Napoleonic wars, Europe's great powers formally recognised Swiss neutrality. Then, In 1848, a new constitution turned a loose group of cantons into a federal state, with a parliament, a government and a federal court.

> `neutrality_1815` [source_located] Europe's great powers formally recognised Swiss neutrality in 1815 (Congress of Vienna and the Second Treaty of Paris). (sources: admin_neutrality, eda_neutrality)  
> `constitution_1848` [source_located] The Federal Constitution of 12 September 1848 established the Swiss federal state, turning a group of cantons into a federal state with new federal authorities. (sources: parlament_constitution, bar_new_authorities)  
> "End of the Napoleonic wars" places 1815 historically (Congress of Vienna); verify phrasing against admin.ch.  

## stab_eu - Stability

That independent streak never went away. Switzerland is not a member of the European Union. Instead, it manages its relationship with the bloc through a web of bilateral agreements, negotiated sector by sector.

> `eu_bilateral` [source_located] Switzerland is not a member of the European Union; it pursues its European policy through bilateral sectoral agreements. (sources: eda_bilateral)  

## stab_meaning - Stability

Why does this matter for money? Because capital hates surprises. A country where rules change slowly, and in public, is a country where a factory, a laboratory or a savings account can plan for decades. Predictability is hard to measure, but it may be the most valuable thing Switzerland has ever produced.

## act3_card - Money




## money_1934 - Money

Stability attracted money, and Switzerland learned to protect it. In 1934, a new federal banking law made it a criminal offence for bankers to reveal their clients' secrets. Before that, confidentiality had been a matter of civil law. Now, breaking it could mean prosecution.

> `banking_act_1934` [source_located] The 1934 Federal Act on Banks and Savings Banks (in force 1 March 1935) made breaching banking secrecy a criminal offence (Article 47); before that, banking secrecy was protected by civil law. (sources: iheid_banking_secrecy, goldblum_secrecy)  

## money_pressure - Money

For decades, discretion became part of the Swiss brand. But secrecy that protects privacy can also protect people hiding money from their own governments, and pressure from abroad kept growing.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> General interpretation; no specific case or figure is asserted.  

## money_aeoi - Money

The turning point came quietly. In 2018, under a global standard for tax transparency, Switzerland automatically shared financial account information with other countries for the first time. That first exchange covered around two million accounts. The law on secrecy still exists at home, but foreign tax authorities now get the data.

> `aeoi_2018` [source_located] In 2018 Switzerland exchanged financial account information automatically with partner states for the first time, covering around 2 million financial accounts (FTA release, 5 October 2018), under the global AEOI standard. (sources: fta_aeoi_2018)  
> `banking_act_1934` [source_located] The 1934 Federal Act on Banks and Savings Banks (in force 1 March 1935) made breaching banking secrecy a criminal offence (Article 47); before that, banking secrecy was protected by civil law. (sources: iheid_banking_secrecy, goldblum_secrecy)  
> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> "The law on secrecy still exists at home" relies on Article 47 still being in force (secondary sources); verify on fedlex.  

## money_haven - Money

There is another way Switzerland attracts money, and it does not require a bank account at all. In times of crisis, investors look for places to park their wealth that are politically stable and unlikely to inflate it away. Economists call that a safe haven, and the Swiss franc is often described as one.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Definition of a safe haven and the common description of the franc as one; general framing, no statistic.  

## money_floor_2011 - Money

Safety has a side effect. When investors trust a country, they buy its currency, and a strong currency makes everything that country sells abroad more expensive. On September 6th, 2011, the Swiss National Bank drew a line in the sand. It would no longer tolerate a euro worth less than one franc twenty.

> `snb_floor_2011` [source_located] On 6 September 2011 the Swiss National Bank set a minimum exchange rate of CHF 1.20 per euro, saying it would no longer tolerate a EUR/CHF rate below it. (sources: ecb_snb_2011, bis_jordan_2015)  
> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> The first two sentences are general economics framing; the date and the 1.20 rate are sourced.  

## money_floor_2015 - Money

Defending that line meant buying foreign currency again and again. The central bank later said it could only have been held with interventions of rapidly increasing size. On January 15th, 2015, it abandoned the minimum rate with immediate effect. The franc jumped, and markets were caught off guard.

> `snb_floor_interventions` [source_located] According to the SNB (April 2015), the minimum exchange rate could only have been enforced through permanent currency interventions of rapidly increasing magnitude. (sources: snb_speech_2015_04)  
> `snb_floor_end_2015` [source_located] On 15 January 2015 the Swiss National Bank discontinued the minimum exchange rate of CHF 1.20 per euro with immediate effect and ceased the associated foreign currency purchases; the franc appreciated sharply and markets were surprised. (sources: bis_jordan_2015, swissinfo_floor_2015)  

## money_after - Money

The battle over the franc never really ended. In twenty twenty-two and twenty twenty-three, the central bank did the opposite: it sold foreign currency to strengthen the franc and make imports cheaper, to dampen inflation. In twenty twenty-three alone, it sold about 148 billion dollars worth. In twenty twenty-four, it was buying again, but only about 1.4 billion dollars.

> `snb_fx_2024` [source_located] For all of 2024 the SNB purchased CHF 1.2bn of foreign currency, compared with sales of CHF 132.9bn the previous year; in 2022 and 2023 it boosted the franc this way to dampen inflation. (sources: swi_snb_2024, fed_h10)  

## money_cs - Money

Even Swiss banking is not immune to crisis. In March 2023, Credit Suisse was taken over by its rival UBS, in a deal approved by the financial regulator and backed by the federal government and the central bank.

> `cs_ubs_2023` [source_located] On 19 March 2023 FINMA approved the takeover of Credit Suisse by UBS; the Swiss Confederation and the SNB supported the deal (federal guarantee for additional SNB liquidity assistance, emergency law). (sources: finma_cs_ubs, efd_cs_timeline)  

## act4_card - Specialisation




## spec_ring - Specialisation

But here is the twist. Look at what Switzerland physically ships abroad, and banks barely appear. In 2024, chemical and pharmaceutical products made up 52.7 percent of Swiss exports. More than half of everything the country sold abroad came from one industry.

> `pharma_share_2024` [source_located] Chemical and pharmaceutical products accounted for 52.7% of Swiss (goods) exports in 2024 (CHF 149,058 million). (sources: bazg_trade_2024, kmu_pharma_exports)  
> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> "Banks barely appear" = financial services are not goods exports, so they are not in goods trade statistics by definition.  

## spec_basel - Specialisation

The centre of gravity is Basel, a city on the Rhine, close to the borders with France and Germany. The drug makers Roche and Novartis both have their global headquarters here.

> `pharma_hq_basel` [source_located] Roche and Novartis both have their global headquarters in Basel. (sources: swissinfo_roche_hq, swissinfo_roche_rd)  
> Basel's location on the Rhine near France and Germany is visible on the Natural Earth map.  

## spec_why - Specialisation

Medicines are almost the perfect product for a small, landlocked, expensive country. They are light, they are valuable, and they are protected by patents and know-how that take years to build. Switzerland does not compete on what is cheap to make. It competes on what is hard to copy.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Interpretation by the channel; 'expensive' is supported by the price-level claim in act 6.  

## spec_watches - Specialisation

And the watches? They are real, and they are big business. In twenty twenty-four, Swiss watch exports were worth about 29.4 billion dollars, down 2.8 percent on the year, according to the watch industry's federation. It is the same lesson as medicine: small, light, expensive objects that are very hard to copy.

> `watches_2024` [source_located] Swiss watch exports reached CHF 25.9 billion in 2024, down 2.8% (FH statistics, via Keystone-SDA/swissinfo: 'which reached a volume of CHF25.9 billion'). (sources: swi_watches_2024, fed_h10)  

## act5_card - Skills




## skills_vet - Skills

Hard-to-copy products need people who are hard to replace. About two thirds of young people in Switzerland go into vocational education and training, usually apprenticeships that combine the classroom with real work inside a company. In many countries, that path is seen as a fallback. Here, it is the mainstream.

> `vet_share` [source_located] About two-thirds of young people in Switzerland enter vocational education and training (VET); the most common form is dual-track VET combining classroom instruction with workplace training. (sources: seri_vet)  
> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> "Seen as a fallback in many countries" is interpretation, not a cross-country statistic.  

## skills_gii - Skills

Skills feed ideas. In 2025, the World Intellectual Property Organization ranked Switzerland first out of 139 economies in its Global Innovation Index. Sweden, the United States, South Korea and Singapore completed the top five.

> `gii_2025_rank` [source_located] In the Global Innovation Index 2025, WIPO ranked Switzerland first of 139 economies. (sources: wipo_gii_2025, ige_gii_2025)  
> `gii_2025_top5` [source_located] GII 2025 top five: Switzerland, Sweden, United States, Republic of Korea, Singapore. (sources: wipo_gii_2025)  

## skills_jobs - Skills

Skills also keep people in work. In twenty twenty-four, the average unemployment rate in Switzerland was 2.4 percent, according to the economics ministry, SECO. A year earlier, at 2.0 percent, it had been at its lowest level since two thousand and one. And the jobs pull people in from across the borders: around 403,000 cross-border commuters, most of them living in France, Italy and Germany, were working in Switzerland at the end of September twenty twenty-four.

> `unemployment_2024` [source_located] SECO: the average unemployment rate for 2024 was 2.4%, 0.4 points higher than the previous year; at 2.0% the 2023 figure was the lowest since 2001. (sources: swi_unemployment_2024)  
> `crossborder_2024` [source_located] Around 403,000 people with a G permit (cross-border commuters) were working in Switzerland at the end of September 2024 (FSO); about 57% lived in France, 23% in Italy and about 16% in Germany. (sources: swi_crossborder_2024)  

## act6_card - The hidden costs




## cost_prices - The hidden costs

Now for the part that rarely makes the brochures. Success is expensive, literally. In 2024, Switzerland had the highest price level of all the countries compared by Eurostat, almost 60 percent above the EU average.

## cost_tariff - The hidden costs

Then, in twenty twenty-five, the risk of depending on a few big customers became very real. From August seventh, Swiss exports to the United States faced a tariff of 39 percent. The Swiss government called it unjustified and a heavy burden for exporters. In November, the two countries signed a non-binding memorandum: the extra tariff would fall to 15 percent, in line with the European Union, and Swiss companies planned 200 billion dollars of investment in the United States by the end of twenty twenty-eight.

> `tariff_2025` [source_located] Since 7 August 2025, Swiss exports to the US were subject to a 39% tariff (SME Portal, 11.08.2025). On 14 November 2025 Switzerland and Liechtenstein signed a non-binding memorandum of understanding; the US would reduce the country-specific additional tariff to 15%, in line with the EU; Swiss companies plan USD 200bn of direct investment in the US by the end of 2028. (sources: kmu_tariff_2025, swi_tariff15_2025)

## act7_card - Conclusion




## close - Conclusion

So, why is Switzerland so rich? Not because of what lies under its ground, but because of what it built on top of it. Stability that investors trust, earned over two centuries and defended, sometimes at great cost. A financial system that had to reinvent itself when secrecy stopped being acceptable. Specialisation in products that are hard to copy. And skills that keep them that way. None of these forces is guaranteed. A strong currency, high prices and a dependence on a few giants are the price of the model. The question for the next decade is whether a small country can keep it running in a world of bigger, louder powers. Thanks for watching. This is The Hidden Economics.
