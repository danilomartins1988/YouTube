# The Metal Trade That Broke a Billion-Dollar Market

_On the morning of March eighth, twenty twenty-two, the price of nickel on the London Metal Exchange doubled in a few hours and passed one hundred thousand dollars a tonne. The exchange stopped trading and erased thirteen billion dollars of deals. What happened that night, why the exchange tore up the trades, and who paid for it._

## h_stakes - The night nickel went crazy

In March twenty twenty-two, it happened in London. The price of nickel, a metal used in stainless steel and batteries, shot past one hundred and one thousand three hundred and sixty-five dollars a tonne. The London Metal Exchange suspended trading. Then it cancelled every trade made that morning, worth about thirteen point four billion dollars. Some traders lost fortunes they thought they had made. Others were saved from ruin.

## m1_lme - A market that never sleeps

For most of the metals in your phone, your car or your kitchen sink, a key price is set in London. The London Metal Exchange trades contracts to deliver copper, aluminium, nickel and other metals, usually three months ahead. Miners, factories and traders use those contracts to protect themselves against price swings. And the market runs almost around the clock, with seven of its nineteen trading hours handled overnight from Hong Kong.

> `blind` [source_located] FCA: 'seven of LME's nineteen daily trading hours were managed overnight in Hong Kong'; 'The LME, at that stage, could not see certain positions in the OTC market that it later found were a material cause of the prices rises'; staff speculated the rise was short covering. High Court: the claimants argued 'the major factor was the short positions built up by entities within the Tsingshan Holding Group… a Chinese industrial user of nickel', acquired 'not on the LME but on the over-the-counter (OTC) market, making it less visible to the LME'. (sources: fca, highcourt)  

## m1_draw - A market that never sleeps

Let me draw how a short position works. A company that will sell nickel in the future agrees today to deliver it at a fixed price. That is a short. If the price falls, it wins. If the price rises, it loses, and the exchange asks it to put up more cash every day to cover the loss. That cash is called margin. And if the price rises fast enough, the margin calls can grow faster than anyone can pay.

> `margin` [source_located] FCA: total margin calls on Friday 4 March were $3.5bn, a record; the Monday 7 March morning call set a new record of $5.1bn; by 2.00pm on 7 March LME Clear had called more than $7.4bn; on 8 March the morning's additional variation margin calls for 3M nickel 'totalled $19.7bn'; 'Had margin calls been made… based on the $80,000 figure, they would have totalled some $20bn and been likely to trigger multiple LME Member defaults'. (sources: fca)

## m1_short - A market that never sleeps

In early twenty twenty-two, there was a very large short in nickel. According to the claimants in the later court case, it was built up by companies within Tsingshan, a Chinese industrial user of nickel. And much of it was held not on the exchange itself, but in private deals between banks and traders, called the over the counter market. The exchange could not see it.

## m3_calls - Tearing up the trades

The numbers on the table were frightening. That morning's margin calls on nickel totalled nineteen point seven billion dollars. According to the regulator, calls on that scale were likely to push several members of the exchange into default. And when a member defaults, the losses can spread through the clearing house to everyone else.

> `margin` [source_located] FCA: total margin calls on Friday 4 March were $3.5bn, a record; the Monday 7 March morning call set a new record of $5.1bn; by 2.00pm on 7 March LME Clear had called more than $7.4bn; on 8 March the morning's additional variation margin calls for 3M nickel 'totalled $19.7bn'; 'Had margin calls been made… based on the $80,000 figure, they would have totalled some $20bn and been likely to trigger multiple LME Member defaults'. (sources: fca)  

## m3_suspend - Tearing up the trades

At eight fifteen, the exchange suspended nickel trading. Later that day, it went further. It cancelled every nickel trade made since midnight, worth about thirteen point four billion dollars. The market stayed shut for more than a week, and reopened on March sixteenth. Night trading from Asia did not return for a whole year.

> `cancel` [source_located] FCA: the nickel market was suspended from 8.15am on 8 March to 8.00am on 16 March 2022; the LME cancelled all trades executed on or after 00:00 UK time on 8 March 2022; 'The notional value of the cancelled trades was $13.373bn'; nickel did not re-open in Asian trading hours until March 2023. (sources: fca)  

## m3_rewind - Tearing up the trades

The exchange chose midnight as the cut-off, not the moment the brakes came off. Everything traded from the start of the day was wiped out, as if the morning had never happened. And the billions in margin that the morning's prices had created simply disappeared with it.

## m3_winners - Tearing up the trades

Think about what that meant. A trader who had sold nickel at one hundred thousand dollars that morning suddenly had no trade at all. And a company caught short, facing billions in losses, was suddenly spared the worst prices of the day. The cancellation saved the market from a chain of defaults. But it also took real profits away from people who had done nothing wrong.

## m4_claims - The court

The American hedge fund Elliott said the cancellation had cost it about four hundred and fifty-six million dollars in profits. The trading firm Jane Street put its loss at about fifteen million dollars. They argued that the market had not been disorderly at all, that the price was simply the result of a squeeze on traders like Tsingshan, and that the exchange had acted unlawfully.

## m4_rulings - The court

In November twenty twenty-three, the High Court in London disagreed, and dismissed the case. The traders had signed up to the exchange's rules, and those rules gave it the power to cancel trades. In October twenty twenty-four, the Court of Appeal upheld that decision. The cancellation, the courts found, had been lawful.

The exchange chose the loser it thought the market could survive.

## m5_fca - The verdict

In March twenty twenty-five, Britain's Financial Conduct Authority fined the London Metal Exchange about twelve million dollars. It found that the exchange's policies, controls and training had not prepared it for extreme volatility.

> `fine` [source_located] FCA Final Notice (19 Mar 2025): a financial penalty of £9,245,900 on the LME, after a 30% settlement discount; the LME's policies, controls and training 'had not sufficiently catered for the risks to market orderliness presented by extreme volatility'. At the H.10 rate of 19 Mar 2025 (1.2969 $/£), ≈ $12.0 million. (sources: fca, fred_gbp)  

## m5_philosophy - The verdict

The regulator also described the culture behind the decisions. The prevailing philosophy at the exchange, it said, was that trading should not be restricted even in times of extreme volatility, unless the market became disorderly. Its price bands had been designed mainly to catch mistakes, a fat finger on a keyboard or a rogue computer program. Not a real squeeze, in the middle of the night, with billions at stake.

## m5_failures - The verdict

The notice describes an exchange that could not see the risk building. Its overnight team in Hong Kong had switched off the price bands without consulting senior management, and had never been trained to escalate such a decision. And it could not see the private positions that, it later found, were a material cause of the price rise.

## payoff - The lesson

So what does the nickel crisis teach about markets? That a price is only as real as the system behind it. When enough people bet one way, and the bets are hidden from view, a market can break in a few hours. The exchange saved itself by rewriting the morning. That kept the system standing. But it also showed that, in a real crisis, the rules of the market can change while the game is being played.
