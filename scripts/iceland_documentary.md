## h_stakes - The week a country nearly went bankrupt

In October twenty oh eight, Iceland found out. Its three biggest banks collapsed in less than one week. According to the International Monetary Fund, the banking system had grown to almost nine times the size of the country's entire economy. The prime minister went on television and warned of national bankruptcy. And the British government froze an Icelandic bank's money using an anti-terrorism law.

Cambridge: the banks collapsed on 8 and 9 October; the bankruptcy of the three banks is the third largest in the world, after Lehman Brothers and Washington Mutual. (sources: imf_pr, cambridge)

## m1_fish - From fish to finance

For most of its modern history, Iceland lived mainly on two things. Fishing, and later aluminium. Its banks were small, and mostly owned by the state. Then, around the turn of the century, the government sold them off. A study from the University of Cambridge describes the sale as arbitrary and opaque, with the banks going to groups close to the parties in power.

> `growth` [source_located] Cambridge: Iceland had depended on fishing and aluminium; the banks were privatised (completed 1999-2002, 'arbitrary and opaque procedures'); 'By 2003, Icelandic banking began to grow at an extraordinary fast rate, aided by low world interest rates and free capital mobility'; the owners of all three banks 'also became the major borrowers from the banks'; Table 2: current account balance −20.7% of GDP in 2006. (sources: cambridge)  

## m1_boom - From fish to finance

From about twenty oh three, the three banks, Kaupthing, Landsbanki and Glitnir, began to grow at an extraordinary speed. Money was cheap around the world, and capital could flow across borders freely. The banks borrowed abroad, invested abroad, and lent to Iceland's own businessmen. In fact, the owners of the banks became some of their biggest borrowers.

> `growth` [source_located] Cambridge: Iceland had depended on fishing and aluminium; the banks were privatised (completed 1999-2002, 'arbitrary and opaque procedures'); 'By 2003, Icelandic banking began to grow at an extraordinary fast rate, aided by low world interest rates and free capital mobility'; the owners of all three banks 'also became the major borrowers from the banks'; Table 2: current account balance −20.7% of GDP in 2006. ## m2_icesave - Icesave

Because it paid more. In October twenty oh six, Landsbanki launched an internet savings account in Britain called Icesave. It offered some of the best interest rates available, and the deposits flooded in. It also took savers in the Netherlands. In the end, around three hundred and forty thousand British and Dutch savers had money in it.

> `icesave` [source_located] Cambridge: Landsbanki set up the internet bank Icesave in the UK in October 2006; it 'paid the best interest rates available to UK savers… and the deposits flooded in'. Irish Times (Mar 2010): around 340,000 British and Dutch savers had Icesave accounts. (sources: cambridge, irish_2010)

## m2_warning - Icesave

There were warning signs. Iceland was running huge deficits with the rest of the world, and its banks depended on borrowing that could vanish overnight. The Cambridge study notes that the banks did not hold the toxic mortgage-linked securities that sank others. Their weakness was simpler. They were too big, and they ran on borrowed money.

## m2_praise - Icesave

And yet, the experts were calm. In twenty oh seven, a report by respected international economists concluded, in its own words, that these were in fact very well run banks. Iceland's regulators seemed to agree. When the people paid to watch the banks were reassured, very few outsiders asked the obvious question. What if the money stops?

> `praise` [source_located] Cambridge (quoting the Portes et al. 2007 report on Iceland): 'Our view, which appears to be shared by the regulatory authorities (the [FME] and the CBI) is that these are in fact very well run banks.' Cambridge also notes Mishkin and Herbertsson (2006) judged the banks fundamentally sound. (sources: cambridge)

## m3_fall - Seven days in October

After the collapse of Lehman Brothers, banks everywhere stopped lending to each other. For Iceland's banks, that was fatal. They could not roll over their debts, and the country's central bank could not print the foreign money needed to rescue them. One after another, Glitnir, Landsbanki and Kaupthing fell, in less than one week. Together, they were about eighty-five percent of the country's banking system.

> `collapse` [source_located] IMF: the three main banks (Glitnir, Landsbanki and Kaupthing) 'collapsed within less than one week'; 'they accounted for about 85 percent of the banking system'; 'króna depreciated by more than 70 percent in the off-shore market'; 'equity market fell by 80 percent'. Cambridge: the banks collapsed on 8 and 9 October; the bankruptcy of the three banks is the third largest in the world, after Lehman Brothers and Washington Mutual. (sources: imf_pr, cambridge)  

## m3_speech - Seven days in October

On October sixth, Prime Minister Geir Haarde addressed the nation. There was, he said, a very real danger that the economy could be sucked with the banks into the whirlpool, and the result could be national bankruptcy. He announced an emergency bill, to be passed that same day. And he ended with three words. God bless Iceland.

> `speech` [source_located] Haarde, Address to the Nation (6 Oct 2008): 'There is a very real danger, fellow citizens, that the Icelandic economy, in the worst case, could be sucked with the banks into the whirlpool and the result could be national bankruptcy'; he would present a bill to parliament the same day; closing words: 'God bless Iceland.' (sources: haarde)  

## m3_scale - Seven days in October

The next day, October seventh, the government took control of the banks. Measured together, the failure of the three Icelandic banks was the third largest bankruptcy in the world, after Lehman Brothers and Washington Mutual.

> `recession` [source_located] Cambridge: Iceland's growth of 1.1 per cent in 2008 'the very next year declined to minus 6.6 per cent and the following year was minus 4.1 per cent, the worst recession of any of the OECD countries'; on 7 October 2008 the government was compelled to nationalise the three major banks; the bankruptcy of the three banks is the third largest in the world, after Lehman Brothers and Washington Mutual. (sources: cambridge)  
> Nationalisation and 'third largest' from the Cambridge paper.  

## m3_terror - Seven days in October

Two days later, Britain struck. At ten in the morning on October eighth, the British Treasury made an order freezing Landsbanki's assets in the country. The power it used came from a law of twenty oh one, the anti-terrorism, crime and security act. An anti-terrorism law, used against the bank of a friendly European country.

> `freeze` [source_located] The Landsbanki Freezing Order 2008 was made at 10.00 a.m. on 8 October 2008 by the UK Treasury under the Anti-terrorism, Crime and Security Act 2001, and came into force at 10.10 a.m. (sources: freeze)

## m4_krona - The bill

First, everyone holding Iceland's currency. According to the IMF, the currency fell by more than seventy percent in offshore trading, and the stock market fell by eighty percent. Even at official rates, the currency lost more than half of its value against the dollar in about a year. Imports became far more expensive, and inflation rose to over eighteen percent in twenty oh nine.

> `collapse` [source_located] IMF: the three main banks (Glitnir, Landsbanki and Kaupthing) 'collapsed within less than one week'; 'they accounted for about 85 percent of the banking system'; 'króna depreciated by more than 70 percent in the off-shore market'; 'equity market fell by 80 percent'. Cambridge: the banks collapsed on 8 and 9 October; the bankruptcy of the three banks is the third largest in the world, after Lehman Brothers and Washington Mutual. (sources: imf_pr, cambridge)  
> `krona` [source_located] OECD/FRED monthly average: about 60.7 krónur per US dollar in October 2007 and about 135.3 in November 2008, i.e. the krona lost more than half its dollar value onshore. Cambridge: inflation increased to over 18 per cent in 2009, largely because of the depreciation. (sources: fred_isk, cambridge)  

## m4_recession - The bill

Then came the recession. The economy shrank by minus six point six percent in twenty oh nine, and by another minus four point one percent the year after. According to the Cambridge study, it was the worst recession of any country in the OECD, the club of rich economies. Unemployment soared, and the weak currency pushed prices up.

> `recession` [source_located] Cambridge: Iceland's growth of 1.1 per cent in 2008 'the very next year declined to minus 6.6 per cent and the following year was minus 4.1 per cent, the worst recession of any of the OECD countries'; on 7 October 2008 the government was compelled to nationalise the three major banks; the bankruptcy of the three banks is the third largest in the world, after Lehman Brothers and Washington Mutual. (sources: cambridge)

## m4_imf - The bill

In November, the IMF approved a loan to Iceland of about two point one billion dollars, with other governments lending the rest of what was needed. In return, Iceland had to stabilize its currency and its public finances.

> `imf` [source_located] IMF (19 Nov 2008): the Executive Board approved a two-year SDR 1.4 billion (about US$2.1 billion) Stand-By Arrangement for Iceland, expected to fill about 42 percent of the 2008-2010 financing gap, the rest from official bilateral creditors. (sources: imf_pr)  

## m4_icesave - The bill

And then there was Icesave. Britain and the Netherlands paid back their own savers, and sent the bill to Iceland. Britain alone had paid out four and a half billion pounds, about seven point eight billion dollars at the time. The governments negotiated a deal for Iceland's taxpayers to repay it, with interest.

> `repaid` [source_located] HM Treasury (15 Jan 2016): 'Taxpayers paid £4.5 billion to UK depositors in Icesave'; the government received the final payment of £740 million from the Landsbanki estate and 'has now received £4.6 billion from the Landsbanki estate and litigation cases'. At the H.10 rate of 8 Oct 2008 (1.7310 $/£), £4.5bn ≈ $7.8bn. (sources: ukgov_2016, fred_gbp)  
> `votes` [source_located] Irish Times (7 Mar 2010): 'just over 93 per cent of voters said No' to a €3.8 billion plan to repay Britain and the Netherlands; compensation of £2.3bn to Britain and £1.1bn to the Netherlands paid to around 340,000 savers. Fondation Robert Schuman (Apr 2011): the new Icesave law was rejected by 58.9% No to 39.7% Yes. (sources: irish_2010, schuman_2011)  

## m4_vote - The bill

Icelanders said no. In a referendum in March twenty ten, just over ninety-three percent voted against the repayment deal. A new, softer deal went to a second vote in April twenty eleven. It was rejected again, by almost fifty-nine percent. Ordinary people refused to pay for the debts of private banks.

> `votes` [source_located] Irish Times (7 Mar 2010): 'just over 93 per cent of voters said No' to a €3.8 billion plan to repay Britain and the Netherlands; compensation of £2.3bn to Britain and £1.1bn to the Netherlands paid to around 340,000 savers. ## m5_efta - The verdicts

The case went to the EFTA Court, the court for the European countries outside the European Union that share its single market. On January twenty-eighth, twenty thirteen, it ruled. The claim against Iceland was dismissed. Iceland had not broken the rules by refusing to guarantee the savers with its own taxpayers' money.

> `efta` [source_located] EFTA Court, Case E-16/11 (28 Jan 2013): 'On those grounds, THE COURT hereby: 1. Dismisses the application.' Iceland had not failed to comply with the deposit-guarantee directive. (sources: efta)  

## m5_estate - The verdicts

The savers' money came back another way. Landsbanki's remaining assets were sold off over the following years, and the deposit claims had priority. In January twenty sixteen, the British Treasury announced it had received its final payment, and said it had now recovered all the taxpayer money used for Icesave.

> `repaid` [source_located] HM Treasury (15 Jan 2016): 'Taxpayers paid £4.5 billion to UK depositors in Icesave'; the government received the final payment of £740 million from the Landsbanki estate and 'has now received £4.6 billion from the Landsbanki estate and litigation cases'. At the H.10 rate of 8 Oct 2008 (1.7310 $/£), £4.5bn ≈ $7.8bn. (sources: ukgov_2016, fred_gbp)  

## m5_people - The verdicts

Iceland also did something few countries did after the crisis. It took people to court. In twenty twelve, former prime minister Geir Haarde was convicted on one charge, with no punishment, the first government leader prosecuted over the global banking crisis. In twenty fifteen, Iceland's Supreme Court sent the former head of Kaupthing to prison for five and a half years for market manipulation.

> `courts` [source_located] CBC/AP (23 Apr 2012): former prime minister Geir Haarde was convicted of one criminal charge, cleared on four others and faces no punishment; the first government leader to face criminal prosecution over the global banking crisis. Fox/AP (12 Feb 2015): Iceland's Supreme Court gave former Kaupthing CEO Hreidar Mar Sigurdsson 5½ years and former chairman Sigurdur Einarsson 4 years for market manipulation linked to a Qatari investor's purchase of 5.1 percent of Kaupthing shortly before its collapse. (sources: cbc_2012, fox_2015)  

## payoff - The lesson

So what is the lesson of Iceland? That a bank can grow bigger than the country that stands behind it. When that happens, the country stops controlling the bank, and the bank starts controlling the country. In the end, Iceland let its banks fail, and refused to make its taxpayers carry their foreign debts. Bigger countries in twenty oh eight made a different choice. Which one was right is still being argued today.
