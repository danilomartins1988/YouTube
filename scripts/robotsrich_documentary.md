## a4_card - What the evidence says




## a4_find - What the evidence says

So is AI already moving money from people like Dana to the owners? Let's separate what's been measured from what's been predicted. First, a finding. When a company gave its customer-support agents an AI assistant, they solved more problems per hour, and the newest, least experienced agents gained the most.

> `bryn` [source_located] Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (NBER w31161): AI assistance raised issues resolved per hour by 14% on average among 5,179 support agents, 34% for novice and low-skilled workers, minimal for experienced workers. (sources: bryn)  

## a4_denmark - What the evidence says

In Denmark, researchers matched chatbot use with official payroll records. Since ChatGPT arrived, they've found no measurable effect on pay or hours.

> `humlum` [source_located] Humlum & Vestergaard (NBER w33777, rev. 2026): in Denmark, precise null effects of AI chatbots on earnings and recorded hours, ruling out effects larger than 2% two years after ChatGPT's launch. (sources: humlum)  

## a4_young - What the evidence says

But in America, payroll data shows something to watch. Among the youngest workers in the jobs most exposed to AI, employment is almost a fifth below where it would otherwise be. Experienced workers show no such gap. The researchers call it an early warning, not proof.

> `canaries` [source_located] Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen (Stanford, rev. Aug 2026): employment of workers aged 22-25 in AI-exposed occupations stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers; no comparable gap for experienced workers; descriptive, not causal. (sources: canaries)  

## a4_robots - What the evidence says

Factory robots have a longer record. Economists found that American towns that added more robots saw fewer jobs and slightly lower pay.

> `robots` [source_located] Acemoglu & Restrepo (NBER w23285): 1990-2007, one more robot per thousand workers reduced the employment-to-population ratio by about 0.18-0.34 percentage points and wages by 0.25-0.5 percent. (sources: ar_robots)  

## a4_forecast - What the evidence says

Then there are forecasts. MIT economist Daron Acemoglu predicts AI will give productivity only a small lift over the next decade, and widen the gap between what capital earns and what work earns. That's a model, not a measurement. Forecasts differ widely.

> `acemoglu` [source_located] Acemoglu (NBER w32487), a forecast: AI adds no more than 0.66% to total factor productivity over 10 years (possibly under 0.53%); AI predicted to widen the gap between capital and labor income. (sources: acemoglu)

## a5_draw - We've been here before

Economic historian Robert Allen measured it. Let me draw his story. Output per worker kept climbing. Real wages stayed flat. The profit rate doubled. Historians call it Engels' pause, after Friedrich Engels, who described the factory workers of Manchester.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Allen (2009) abstract (allen): real wage stagnated while output per worker expanded; profit rate doubled; profit share rose.  

## a5_end - We've been here before

Then, around the middle of the eighteen hundreds, wages finally began to rise with productivity. The pause ended. But it lasted decades. For the workers who lived through it, eventually came too late.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Allen (2009): after mid-century real wages grew in line with productivity; factor shares stabilized.  

## a5_today - We've been here before

So are we living through a new Engels' pause? Nobody can say yet. Workers' share of business output is at a record low, but AI has barely reached most workplaces. What history does show is that the pause wasn't automatic, and neither was the catch-up. It depends on new jobs, new skills, and who gets to own the new machines.

## a6_card - The case against the doom story




## a6_newjobs - The case against the doom story

Now, the other side. First, new jobs. A team led by economist David Autor found that most Americans today work in job specialties that didn't exist in nineteen forty. Machines take over tasks. New kinds of work keep appearing.

> `newwork` [source_located] Autor, Chin, Salomons & Seegmiller (NBER w30389): the majority of current employment is in new job specialties introduced after 1940. (sources: autor_newwork)  

## a6_wages - The case against the doom story

Second, productivity can lift pay. When things get cheaper to make, people buy more. And workers who use AI well can become more valuable. Like Dana, who now runs the robot fleet.

> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Productivity and new-task effects (ar_framework); support-agent finding (bryn). Dana is fictional.  

## a6_degree - The case against the doom story

Third, skills still pay. A full-time worker with at least a bachelor's degree typically earns about three-quarters more than a high school graduate.

> `degree` [source_located] BLS CPS (FRED), 2026 Q3: median usual weekly earnings, full-time workers 25+: bachelor's degree and higher $1,752; high school graduates, no college $993. Ratio 1,752/993 = 1.76, i.e. about 76% more (our calculation). (sources: bls_edu)  

## a6_policy - The case against the doom story

And fourth, policy can change the split. Some want to spread ownership wider, with employee ownership, accounts for kids, or public funds like Alaska's oil dividend, which we covered in another film. Others prefer training, or changing how work and capital are taxed. Others say markets will sort it out. Each has trade-offs. This film takes no side.

## a7_card - What can you actually own?




## a7_now - What can you actually own?

So Dana asks the practical question. What can an ordinary person actually own? Quick note. This is education, not investment advice. We're not telling anyone what to buy. Most American families own at least some stock, often through a retirement account. But among lower-income families, fewer than one in three do.

> `scf` [source_located] Federal Reserve SCF 2025: 56% of families held stock directly or indirectly; 31% in the bottom half by usual income, 97% in the top decile; retirement accounts held by 54.9% of families. (sources: scf25)  

## a7_doors - What can you actually own?

Let me draw the doors. The most common is a workplace retirement account, like a four-oh-one-k. Inside, many people hold index funds, which own small slices of hundreds of companies at once. Some employers offer stock plans. Employee stock ownership plans alone cover millions of people.

> `esop` [source_located] NCEO (Jan 2026; plan year 2023): ESOPs cover 15.1 million participants (10.9 million active) in 6,609 plans. (sources: nceo)  

## a7_risk - What can you actually own?

But owning isn't free money. Stock prices can fall hard and take years to recover. Fees eat returns. And betting your savings on your own employer, or on a single hot company, piles risk on risk. Owning a slice of the machines means sharing their bad years too.

## a8_card - Who gets rich?




## a8_verdict - Who gets rich?

So, who gets rich when robots do the work? On the evidence so far, mostly the people who own them. The shareholders of the companies that build, sell and run the machines. And in America, that's a group where the richest tenth hold nearly the whole pile.

> `equities` [source_located] Federal Reserve DFA, 2026 Q2: the top 10% of households by wealth held 88.1% of household corporate equities and mutual fund shares (top 1% 50.9%; next 9% 37.2%; next 40% 11.4%; bottom 50% 0.6%). (sources: fed_dfa)  

## a8_but - Who gets rich?

But it doesn't have to stay that narrow. History says pay can catch up, after a delay. New jobs do appear. And ownership isn't a closed club. more than half of American families already own some stock, even if most own very little.

> `scf` [source_located] Federal Reserve SCF 2025: 56% of families held stock directly or indirectly; 31% in the bottom half by usual income, 97% in the top decile; retirement accounts held by 54.9% of families. (sources: scf25)
