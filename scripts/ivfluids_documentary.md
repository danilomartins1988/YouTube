# How One Flooded Factory Ran America Short of IV Fluids

_In September 2024, a hurricane flooded one factory in North Carolina, and American hospitals started rationing the most basic product they use. How a bag of salt water became a single point of failure, and why nobody fixed it._

## h_open - Sixty percent

About sixty percent. That is how much of America's daily supply of IV fluids came from a single factory in the mountains of North Carolina, according to the American Hospital Association.

(sources: aha, baxter)

## m2_storm - The flood

Hurricane Helene hit the southeast United States and dumped enormous amounts of rain on the mountains of western North Carolina. Rivers rose fast. At North Cove, Baxter said, heavy rain and the storm surge triggered a levee breach, and water got into the site.

## m2_closed - The flood

By September twenty-ninth, Baxter announced that the site was closed for production. The bridges that led to the factory were damaged too. So the problem was not only the machines. Workers and trucks could not easily reach the building.

## m2_inside - The flood

Making sterile fluids is not like making ordinary products. Every production line has to be cleaned, checked and proven safe before a single bag can be shipped. A flooded line cannot simply be dried out and switched back on.

## m3_alloc - The rationing

Baxter put its customers on allocation. That means each hospital could only order a set share of what it normally bought. At first, direct customers could get forty percent of their usual orders of the most needed fluids. For distributors, it was only ten percent. On October ninth, Baxter raised both to sixty percent, and set a goal of returning to ninety or one hundred percent for certain products by the end of the year.

> `ration` [source_located] Baxter (Oct 9, 2024): allocations of highest-demand IV fluids raised from 40% to 60% for direct customers and from 10% to 60% for distributors; goal to return to 90-100% allocation of certain IV codes by end of 2024; FDA (Oct 11) temporary compounding guidance; providers urged to conserve. ## m4_restart - The airlift

Inside North Cove, the repair went line by line. Baxter restarted its first line about four and a half weeks after the flood, and released its first bags made after the storm on November twenty-first. It was the biggest one, about a quarter of the site's output. By late January twenty twenty-five, all ten lines were running again. On February seventeenth, Baxter said the site was back to its production levels from before the storm. That was less than five months after the flood.

## m5_cheap - Why one factory

The answer is in the price tag. The FDA studied drug shortages and listed the first root cause as a lack of incentives to make less profitable drugs. Of the shortage drugs it studied, sixty-three percent were sterile injectables. In the year before the shortage, the median price per unit was just eight dollars and seventy-three cents.

## m5_margin - Why one factory

A government watchdog, the GAO, had found the same thing years earlier. Low profit margins had limited investment in factories for these cheap sterile products. When margins are thin, the cheapest way to make a product is in one huge plant, not in several smaller ones spread across the country.

> `why` [source_located] FDA root causes report (Oct 2019, updated Feb 2020): root cause 1 'Lack of incentives for manufacturers to produce less profitable drugs'; of 163 shortage drugs, 63% sterile injectables; median per-unit price $8.73 ($11.05 injectables) in the year before shortage. ## m5_maria - Why one factory

And it had happened before. In twenty seventeen, Hurricane Maria disrupted saline production at a Baxter plant in Puerto Rico. Saline was already in short supply, and the storm made it worse. Then too, regulators let in fluids from Baxter plants abroad. The FDA described it in its own annual report on drug shortages for that year. Two storms, seven years apart, and the same lesson about the same kind of product.

## payoff - The lesson

So how did one flooded factory run America short of IV fluids? Because the product is so cheap that nobody is paid to build a backup. The system is efficient on a normal day, and fragile on a bad one. The bags came back within months. The shape of the supply chain, with so many arrows starting in one place, is still the part to watch.
