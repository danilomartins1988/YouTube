# The Day Oil Was Worth Less Than Nothing

_On April twentieth, twenty twenty, the price of a barrel of American oil fell below zero for the first time in history and settled at minus thirty-seven dollars and sixty-three cents. Sellers paid buyers to take their oil. How a contract for real barrels, a full storage hub in Oklahoma and a world in lockdown turned oil into something worth less than nothing._

## h_stakes - Minus thirty-seven dollars

On April twentieth, twenty twenty, that is exactly what happened. The price of American crude oil for delivery in May fell below zero, for the first time since that contract began trading in nineteen eighty-three. At the end of the day it settled at minus thirty-seven dollars and sixty-three cents a barrel. People who owned oil were paying others to take it off their hands. To understand how, you have to understand that an oil price is really a promise, and on that day the promise came due.

## m1_demand - A world that stopped

In the spring of twenty twenty, the pandemic shut down much of the planet. Planes stayed on the ground. Roads emptied. Factories went quiet. Analysts cited by the US Congressional Research Service estimated that global demand for crude oil fell by about thirty million barrels a day, around thirty percent of everything the world normally burns.

> `demand` [source_located] CRS (22 Apr 2020): 'The decline in demand ... is estimated by analysts to be about 30% of global crude oil consumption or about 30 million barrels per day.' CFTC: 'OPEC Plus members reached a historic agreement to cut the world's crude oil output by 9.7 million barrels per day ("bpd"), the largest single production cut in history.' (sources: crs, cftc)  

## m1_opec - A world that stopped

The big producers tried to respond. On April twelfth, the OPEC Plus group agreed to cut output by nine point seven million barrels a day, the largest single production cut in history. But it would only start in May. And even a record cut was much smaller than the hole in demand.

## m2_futures - Paper barrels

Most oil is traded through futures contracts. A futures contract is a promise to buy or sell barrels of oil at a set price, on a set date in the future. The best known American contract is called W T I. Each month has its own contract, and each one ends on a fixed day. The May twenty twenty contract expired on April twenty-first.

> `delivery` [source_located] EIA (27 Apr 2020): 'Market participants that hold WTI futures contracts through expiration must make or take physical delivery of WTI crude oil in Cushing, Oklahoma, unless they have made other arrangements ahead of time'; some 'resorting to selling their futures contracts at negative prices, in effect paying a counterparty to allow them to exit their positions.' CRS: 'financial traders—traders that cannot take physical delivery of crude oil— needed to sell before prices fell further.' CFTC: the May Contract 'expired on April 21'. (sources: eia_apr27, crs, cftc)  

## m2_delivery - Paper barrels

And here is the key. These contracts are for real oil. According to the US Energy Information Administration, anyone still holding one when it expires must make or take physical delivery of crude oil in Cushing, Oklahoma, a small town that is home to a major oil storage hub.

> `delivery` [source_located] EIA (27 Apr 2020): 'Market participants that hold WTI futures contracts through expiration must make or take physical delivery of WTI crude oil in Cushing, Oklahoma, unless they have made other arrangements ahead of time'; some 'resorting to selling their futures contracts at negative prices, in effect paying a counterparty to allow them to exit their positions.' ## m3_cushing - Nowhere to put it

The storage tanks at Cushing can hold about seventy-six million barrels of crude oil. In mid-March, they were about half full. By April seventeenth, they held sixty million barrels. They were seventy-six percent full, and filling fast. The three weeks to April seventeenth brought the three largest weekly increases ever recorded.

> `storage` [source_located] EIA (27 Apr 2020): 'crude oil storage facilities at Cushing have 76 million barrels of working storage capacity'; 'As of April 17, Cushing inventories totaled 60 million barrels'; storage 'was 76% full'; 'some of this physically unfilled storage may have already been leased or otherwise committed'. CFTC: storage at Cushing 'had increased from about 50% of capacity in mid-March to approximately 76% of capacity by April 17.' The exchange had seen the risk coming. ## payoff - The lesson

So what does negative oil teach about prices? That a price is not just about how useful something is. It is also about where it is, when it arrives, and whether you have room for it. On April twentieth, the oil had not lost its value. The world had simply run out of places to put it. And a contract that looked like a piece of paper turned out to be a promise to take delivery of something very real.
