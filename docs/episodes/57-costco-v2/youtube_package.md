## Description

```
How Costco makes money is a puzzle: it barely profits on what it sells. A hot dog and a soda cost $1.50, reportedly the same price since 1985. Costco sold $297.2 billion of goods last fiscal year and earned $9.2 billion, yet it keeps only about $2 of every $100 of goods it sells (our calculation). So where do the billions come from? We follow the money like an episode of an animated sitcom: the thinnest margin in retail, the $65 card that more than nine in ten members renew, $5.9 billion of membership fees (equal to about half of operating income), fees paid but not yet counted, the Executive card, a very short shopping list, the treasure hunt, the Kirkland Signature house brand, the protected $1.50 hot dog, gas, the suppliers' money Costco holds before it pays, and the $13.3 billion special dividend declared on November 10, 2026.

In this video:
- Is Costco's business model really about membership fees, and how much do they add up to?
- Why do 92.3% of U.S. and Canadian members renew, and what changes when the fee goes up?
- How do fewer than 4,000 active items per warehouse and Kirkland Signature keep prices low?
- Why has the $1.50 hot dog stayed protected, and what do the food court and gas stations really sell?
- Why does Costco owe its suppliers more ($22.6 billion) than the inventory on its shelves ($19.3 billion)?

Every figure is sourced: Costco's fiscal 2026 Form 10-K (52 weeks ended Aug 30, 2026), its fiscal 2026 results release, its Nov 10, 2026 8-K, its 2024 fee-increase release, its fiscal 2012 and 2017 10-Ks, its 2012 proxy statement and its 2025 annual report letter (SEC EDGAR); the hot-dog quotes are as reported by CNN (May 2024). Values marked "our calculation" are our own arithmetic; Kirkland's ≈ $90B is a company statement. Ron Vachris and Jim Sinegal appear as respectful caricatures; every other character, the stores and the member card are fictional and original. Not affiliated with Costco. Not financial advice.
```
