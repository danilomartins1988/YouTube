# Episode 46: "Why Banks Still Make Billions From Overdrafts". Research notes

Fact-check date: 2026-11-10. All amounts are in US dollars. Each bullet gives the claim, then the exact quote or figure, the URL, and the date published. "Fetched" means the primary page or PDF was opened and read, not just a search snippet.

## 1. Headline revenue figure (re-checked)

- **2019 to 2023 decline: CONFIRMED.** Reporting banks' overdraft/NSF revenue was "$11.96 billion" in 2019 and $5.83 billion in 2023, down 51%, or $6.13 billion. 2023 was also about $1.8 billion (24%) lower than 2022. Average saving: "$185" a year per overdrafting household. Coverage: 617 reporting banks with assets over $1 billion. Credit unions and banks of $1 billion or less are excluded. The page also notes that quarterly fees were flat through 2023. URL: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/data-spotlight-overdraft-nsf-revenue-in-2023-down-more-than-50-versus-pre-pandemic-levels-saving-consumers-over-6-billion-annually/ . Published 2024-04-24 (last modified 2024-07-12). Fetched.
  - Wording for the script: say "banks with more than $1 billion in assets", not "all banks". The figure leaves out credit unions.
- **This is the CFPB's latest release.** The CFPB's overdraft/NSF trends page lists 2024-04-24 as its newest data release, and 2023 as the newest year of data. It has no 2024 or 2025 figures. URL: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/trends-in-overdraftnon-sufficient-fund-nsf-fee-revenue-and-practices/ . Fetched 2026-11-10.

## 2. The December 2024 rule and its repeal

- **The final rule.** The CFPB announced it on 2024-12-12. It covers institutions with "more than $10 billion in assets". Banks could choose one of three options:
  - cap the fee at $5;
  - charge a fee that covers their own costs and losses;
  - treat overdraft as a loan and follow the Truth in Lending Act (TILA) rules.

  Expected savings were "up to $5 billion in annual overdraft fee savings", or $225 per household that pays overdraft fees. The effective date was to be 2025-10-01. The release also says consumers "still paid more than $5.8 billion in 2023". URL: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/cfpb-closes-overdraft-loophole-to-save-americans-billions-in-fees/ . Published 2024-12-12. Fetched.
- **Rule citation:** 89 Fed. Reg. 106768 (Dec. 30, 2024), "Overdraft Lending: Very Large Financial Institutions". The citation comes from the text of the Public Law, fetched below.
- **Repeal: S.J.Res. 18, Public Law 119-10, approved May 9, 2025.** The law says the rule "shall have no force or effect." URL: https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ10/PLAW-119publ10.htm . Fetched.
- **House vote:** passed 217–211 (0 present, 5 not voting) on 2025-04-09, Roll Call 96. URL: https://clerk.house.gov/Votes/202596 . Fetched.
- **Senate vote:** the Senate passed it in March 2025. CRS report IN12513 confirms the month but gives no date or tally. URL: https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN12513.html . First published 2025-02-24, updated 2025-09-10. Fetched. The exact day and tally are under Flags.
- **Bank 10-K corroborates the rule.** Wells Fargo's 2024 Annual Report describes the "three options": "charge no more than a five-dollar fee, charge a fee that covers no more than a bank's costs or losses ... or treat the overdraft as a loan." It adds: "we would expect a significant reduction to our fees for overdraft services, which are included in deposit-related fees." URL: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/72971/000007297125000092/wfc2024annualreport.pdf . Filed early 2025. Fetched.
- **CFPB guidance also withdrawn.** CFPB Circular 2022-06 ("Unanticipated Overdraft Fee Assessment Practices") was among the 67 guidance documents the CFPB withdrew around 2025-05-12. Source: law-firm summaries only, for example https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2025/05/16/cfpb-rescinds-67-guidance-documents/ (2025-05-16). The Federal Register notice was not fetched. See Flags.

## 3. Revenue in 2024 and 2025

- **No official CFPB total exists for 2024 or 2025.** See the trends page in section 1. All later totals are analyses of FFIEC call report data by third parties.
- **2024, banks with over $1 billion in assets: about $6.1 billion.** Bates White's analysis of call reports: "reporting banks collected $6.1 billion in overdraft revenue in 2024". That is "a somewhat modest 3% increase over 2023" and marks "the apparent end of steep structural declines seen since 2019". The paper also says JPMorgan Chase and Wells Fargo "each collected $1 billion or more in overdraft revenue in 2024, accounting for one-third of the $6.1 billion total." Author: Amanda Kurzendoerfer (Law360 article reprint). URL: https://www.bateswhite.com/media/publication/253_Surveying_The_Changing_Overdraft_Fee_Landscape.pdf . Published 2025-08-11. Fetched. This is a secondary analysis, not a government publication.
- **2024, banks over $10 billion: $4.88 billion.** The Consumer Federation of America (CFA) analysis of FFIEC call report variable RIADH032 gives "$4,883,630,000". Its Table 1 shows JPMorgan Chase at $1.028 billion and Wells Fargo at about $1 billion. URL: https://consumerfed.org/curious-how-much-banks-make-from-overdraft-fees-we-have-the-data/ . Published 2025-04-07. Fetched. This is a secondary analysis.
- **2024, all banks and credit unions: an estimated $12.1 billion.** The National Consumer Law Center (NCLC) cites the Financial Health Network: "Consumers incurred an estimated $12.1 billion in overdraft and NSF fees in 2024." NCLC also says "Overdraft fee revenues at the largest banks increased 2.3% from 2024 to 2025", and that the industry total for 2025 "could exceed $12.4 billion" (an extrapolation). JPMorgan Chase and Wells Fargo each took "about $1 billion each a year in overdraft fees in 2025". PNC was third with $279 million. URL: https://www.nclc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/2026.06_Report_The-12-Billion-Overdraft-Trap.pdf . Published June 2026. Fetched.
  - Companion issue brief: https://www.nclc.org/resources/overdraft-fees-rising-in-absence-of-cfpb-rule/ (2026-06-03). It says the top 20 banks' 2025 FFIEC overdraft revenue was about $4 billion combined. Fetched.
- **Credit union data is no longer reported.** NCLC: "in 2025 the National Credit Union Administration rescinded a requirement that credit unions with over $1 billion in assets report their overdraft and NSF fee revenue." Same NCLC report URL, June 2026.

## 4. Typical fee (about $35)

- **The median fee is $35. CONFIRMED (CFPB).** "the median overdraft fee for the 119 banks and credit unions with more than $10 billion in total assets that were charging overdraft fees was $35. The analogous figure for the 53 banks and credit unions still charging NSF fees was $32." This comes from CFPB market monitoring between December 2022 and August 2023. Bankrate's 2023 average of $26.61 is cited in the same report. URL: https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_overdraft-nsf-report_2023-12.pdf (CFPB Office of Research Publication No. 2023-9). Published December 2023. Fetched.
- **Earlier median: $34.** The CFPB 2014 Data Point gives a $34 median fee. The 2023 report above cites it ("the median overdraft fee ($34) from the CFPB's 2014 report").
- **Wells Fargo now.** "Our overdraft fee for Consumer checking accounts is $35 per item". The bank charges at most 3 fees per business day, no fee on items of $10 or less, and no NSF/returned-item fee. URL: https://www.wellsfargo.com/checking/overdraft-services/ . Fetched 2026-11-10; the page shows no date.
- **JPMorgan Chase and Wells Fargo.** Bates White: "At $34-$35, overdraft fees for these banks are in line" with the median. URL as in section 3, 2025-08-11.

## 6. Who pays

- **CFPB 2017, exact numbers.** "Together, the two groups of frequent overdrafters account for 9.1 percent of accounts, but pay 78.7 percent of all overdraft and NSF fees." In the key findings, rounded: "nine percent of all accounts ... paid 79 percent".
  - "Frequent" means more than 10 overdrafts and NSFs combined in 12 months.
  - "Very frequent" (more than 20 a year): "4.9 percent of accounts but incur 63.3 percent of all overdraft and NSF fees."

  URL: https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/201708_cfpb_data-point_frequent-overdrafters.pdf (CFPB Data Point: Frequent Overdrafters). Published August 2017. Fetched. Data covers July 2011 to June 2012.
- **CFPB 2014:** "eight percent of customers incur nearly 75 percent of all overdraft fees." Opted-in accounts pay "over $250 per year" in overdraft and NSF fees. URL: https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/201407_cfpb_report_data-point_overdrafts.pdf . Published July 2014. Fetched.
- **CFPB April 2024 spotlight (endnote):** nearly 80% of overdraft and NSF fees came from under 9% of accountholders with more than 10 overdrafts a year. Same URL as section 1.
- **Income (CFPB 2023):** "consumers from households with incomes below $35,000 are 15 percent more likely to be charged an overdraft fee". Households with more than 10 overdraft fees paid "a minimum of $385" a year at the $35 median. URL: the December 2023 report in section 4.

## 7. Debit card transactions

- **Best available CFPB-cited figure.** It counts transactions, not revenue, and is from 2006: "The FDIC found in its study of bank overdraft programs that 41% of all NSF and overdraft transactions occurring in 2006 at banks with automated overdraft programs were related to use of debit cards at the point of sale." The original source is the FDIC Study of Bank Overdraft Programs (Nov. 2008). URL: https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/201306_cfpb_whitepaper_overdraft-practices.pdf (CFPB Study of Overdraft Programs white paper). Published June 2013. Fetched.
- **Debit overdrafts are small.** "In the case of debit card transactions, the median amount that leads to an overdraft fee is $24", against $50 for all debits. Debit card is "by far the most frequent transaction type" at 17.4 transactions per account per month. URL: the CFPB 2014 Data Point in section 6 (July 2014). Fetched.
- **No current revenue share found.** No primary source gives the share of overdraft revenue that comes from debit card transactions today. See Flags.

## 8. Posting order and "authorize positive, settle negative" (APSN)

- **Re-ordering, CFPB 2013.** Posting larger items first ("high-to-low") means "larger items may exhaust available funds earlier ... and increase the likelihood that more items will take the balance negative". The white paper also found that "No two participants in the study follow identical posting order policies". URL: the 2013 white paper in section 7. Fetched.
- **APSN, CFPB Circular 2022-06.** APSN covers debit card transactions that were authorized against a sufficient available balance but settled against a negative one because of intervening transactions or settlement order. The circular says charging overdraft fees on these is likely unfair under the Consumer Financial Protection Act (CFPA). It cites fees of up to $36. URL: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/circulars/consumer-financial-protection-circular-2022-06-unanticipated-overdraft-fee-assessment-practices/ . Published 2022-10-26. Fetched. It was later withdrawn (see section 2 and Flags).
- **Supervisory Highlights, Issue 29.** This is the Junk Fees Special Edition, Winter 2023, released 2023-03-08 and published in the Federal Register at 88 FR 16945, 2023-03-21. Section 2.1.1 is "Unfair Authorize Positive, Settle Negative Overdraft Fees". It says "Supervision has cited institutions for unfair unanticipated overdraft fees", found "at least tens of millions of dollars of consumer injury", and that "institutions are providing redress to over 170,000 consumers." URL: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-03-21/html/2023-05667.htm . Fetched.
- **Enforcement example: Regions Bank (Sept. 2022).** The CFPB ordered $141 million in refunds plus a $50 million penalty for "authorized-positive" overdraft fees. Source: DLA Piper and DWT summaries only; the CFPB order was not fetched.

## 9. Regulation E opt-in (2010)

- **Federal Reserve final rule, announced 2009-11-12.** It bars institutions from charging overdraft fees on ATM and one-time debit card transactions unless the consumer opts in. It also bars treating customers who don't opt in worse than those who do. "The final rules are effective July 1, 2010." URL: https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/bcreg20091112a.htm . Published 2009-11-12. Fetched.
- **Current rule text, 12 CFR 1005.17(b)(1).** An institution "shall not assess a fee or charge on a consumer's account for paying an ATM or one-time debit card transaction" without affirmative consent. For accounts opened before July 1, 2010, no fees "on or after August 15, 2010" without opt-in (§ 1005.17(c)(1)). URL: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1005/17/ . Fetched 2026-11-10.
- **What the rule does not cover:** checks, ACH payments and recurring debit card payments. The 2014 Data Point says "the Regulation E opt-in requirement only covers ATM and non-recurring debit card transactions" (section 6 URL).
- **Opt-in rate:** 16.1% of accounts at the study banks were opted in at the end of 2011, per the 2013 white paper (section 7 URL).

## 10. Bank 10-K lines (Wells Fargo)

- **Line name.** Wells Fargo's income statement line is "Deposit-related fees", not "service charges on deposit accounts". It is defined as fees "for account charges, overdraft services, cash network fees, wire transfer and other remittance fees, and safe deposit box fees". The company does not report overdraft fees separately in the 10-K.
- **FY2024 Annual Report (Table 5):**
  - deposit-related fees: $5,015 million in 2024, $4,694 million in 2023 (+7%), $5,316 million in 2022;
  - Consumer Banking and Lending segment: $2,734 million (2024), $2,702 million (2023), $3,093 million (2022).

  URL: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/72971/000007297125000092/wfc2024annualreport.pdf . Fetched.

  - Consumer Banking and Lending segment: $2,694 million (2025), down 1%.

## Flags / not verified

1. **No official total for 2024 or 2025.** The CFPB's last overdraft/NSF data release is 2024-04-24 (2023 data). The 2024 figure of about $6.1 billion (Bates White), the $4.88 billion figure (CFA, banks over $10 billion) and the $12.1 billion and $12.4 billion figures (Financial Health Network, via NCLC, including credit unions) are third-party analyses or estimates. They measure different things. I did not pull the raw FFIEC call report data (RIADH032) myself.
2. **NCLC wording error.** The June 2026 NCLC report contains a garbled sentence: "Overdraft revenues at the largest 20 consumer banks rose 2.6%, from $12.1 billion to about $12.4 billion in 2025 over 2023". It mixes industry-wide totals with top-20 bank data, and elsewhere the report says 2.3% from 2024 to 2025. Do not quote it.
3. **"Billions" framing.** The $12 billion-plus figures include credit unions and banks under $1 billion. Bank-only call report data for 2024 is about $6.1 billion. The script should say which one it uses.
4. **Senate vote on S.J.Res. 18 not confirmed.** The reported tally is 52–48, around March 26–27, 2025. I could not fetch the Senate roll call or congress.gov actions (HTTP 403). The CRS report confirms only "March 2025". The House vote (217–211, April 9, 2025) and the signing date (May 9, 2025) are confirmed from primary sources. One tracker gives May 20, 2025 as the signing date; that is wrong.
5. **Bank of America's $10 fee is not confirmed from the bank's own page.** The BofA newsroom page would not load (connection reset). The $35 to $10 cut (May 2022) and the end of NSF fees (February 2022) are confirmed by CNBC, and the policy change by BofA's own 8-K and 10-K, but the $10 figure itself is not in the filings I read. Also not confirmed: the "97% reduction vs 2009" claim (search snippet only), and whether the $10 fee is still in force today (CFA's 2025 article says it is).
6. **Debit card share of revenue.** I found no current primary figure for the share of overdraft revenue from debit card transactions. The only CFPB-cited number is 41% of overdraft and NSF transactions (not revenue) in 2006, from the FDIC's 2008 study. Do not present it as a current revenue share.
7. **"79.7%" is wrong.** The exact 2017 CFPB figure is 78.7% of fees paid by 9.1% of accounts (rounded to 79% and 9%). The data is from 2011–2012.
8. **Re-ordering versus APSN.** These are different practices. High-to-low re-ordering is described in the 2013 white paper. APSN is about fees charged at settlement after authorization on a positive balance. The Supervisory Highlights material covers APSN, not re-ordering as such. Circular 2022-06 was withdrawn in May 2025 (law-firm summaries; Federal Register notice 90 FR 20084 not fetched). Treat it as past guidance.
9. **Citi's effective date** (2022-06-19) comes from PYMNTS only. Ally's June 2021 elimination and the Regions Bank order amounts come from secondary sources only.
10. **JPMorgan Chase.** Its current $34 fee is not confirmed from a Chase page; the two URLs I tried returned 404. Bates White says "$34-$35". The JPMorgan 10-K line ("Deposit-related fees") was not fetched.
11. **Typical fee.** "$35" is the median at institutions over $10 billion in 2022–23 (CFPB). Bankrate's 2023 average across all banks was lower, at $26.61. Say "the typical big-bank fee is about $35".
