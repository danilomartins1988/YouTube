## act1_card - The city that looks impossible




## a1_map - The city that looks impossible

First, a common mistake. Dubai is not a country. It is one of the seven emirates that form the United Arab Emirates. Its bigger neighbour, Abu Dhabi, is the federal capital.

> `uae_seven` [source_located] The United Arab Emirates is a federation of seven emirates, of which Dubai is one; Abu Dhabi is the federal capital. (sources: uae_constitution)  

## a1_oil_split - The city that looks impossible

That difference matters. Under the UAE constitution, each emirate owns its own natural resources. And Abu Dhabi holds about 94 percent of the country's oil reserves. Dubai holds about 4 percent.

> `art23_resources` [source_located] Under Article 23 of the UAE Constitution, the natural resources and wealth in each emirate are the public property of that emirate. (sources: uae_constitution)  
> `reserves_split` [source_located] Abu Dhabi holds about 94% of the UAE's oil reserves and Dubai about 4% (US ITA citing the 2019 UAE State of Energy Report). (sources: trade_gov_uae_energy)

## a2_creek - Dubai before the skyscrapers

Long before the towers, Dubai was a small trading town on a saltwater creek. Its economy floated on pearls, gathered by divers and sold on to distant markets.

> `pearl_collapse` [source_located] The Gulf pearling industry declined after Japan developed cultured pearls from 1916 onward, compounded by the 1929 financial crash, from which it never fully recovered. (sources: qdl_pearls)

## a2_pearls - Dubai before the skyscrapers

Then the pearl trade collapsed. Japan began producing cultured pearls from 1916, and the crash of 1929 finished what was left of the old market.

> `pearl_collapse` [source_located] The Gulf pearling industry declined after Japan developed cultured pearls from 1916 onward, compounded by the 1929 financial crash, from which it never fully recovered. (sources: qdl_pearls)  

## a2_merchants - Dubai before the skyscrapers

But Dubai had already learned a different trick. In the years after 1902, as the Persian port of Lingeh declined, merchants moved across the water, and Dubai became the main trading centre of its coast. Its advantage was openness, not natural resources.

> `lingah_shift` [source_located] According to Lorimer's Gazetteer, Dubai grew rapidly after 1902, helped by the decline of Lingeh, and became the chief commercial emporium of the Trucial Coast. (sources: qdl_lorimer)  
> Contains editorial interpretation (marked analysis).  
> Lorimer: Dubai grew "assisted by the decadence of Lingeh". 'Openness' is our interpretation; sources describe duty cuts around 1901-1904 but disagree on the date, so no date is given.  

## a2_dredge - Dubai before the skyscrapers

In 1959, Dubai borrowed money, largely from Kuwait, to dredge its silting creek so that bigger ships could reach the town. It was investing in trade infrastructure before it had found any oil at all.

> `creek_dredging` [source_located] Dubai Creek was dredged from 1959, financed largely by a loan from Kuwait together with local 'creek bonds'. (sources: gulfnews_creek)  
> `oil_discovery` [source_located] Oil was discovered offshore Dubai at the Fateh field in 1966. (sources: gulfnews_oil)  
> Oil was discovered in 1966 (oil_discovery), after the dredging.

## a4_jafza - Jebel Ali and the trading machine

Next to the port, Dubai created the Jebel Ali Free Zone, in 1985. It started with nineteen companies. Today it hosts more than 11,000 businesses.

## act7_card - Free zones, finance and foreign capital




## a7_freezones - Free zones, finance and foreign capital

Dubai's other export is its rulebook. The emirate has more than 20 free zones, where foreign companies can be wholly foreign-owned. And Since 2021, full foreign ownership has been allowed for most businesses outside them too.

> `free_zones` [source_located] Dubai has more than 20 free zones (Invest in Dubai). (sources: invest_dubai_hub)  
> `onshore_ownership` [source_located] Since 1 June 2021, foreign investors can own 100% of most onshore UAE companies, after the amendment of the Commercial Companies Law (Federal Decree-Law No. 26 of 2020). (sources: wam_ownership_2021)  

## a7_difc - Free zones, finance and foreign capital

In finance, Dubai went further. In 2004, it created the Dubai International Financial Centre, a zone with its own English-language, common-law courts. By the end of 2025, it had 8,844 active companies.

> `difc_courts` [source_located] The DIFC, created under Dubai law in 2004, has its own English-language common-law courts. (sources: difc_courts_framework)  
> `difc_2025` [source_located] At the end of 2025 the DIFC had 8,844 active companies and a workforce of 50,200, including more than 290 banks and capital-markets firms. (sources: dmo_difc_2025)  

## a7_tax - Free zones, finance and foreign capital

And taxes, long close to zero, are no longer zero. Most companies now pay 9 percent corporate tax on profits above 375,000 dirhams. Qualifying free-zone companies can still pay zero percent on qualifying income. Large multinationals face a 15 percent minimum tax, and VAT of 5 percent applies to most spending. Individuals still pay no income tax on their salaries.

> `ct_rate` [source_located] The UAE corporate tax rate is 9% on taxable income above AED 375,000 (0% below), for financial years starting on or after 1 June 2023. (sources: uae_ct, mof_tax)  
> `qfzp` [source_located] A Qualifying Free Zone Person pays 0% corporate tax on qualifying income and 9% on other income, subject to substance and other conditions. (sources: fta_qfzp)  
> `dmtt` [source_located] Since financial years starting on or after 1 January 2025, large multinational groups (global revenue of EUR 750 million or more) pay a 15% domestic minimum top-up tax in the UAE. (sources: mof_dmtt)  
> `vat` [source_located] VAT was introduced across the UAE on 1 January 2018 at a standard rate of 5%. (sources: mof_tax)  
> `no_pit` [source_located] The UAE does not levy personal income tax on individuals' employment income (PwC, reviewed March 2026). (sources: pwc_uae_individual)

## act8_card - Who benefits, and what are the risks?




## a8_workers - Who benefits, and what are the risks?

This model runs on people from somewhere else. Human Rights Watch says migrant workers make up 88 percent of the UAE's population. They build the towers, staff the hotels and drive the city.

> `migrant_share` [source_located] Human Rights Watch says migrant workers make up 88% of the UAE's population (2023). (sources: hrw_2023)  

## a8_labour - Who benefits, and what are the risks?

The group has documented recruitment fees, unpaid wages, and a sponsorship system, known as kafala, that ties workers' visas to their employers. The labour ministry handled 46,819 unpaid-wage cases in 2022. The government points to measures such as a summer ban on outdoor work from half past twelve to three in the afternoon. Human Rights Watch says that is not enough.

> `hrw_findings` [source_located] HRW reports that the kafala sponsorship system ties migrant workers' visas to their employers, that interviewees paid recruitment fees, and that the UAE relies on a summer midday work ban to manage heat risk, which HRW calls ineffective; it cites 46,819 unpaid-wage cases handled by the labour ministry in 2022. (sources: hrw_2023)  
> `midday_break` [source_located] The UAE bans outdoor work in direct sun from 12:30 to 15:00 between 15 June and 15 September; 2025 was the 21st consecutive year. (sources: mohre_midday_2025)  

## a8_crash - Who benefits, and what are the risks?

The second risk is debt. When the global financial crisis hit, Dubai property prices fell by about half. In November 2009, Dubai asked creditors of Dubai World, a state-owned conglomerate, for a pause on about 26 billion dollars of debt.

> `crash_2009` [source_located] Dubai property prices fell by about half from their mid-2008 peak by mid-2009 (Colliers International, as reported by Gulf News). (sources: gulfnews_prices_2009)  
> `standstill_2009` [source_located] On 25 November 2009 Dubai asked creditors of Dubai World and its property units for a standstill on about $26 billion of debt. (sources: gulfnews_dubai_world_2009)  

## a8_bailout - Who benefits, and what are the risks?

Abu Dhabi stepped in with 10 billion dollars. Weeks later, in January 2010, the world's tallest tower opened, and its name changed from Burj Dubai to Burj Khalifa, after Abu Dhabi's ruler.

> `bailout_2009` [source_located] On 14 December 2009 Abu Dhabi provided $10 billion to Dubai, part of which repaid a maturing Nakheel Islamic bond. (sources: ibtimes_bailout_2009)  
> `burj_renamed` [source_located] The world's tallest building opened on 4 January 2010 and was renamed from Burj Dubai to Burj Khalifa, after Abu Dhabi's ruler and UAE President Sheikh Khalifa. (sources: csm_burj_2010)  

## a8_imf - Who benefits, and what are the risks?

Today, the IMF warns that a reversal of capital flows, or a shift in investor sentiment, could trigger an unexpected price correction. It also notes that the finances of government-related companies are hard to assess, because the data are not published in full.

> `imf_correction_risk` [source_located] The IMF warns the real estate sector may be affected by a slowdown or reversal of capital flows or a shift in investor sentiment that could lead to an unexpected price correction. (sources: imf_cr25327)  
> `imf_gre_info` [source_located] The IMF notes that limited information on the financial positions of individual GREs and the absence of aggregate public sector financial statements hinder comprehensive risk assessment. (sources: imf_cr25327)  

## a8_war - Who benefits, and what are the risks?

Then came a real test. A regional war broke out at the end of February 2026, disrupting travel and trade. In the first half of the year, Dubai's economy was 2.3 percent smaller than a year earlier.

> `war_2026` [source_located] Reporting by The National ties the 2026 slowdown to the regional war that began on 28 February 2026, which disrupted travel and trade; Q1 2026 real GDP still grew 2.4%. (sources: national_iran_war_q1, dmo_gdp_q1_2026)  
> `gdp_h1_2026` [source_located] Dubai's real GDP was AED 473 billion in H1 2026, 2.3% lower than a year earlier, according to DDSE. (sources: wam_gdp_h1_2026, national_gdp_h1_2026)

## a9_engine - The real economic engine

So how does Dubai really make money? Not mainly from oil. It makes money by being the place in between: where goods change ships, passengers change planes, capital finds a home and companies set up under rules they trust. The state builds the hub, through companies it owns. Foreign money and foreign workers fill it.
