## 6. Animated explanatory sequences (≥3, presenter or character interacts)
1. **Follow one paycheck through one house at two rates** (s12 + presenter board p13): the same $416,700 house, 20% down:
   $1,343/month at 2.65% vs $2,243 at 7.11% (our calc.); then the presenter draws the income test (payment ≤ 25% of income)
   and two bars, ≈ $107,600 needed vs $87,460 median, and brackets the ≈ $20K gap.
2. **The rate rollercoaster** (s11): Maya rides a coaster whose track is the Freddie Mac weekly 30-year rate since 2019 on a
   real % × year grid; flags pop at the 2.65% record low, the climb above 7%, the 7.40% October peak and 7.11% last week.
3. **The golden handcuffs** (s17–s19): a street of homes padlocks itself ("< 4%"), Rex's empty-nest house clamps shut with
   "MY OLD RATE" while Maya runs toward it; FHFA's estimate (1.33M sales prevented, prices +5.7% vs −3.3%) builds as a chart.
4. **The Shrink-O-Matic** (s08, s15): a machine zaps a starter home smaller as the "affordable listings" gauge falls 49% → 23%.
5. **The zoning board** (s27–s29 + presenter board p30): the parcel map turns 78% yellow; the presenter draws one lot as
   one house, a duplex and a fourplex, splitting the land cost.
6. **Presenter boards** also draw the years-of-income bars (p09) and why shortage estimates differ (p24, boxes + arrow).

## 7. Payoff
Specific answer: the math broke in two places at once — the price rose to ≈ 4.7 years of income (JCHS) and the rate added
≈ $900 a month to the same house (our calc.), so buying the typical home now takes ≈ $108,000 a year, about $20,000 more than
the typical household earns (our calc. vs Census). And the fixes are stuck: owners locked into sub-4% loans don't sell, a
decade of underbuilding left a shortage (estimates 1.2M–5.5M), and local rules make the cheapest homes the hardest to build.
Insight line: the starter home wasn't lost by accident; it was "priced out, rated out, and zoned out."
