On a Thursday in November, June, a nurse from Ohio, got what she'd asked her brokerage app for. One share of the most talked-about company on Wall Street, at the offer price of eighty-three dollars. By lunchtime, it was worth a lot more. By the closing bell, a bit less. But here's what puzzled her. The company behind that share is barely old enough for kindergarten. It doesn't make chips. It doesn't own a giant cloud. And its biggest partners are also its shareholders, its suppliers and its rivals. So what exactly did June just buy?
Here's how the day went. The shares went to the first buyers the night before, at June's price. When trading opened on the Nasdaq, under the ticker A, N, T, buyers rushed in and the price leapt. Then the excitement cooled, and early buyers took quick profits. By the close, one share was worth about ninety-seven dollars. Still a gain for June. Just a smaller one.
Now, the headline number. You'll see this company valued at several prices, and they can all be right. Let me draw why. Share price times the shares that exist today gives one number. Add every share that could exist soon, from employee options, stock awards and notes that turn into stock, and you get a bigger one, called fully diluted. At June's price, fully diluted, Wall Street called it about one and a half trillion dollars. Same company. Different counting.
The sale itself was the biggest stock offering on record, about eighty-one billion dollars. And almost all of that money went to the company, as new shares, not to insiders cashing out. The co-founders each sold only a sliver. Then, right after the sale, a group of chip and cloud giants agreed to buy even more stock, at the same price. Remember that group.
Rewind to the beginning. A group of researchers left OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, to start their own lab. Among them, a brother and sister: Dario Amodei, who became chief executive, and Daniela Amodei, president. They made Anthropic a public benefit corporation, allowed to weigh its mission alongside profit. Their product was an AI assistant called Claude.
At first, Claude was one chatbot among many. Then it found its niche. Programmers used it to write code, and companies plugged it into their own products. And most of the money doesn't come from monthly subscriptions. It comes from usage. Businesses pay for the work Claude does, a little like an electricity bill.
And that bill grew at a speed Wall Street had rarely seen. When Claude first went on sale, revenue was tiny. So far this year, through September, Anthropic booked thirty-three billion dollars in sales. That's the rocket the whole price is strapped to.
But growth is only one scoreboard. Let me draw the others. Revenue is what customers paid. The run rate is this month's pace, stretched over a year. Operating profit is what's left after running the business. Net profit counts everything else, and this year it shows a giant loss, mostly accounting on notes that turn into stock. And cash flow, the money that actually came in, turned positive.
So where does the money go? Follow June's share into a data center. Every answer Claude writes runs on rooms full of specialized chips that need power, cooling and buildings. Training a new model costs a fortune. Running it costs money every time someone asks a question. That's called inference, and it grows with every customer.
Here's the key ratio. Last year, for every dollar of sales, Anthropic spent about a dollar sixty on computing and infrastructure. It was selling dollars that cost more than a dollar to make.
This year, the picture flipped. For every dollar of sales, computing cost about fifty-seven cents. Bigger customers, more efficient models, better chip deals. That's the chart the optimists point to.
And here's the chart the skeptics point to. To keep growing, Anthropic has signed contracts for future computing power, data centers and chips, worth about nine hundred billion dollars, stretching over years, some over decades. That's not money already spent. But much of it is promised, whether the customers show up or not.
Let me separate some words that get mixed up. A commitment is a promise to buy later. Spending is cash going out. Debt is borrowed money that must be repaid. An expense hits profit as the service gets used. Anthropic's giant number is mostly the first kind, and its actual debt is small next to its cash pile. The risk isn't a loan. It's the promise.
So the bet comes down to one question. Can Anthropic earn more from every dollar of computing than it costs, while those bills come due? If demand keeps climbing, the promises will look cheap. If it stalls, they'll look enormous.
Now meet the strangest relationship in this story. Amazon. It has invested billions in Anthropic since its early days. Its cloud rents Anthropic much of its computing power, and Anthropic has promised to spend at least a hundred and ten billion dollars there over the next decade. Amazon also sells Claude to its cloud customers, and builds AI models of its own.
Watch the money move. Amazon sends cash to Anthropic as an investment. Anthropic sends cash back to Amazon to pay for computing, and Amazon books that as sales. When Amazon sells Claude to its customers, it keeps a cut. And when Anthropic's value rises, the stake on Amazon's books rises too. Google runs a similar loop, as investor, cloud supplier and rival.
Critics have a name for this. Circular financing. Their worry: when the same money travels around a loop, demand can look stronger than it is, and everyone in the loop looks healthier because of the others. A rival has argued that the way Anthropic counts cloud sales makes its revenue look bigger. Some warn the risk lands on ordinary investors, like June. And Anthropic's own filing warns these ties could create conflicts of interest.
The other side has a case too. The cash is real, and most of Claude's paying customers are ordinary businesses. Counting cloud sales in full is a standard accounting choice, and both rivals say they follow the rules. Suppliers lending to customers is an old idea. Carmakers do it every day. And it's all disclosed. So the real question is how much growth would survive if the loop slowed down.
And here's the twist. Remember the group that bought extra stock after the IPO? Google, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and the chipmaker TSMC. Most of them are, directly or indirectly, part of the supply chain Anthropic runs on. The investors are often the suppliers, and part of what they put in can come back as sales.
So who got rich? Let's play detective with a hypothetical early investor. In Anthropic's first funding round, investors paid what works out today to about a quarter a share. A small bet back then is now worth hundreds of times more. On paper.
The biggest paper winners are the giants. Amazon bought its stake in pieces over several years, and before the IPO it was already carrying it at many times what it paid, with much of that gain flowing into its reported profits. But a paper gain isn't cash until the shares are sold.
Then there are the employees, many paid partly in stock options, the right to buy shares at an old, low price. Reports say more than four hundred of them are now worth a fortune. On paper.
And on paper is the key phrase. Most insiders can't sell yet. A lockup keeps most of their shares off the market for about six months, up to a year for the co-founders and biggest holders. When the locks open, a wave of sellers can arrive at once.
And June? Her share has no lockup. But notice the difference. The earliest backers paid pocket change, years ago, when nobody knew if this would work. June paid full price, on the day the story was most exciting. Same share. Very different bet.
So, is it worth it? One simple test. Compare the company's value at the first close with the pace of its sales. The stock is priced at about twenty times its yearly sales pace. Not its profits. Its sales.
Let me draw what that price assumes. Picture Anthropic years from now, when growth slows, as it does for every big company. For today's price to look sensible then, it would need profits on the scale of the most profitable companies on Earth, year after year. Not impossible. Just a lot to assume on day one.
Picture June's share at a fork in the road. Several roads lead away from here, and nobody knows the odds of any of them.
On the bright road, AI becomes as basic as electricity. Anthropic keeps winning big customers, its computing costs keep falling, and those huge contracts turn out to be bargains. On that road, today's price could one day look cheap.
On the middle road, AI keeps growing, but competition squeezes prices. Anthropic becomes a big, healthy company, and the stock does fine, slowly, because so much of the good news was paid for up front.
And on the dark road, growth slows, cheaper models catch up, and the bills keep arriving anyway. Profits stay thin, the company needs more money, and every new share it sells makes June's slice smaller.
Step back, and there's a pattern older than AI. In a gold rush, the steadiest money is often made selling shovels. Here, the shovel sellers are the chipmakers and the clouds. They get paid as long as the building goes on, whether or not any AI lab turns a big profit. Some get paid twice, as investors and as suppliers.
So, what did Wall Street buy? A share of one of the fastest-growing companies ever measured, with real customers and real cash. Attached to a giant stack of promises, and to partners on every side of the table. That doesn't make it a bubble, or a sure thing. It's a bet on how long the growth lasts, at a price that assumes it lasts a very long time.
Anthropic may become one of the most important companies of the next decade. But importance and investment returns are two different things. June hasn't decided what to do with her share. The question isn't whether AI will change the world. It's how much of that future has already been priced into a single stock.
