## Description

```
The internet you use every day isn't in the sky. It's at the bottom of the ocean. According to the UN's telecom agency (ITU), more than 99% of international data travels through about 500 cables on the seabed, more than 1.7 million kilometers of them.

In this animated documentary:
- How your data crosses an ocean as pulses of light, and why satellites carry only a tiny share
- Who owns the cables: from clubs of phone companies to Google, Meta, Microsoft and Amazon
- Who builds and repairs them: a few companies and a small fleet of repair ships
- What happens when a cable breaks: the Red Sea in 2024 and Tonga in 2022
- Why governments now fight over who builds a cable and where it lands

KEY SOURCES
- ITU press release (27 Feb 2025): more than 99% of international data traffic, about 500 cables, over 1.7 million km
- TeleGeography, Submarine Cable FAQs (2026): garden-hose width, fibres as thin as a hair, 25-year minimum design life, more than 600 active and planned cables, owners and investors, satellites 0.37% of U.S. international capacity (FCC data)
- TeleGeography (May 2023): cables carry over 99% of intercontinental traffic
- TeleGeography (May 2025): content and cloud almost three-quarters of international bandwidth demand in 2024; demand tripled 2020-2024 to more than 6.4 Pbps
- Meta Engineering (May 2020): 2Africa planned at 37,000 km, 23 countries
- International Cable Protection Committee (2026): 150-200 faults a year, 70-80% accidental (fishing and anchors); Baltic 2024 repairs within 2 weeks

- TechXplore and Al Jazeera (Feb 2022): Tonga eruption, 80 km of cable torn, more than five weeks offline, about 90 km replaced
- Reuters (Mar 2023) and Light Reading (Mar 2023): SEA-ME-WE 6, HMN Tech and SubCom; Pacific Light Cable Network's Hong Kong leg
```
