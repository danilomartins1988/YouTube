# Ep. 42: "Why America Stopped Making the Penny": Research Notes

Compiled 2026-11-10. All amounts are US dollars unless marked C$. Every bullet below was read on the page cited, not taken from a search snippet. The one exception is a FY2025 figure, which is listed under Flags.

## Claims to verify

- **"A penny cost 3.69¢ to make in FY2024"**: VERIFIED. U.S. Mint 2024 Annual Report: "The unit cost for pennies (3.69 cents) and nickels (13.78 cents) remained above face value for the 19th consecutive fiscal year." In the unit-cost table, the one-cent "Total Unit cost" is $0.0369: COGS $0.0300, SG&A $0.0066, distribution to FRB $0.0003. FY2023 was $0.0307, so the increase was "20.2 percent". URL: https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/docs/publications/usmint/usmint_2024-Annual-Report.pdf (FRASER copy of the Mint report; usmint.gov blocks automated fetches). Auditor's report dated December 9, 2024.
- **"The Mint lost $85.3M on pennies"**: VERIFIED, with wording. 2024 Annual Report, table "Shipments, Costs, and Seigniorage by Denomination": One-Cent shipments 3,172 million coins, value $31.7M, gross cost $117.0M, seigniorage "($85.3)" million, or ($2.69) per $1 issued. The figure is negative seigniorage on FY2024 shipments, meaning face value minus full cost. Same URL as above. The St. Louis Fed also says the Mint "lost $85.3 million on making pennies that year" (URL in the background section).
- **"The nickel costs about 14¢"**: VERIFIED for FY2024. The exact figure is 13.78¢ (table: Five-Cent Total Unit cost $0.1378, up 19.4% from $0.1154). The nickel's FY2024 seigniorage was "($17.7)" million on 202 million coins. Same URL. Caveat: the FY2025 figure is reportedly lower; see Flags.
- **"The last circulating cent was struck Nov 12, 2025"**: VERIFIED.
  - Mint press release (GlobeNewswire copy), Nov. 12, 2025: the ceremonial strike in Philadelphia marked the end of circulating penny production. The release says: "While circulating production has ceased, the penny remains legal tender." The Mint keeps making numismatic cents in limited quantities. URL: https://www.financialcontent.com/article/gnwcq-2025-11-12-united-states-mint-hosts-historic-ceremonial-strike-for-final-production-of-the-circulating-one-cent-coin
  - Correction issued the same day: "The ceremonial strike was performed by United States Treasurer Brandon Beach" and "not by Secretary of the Treasury Scott Bessent." URL: https://www.financialcontent.com/article/gnwcq-2025-11-12-correction-to-united-states-mint-hosts-historic-ceremonial-strike-for-final-production-of-the-circulating-one-cent-coin
  - St. Louis Fed Open Vault (Jan. 28, 2026): "Penny production stopped Nov. 12, 2025." URL: https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2026/jan/making-sense-of-not-making-cents
  - Official usmint.gov page (returns 403 to fetches): https://www.usmint.gov/news/press-releases/united-states-mint-hosts-historic-ceremonial-strike-for-final-production-of-the-circulating-one-cent-coin

## Background facts

### Treasury announcement and FAQs
- Treasury, "Penny Production Cessation FAQs," December 23, 2025. Quotes: "Over the past 10 years, the total production cost of the penny has risen from 1.3 cents to 3.69 cents per penny." The Mint projects "an immediate annual savings of $56 million in reduced material costs by stopping penny production." "The penny will remain legal tender." URL: https://home.treasury.gov/news/featured-stories/penny-production-cessation-faqs
- Fed FAQ, "Penny Order and Deposit Information": "In May 2025, the U.S. Department of the Treasury shared plans to end production of the U.S. penny coin. Shortly thereafter, the Federal Reserve received its final shipment of new pennies from the U.S. Mint." URL: https://www.frbservices.org/resources/financial-services/fedcash-services-resources/fedcash-frequently-asked-questions/penny-order-deposit-information/ (published 2026-02-02, modified 2026-11-10)

- Per-coin figures (shipments; seigniorage; seigniorage per $1 issued):
  - One-cent: 3,172M coins; **($85.3M)**; ($2.69)
  - Five-cent: 202M coins; **($17.7M)**; ($1.75)
  - Dime: 840M coins; **$35.6M**; $0.42
  - Quarter: 1,605M coins; **$165.6M**; $0.41
  - Half-dollar: 52M coins; **$8.4M**; $0.32
  - Mutilated & other: ($7.1M)
  - **Total circulating seigniorage: $99.5M**, $0.18 per $1 issued
- "Seigniorage decreased 60.0 percent to $99.5 million" (FY2023: $249.0M).
- Unit costs FY2024: dime $0.0576, quarter $0.1468, half $0.3397.
- Financial statements, Note 15: "Total Seigniorage" $107,973K. This breaks down as Seigniorage-FRB $99,598K plus "with the public" $8,375K.

### St. Louis Fed Page One Economics (May 2026)
- Scott A. Wolla, "When a Penny Costs More Than a Penny: The Economics Behind Ending the Cent," May 1, 2026. Key points:
  - The Mint "struck its final circulating penny in Philadelphia," ending 232 years of production.
  - The Mint "lost $85.3 million on making pennies that year" (2024).
  - Its table gives 3.7¢ per penny and 13.8¢ per nickel.
  - Cash was "14% of transactions in 2024."
- URL: https://www.stlouisfed.org/publications/page-one-economics/2026/may/penny-costs-more-than-penny-economics-behind-ending-the-cent

### Pennies in circulation / "lost"

### 1982 switch to zinc
- Christian Science Monitor, Richard L. Strout, April 24, 1981 (contemporary report). Treasurer Angela M. Buchanan told Congress the new cent would be "97.6 percent zinc and 2.4 percent copper," with copper electroplated on the outside; the old cent was 95% copper and 5% zinc. The article says: "The zinc penny will save the Treasury $25 million a year now." URL: https://www.csmonitor.com/1981/0424/042429.html
- Minneapolis Fed, "Small Change: Thoughts on the Penny," March 1, 1992 (reprinted from the Boston Fed's *Penny Points*). Quotes: "a penny that is 97.6 percent zinc and only 2.4 percent copper," versus "95 percent copper and 5 percent zinc" before 1982. The article adds that 1988 figures put the zinc cent at "6/10 cent to manufacture, compared to 8/10 cent for the old copper coin." URL: https://www.minneapolisfed.org/article/1992/small-change
- Legal authority, 31 U.S.C. §5112(c): "The Secretary may prescribe the weight and the composition of copper and zinc in the alloy of the one-cent coin..." URL: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5112

### Rounding rules
- **Treasury guidance (non-binding), Dec. 23, 2025.** It quotes NCSL's symmetrical method:
  - Round down if the final digit "is 1, 2, 6, or 7 cents"; round up if it "is 3, 4, 8, or 9 cents."
  - "Transactions totaling exactly $0.01 or $0.02 might be rounded up to $0.05."
  - "Rounding rules would not apply to payments made via electronic methods, checks, gift cards, or other non-cash instruments."
  - Round "only after all duties, fees, and taxes have been calculated."
  - URL: Treasury FAQ above.
- **Washington State, SHB 2334, Chapter 138, Laws of 2026.** Signed March 23, 2026; effective June 11, 2026.
  - Rounding is permissive ("may be rounded") and symmetrical: 1, 2, 6, 7 round down; 3, 4, 8, 9 round up; 0 and 5 are not rounded.
  - It applies "only to in-person payments made by legal tender."
  - "The total price ... may be paid by the customer at the customer's option if the customer has the exact legal tender."
  - Taxes are computed "based on the sales price before any transaction rounding."
  - Rounding is "not actionable under the consumer protection act."
  - URL: https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/biennium/2025-26/Pdf/Bills/Session%20Laws/House/2334-S.SL.pdf
- **Federal Common Cents Act: not law as of the latest data.**
  - H.R. 3074 passed the House by voice vote on 2026-07-14 and was referred to Senate Banking on 2026-07-15.
  - S. 1525 passed the Senate with an amendment by unanimous consent on 2026-08-07. Its latest action is "Held at the desk" in the House, 2026-08-10.
  - Neither bill has a public law number. Status files updated 2026-09-16 and 2026-09-18.
  - URLs: https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/hr/BILLSTATUS-119hr3074.xml ; https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/s/BILLSTATUS-119s1525.xml

### Richmond Fed "rounding tax"
- Zhu Wang and Russell Wong, "Rounding Up: The Impact of Phasing Out the Penny," Richmond Fed Economic Brief No. 25-27, July 2025.
  - The penny's removal "would cost consumers about $6.06 million annually."
  - "Transactions are more likely to end in 3, 4, 8 or 9 cents than the other digits."
  - Eliminating the nickel as well "could cost consumers approximately $55.58 million per year."
  - The data come from the 2023 Diary of Consumer Payment Choice.
  - URL: https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_brief/2025/eb_25-27

## Flags / not verified

1. **FY2025 Mint figures are not verified on a primary page.** The FY2025 Annual Report is at https://www.usmint.gov/content/dam/usmint/reports/2025-annual-report.pdf, but every fetch returned HTTP 403, and no FRASER copy could be reached. Search snippets give penny 3.02¢ and nickel 13.31¢, "20th consecutive fiscal year." If those figures hold, FY2024 (3.69¢) was the peak, and the latest nickel figure is about 13.3¢, not 14¢. Check by hand before the script says "today a nickel costs 14¢." Label 3.69¢ and 13.78¢ as FY2024.
2. **Seigniorage total conflict.** The St. Louis Fed table says "Total seigniorage in 2024 was $106.6 million." The Mint report gives $99.5M circulating and $107.97M total (financial statements, including $8.4M from numismatic sales). Neither matches $106.6M. Use $99.5M (circulating) and cite the Mint.
3. **"Ten years ago" baseline conflict.** The Treasury FAQ says the cost rose "from 1.3 cents"; the Mint's Nov. 12 release says "from 1.42 cents." Pick one and attribute it.
4. **Pennies in circulation conflict: Mint 300 billion vs. Treasury 114 billion.** Both appear on official pages, and neither page explains its method. Say "estimates range from about 114 billion (Treasury) to 300 billion (Mint)," or attribute a single figure.
5. **No primary "lost pennies" estimate was found.** Secondary reports cite an FRFS estimate of $10–14B in household coin jars, covering all coins, not pennies alone. That figure was not found on a Fed page. Do not use it unless it is sourced.
6. **The "struck by" wording.** The original Mint release said Secretary Bessent struck the coin, and a same-day correction says Treasurer Brandon Beach did. Some AP-syndicated copy says Nov. 11. The Mint's own date is Nov. 12.
7. **Nuance on "stopped making the penny."** Only circulating production stopped. The Mint still makes numismatic cents, and the penny remains legal tender. The Fed received its last new pennies from the Mint shortly after May 2025, months before the ceremonial final strike.
8. **The $85.3M wording.** It is negative seigniorage on 3.172 billion pennies shipped in FY2024, not a separate accounting "loss" line. "The Mint spent $85.3M more than face value" is the most precise phrasing.
9. **The federal rounding law is pending.** The House passed H.R. 3074 and the Senate passed S. 1525, but they are different bills and neither is law as of the September 2026 status data. NCSL's state tracker (ncsl.org) and congress.gov both returned 403, so the full list of state laws was not verified. Only Washington's law was read in full. A May 2026 NCSL piece, seen in search results only, reportedly lists 8 states.
10. **The usmint.gov penny history and specifications pages could not be fetched (403).** The 1982 composition comes from a 1981 news report and a 1992 Fed article (97.6% zinc / 2.4% copper). Many secondary sites say 97.5% / 2.5%. Use the overall split and cite the source.
11. **The Canadian grocery study was not converted to US dollars.** Cheung (2018) found C$3.27M per year moving from consumers to grocers. It is cited only second-hand, via the St. Louis Fed. The Royal Canadian Mint's own 2012 pages (mint.ca) were not reached; Canada figures come from Finance Canada and canada.ca releases.
12. **The original May 2025 Treasury statement was not found on home.treasury.gov.** Treasury's final blank order and its "$56 million" figure are quoted here from the Dec. 2025 FAQ instead. Some news reports cite "$85 million" in savings, which confuses the savings estimate with the FY2024 loss. Do not use that figure.
