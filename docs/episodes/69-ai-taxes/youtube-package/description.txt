Will AI take over jobs? If it does, here's the question almost nobody asks: who pays the taxes? Meet Rosa, the mayor of Pinewick, a small town whose biggest employer just handed the work to robots. Robots lift faster, never get tired and never get a paycheck, so they never pay income tax. Following the last pay stub from the warehouse, Rosa discovers that almost nine of every ten dollars Washington collects come from people's income and paychecks, then tours an idea fair of fixes: robot taxes, taxing owners, taxing shopping, AI dividends and basic income, weighing one good reason and one catch for each. (Our answer: nobody has decided yet. Today the bill sits mostly on workers; if work shrinks, it has to move, and every fix simply chooses whose shoulders it lands on. Social Security's clock means the debate may arrive before the robots do.)

Key figures (exact values; the film rounds them)
- Federal receipts, fiscal year 2026 (to Sep 30, 2026): total $5,403.3bn; individual income taxes $2,845.5bn; social insurance and retirement receipts (mostly payroll taxes) $1,811.8bn; corporate income taxes $381.4bn (Treasury, Monthly Treasury Statement). Individual + payroll ≈ 86% ("almost nine in ten"); corporate ≈ 7% (our calculations). Individual income tax also falls on investment income, not only wages. FY2025: corporate $452.1bn of $5,234.6bn.
- FY2026 federal deficit: $2.0 trillion (CBO).
- Social Security (2026 Trustees Report, PROJECTION): combined trust fund reserves run out in the third quarter of 2034; after that, incoming payroll taxes would cover about 83% of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts.
- Rich democracies (OECD Revenue Statistics 2025, 2023 data, unweighted averages): personal income tax 23.7% + social security contributions 25.5% ≈ half of all tax revenue (our calculation); VAT 20.5%; corporate ≈ 12.7% (our calculation from 36.4% for personal + corporate). The US is the only OECD country without a national VAT.
- Robot tax: Bill Gates floated taxing a robot "at a similar level" to the worker it replaces (Quartz interview, Feb 2017); the European Parliament rejected a robot-tax proposal (Feb 16, 2017); South Korea's Aug 2017 tax plan trimmed its tax credit for automation investment (then 3-7%) by up to 2 points, nicknamed the first robot tax.
- Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend: paid yearly to nearly every resident from an oil-revenue fund (2025: 618,863 paid; population 738,737).
- Stockton SEED (2019-21): 125 residents got $500 a month for 24 months; after one year, full-time employment 28% → 40% vs 32% → 37% in the comparison group (preliminary).
- OpenResearch study (NBER w32719, revised Sept 2025): 1,000 low-income people got $1,000 a month for three years vs 2,000 who got $50; recipients worked about 1-2 fewer hours a week, labor force participation -3.9 points, earned income about $1,800 a year lower; no improvement in job quality; spending rose; a well-being boost faded after year one.
Rosa, Pinewick, Stub, Rivet and Ray are fictional. The film takes no side: each proposal gets one good reason and one catch. USD only.
